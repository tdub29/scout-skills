# Dichotomy of control â€” triage what's up to you

## When to use

Trigger when Trevor is **spending emotional energy on things outside his control** and it's leaking into dread, helplessness, or trying to manage the unmanageable:

- A recruiter has gone silent and he's refreshing the inbox, drafting and re-drafting follow-ups, reading tea leaves.
- He's anxious about a hiring committee's decision, a comp band, the job market, whether a referrer will come through.
- He's trying to control how an interviewer *perceives* him rather than what he actually does and says.
- "There's nothing I can do" paralysis â€” OR its twin, frantic effort poured into a lever that isn't his.

This is the **discipline of will** in Marcus's three-discipline scheme (Hays, Intro): our attitude toward "things that are not within our control, those that we have done to us." Use it to *sort the situation*, then aim all the energy at the controllable half.

## The reframe

> "Remember, nothing belongs to you but your flesh and blood â€” and nothing else is under your control." â€” *3.10*

> "If you are pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs you, but your own judgment about it. And it is in your power to wipe out this judgment now." (the standing Stoic move â€” cf. *8.47*, *9.32*)

Marcus draws a hard line: your **choices, judgments, and actions** are yours; outcomes, other people's behavior, reputation, and results are *not*. Confusing the two is the engine of most needless suffering. The fix is not to care less â€” it's to put the caring where it can actually do work.

## The triage (run this with Trevor)

Make two columns out loud:

| UP TO ME (act here) | NOT UP TO ME (release here) |
|---|---|
| Whether I follow up, and how well I write it | Whether they reply, and when |
| The quality of my application / round prep | The hiring committee's verdict |
| Which roles I pursue and how I show up | The comp band, the market, the level they slot me |
| My conduct toward a rude interviewer | The interviewer's mood or fairness |
| Telling the truth about my work | How impressed they are |

Then three lines back to Trevor:
1. **Here's the one thing in the left column that's still open** â€” name it.
2. **Here's what you're leaking energy on in the right column** â€” name it, and say plainly: this is not yours to carry.
3. **Do the left-column thing, then put it down.** "Choose not to be harmed â€” and you won't feel harmed." (*4.7*)

## Worked example â€” the silent recruiter

Trevor: *"It's been nine days. They said they'd circle back. I keep checking. Did I blow it?"*

- **NOT up to him:** the reply, the timeline, what the committee thought. Replaying the round changes none of it.
- **Up to him:** one calibrated, non-needy follow-up (his to write); and continuing the funnel so *this* outcome stops being load-bearing.
- **Gut-check:** "The silence is data about their process, not a verdict on your worth. Send the one follow-up you can stand behind, then go run the next two applications. Their reply isn't yours to control â€” your pipeline is." (*3.10*; pair with `references/memento-mori.md` if the one outcome has become load-bearing.)

## Common failure modes

- **Counseling passivity on a controllable.** Marcus demands vigorous action on what *is* up to you. "There's nothing I can do" is usually false â€” there's a left column. Find it.
- **Smuggling outcomes into the left column.** "Make them want me" is not controllable; "show up prepared and honest" is. Keep the columns clean.
- **Using it to suppress, not regulate.** The goal isn't to pretend he doesn't care about the offer â€” it's to stop *spending* the caring where it's wasted.
- **Forgetting the handoff.** End by pointing at the one left-column action. Regulation that doesn't return to action is rumination with better vocabulary.
