## The framework

> "Look for the errors behind your mistakes but beware of rearview-mirror hindsight biases." (Chapter 8; Commandments 8 and 9)

A forecast you can't score teaches you nothing. The engine of improvement â€” the thing that turns the superforecaster disposition into actual skill â€” is a tight feedback loop: **ask resolvable questions, keep score, run honest postmortems,** and where possible **aggregate many forecasts into a wiser collective.**

### Resolvable, time-bound questions

You can only keep score if the question has an unambiguous answer at a known time. A scorable question is **explicit, time-bound, and resolvable**: "Will X happen by date D, judged by criterion C?" â€” not "Is tension rising in the region?" Vague verbiage ("might," "could," "a real risk") protects the forecaster from ever being graded, which is exactly why it must be replaced with crisp questions and numeric probabilities. The discipline of writing a resolvable question often clarifies the forecast itself, and it blocks the "bait-and-switch" trap where forecasters quietly swap a hard, narrow technical question for an easier emotional one and answer that instead.

### Postmortems â€” on failures *and* successes

When the question resolves, conduct an **unflinching postmortem**:

- **Own failures.** Don't justify or excuse â€” ask exactly where you went wrong. Beware **hindsight bias**: once you know the outcome, the past looks more predictable than it was, which corrupts the lesson.
- **Calibrate how much to learn.** The common error is learning **too little** (overlooking flawed assumptions), but you can also learn **too much** â€” you were basically right and made one small technical slip; don't tear up a sound method over it.
- **Postmortem your wins too.** A correct forecast may have come from **offsetting errors** â€” you got lucky. If you keep reasoning the same confident way, a nasty surprise awaits. Was I right for the right reasons?

### The dragonfly eye and team aggregation

Synthesizing many perspectives (the **dragonfly eye**, from the mindset reference) outperforms any single view, and that extends to **forecasting teams**. The Good Judgment Project found well-run superteams beat individuals and even prediction-market-style aggregation. The conditions that make a team wise rather than a victim of **groupthink**:

- **Cognitive diversity and independence** â€” members reason from genuinely different models, not a single house view.
- **The team craft of Commandment 9** â€” perspective taking (restate others' arguments to their satisfaction), precision questioning (sharpen claims), and constructive confrontation (disagree without being disagreeable).
- **A giving, open-minded culture** â€” sharing reasoning and rationale, not just numbers, so the group's combined estimate is informed.
- **Mechanical aggregation plus extremizing** â€” averaging diverse independent forecasts and then nudging the aggregate toward the confident end (because diverse voters each hold partial information) reliably sharpened accuracy in the tournament.

## How to apply

1. **Write the question to be resolvable.** Specify event, deadline, and the exact criterion that settles it. Reject vague phrasings and refuse the bait-and-switch to an easier question.
2. **Record the forecast with a date and a number** so it can be graded later (`references/tetlock-calibration-brier-granularity.md`).
3. **Schedule the postmortem** for resolution time â€” on both hits and misses.
4. **Interrogate the reasoning, not just the outcome.** Right for the right reasons? Lucky via offsetting errors? Guard against hindsight bias.
5. **Calibrate the lesson** â€” usually learn more from failure, but avoid over-correcting on a sound method that hit one snag.
6. **Aggregate a team well.** Recruit cognitive diversity, run independent estimates first, apply the Commandment-9 team craft, then combine (average, optionally extremize) rather than deferring to the loudest voice.

## Common failure modes

- **Unresolvable questions** â€” "rising tension," "a real risk," with no deadline or criterion, so scoring is impossible.
- **Bait-and-switch** â€” answering an easier emotional question than the one actually posed.
- **Hindsight bias** â€” "I basically knew it" after the fact, which erases the real lesson.
- **No postmortem on wins** â€” mistaking lucky, offset-error successes for sound reasoning.
- **Groupthink** â€” a "team" that's really one house view in many mouths; correlated, not aggregated, judgments.
- **Forecast-and-forget** â€” issuing a number with no resolution date and no feedback loop, so no skill is ever built.
