## The framework

> "When information is made disfluent, we learn more." (Chapter 8, Absorbing Data)

To actually *learn* from information â€” not just possess it â€” you must **make it harder**: force yourself to manipulate, write, transform, or test the data so your brain genuinely engages with it. This deliberate friction is **disfluency**, and it is the cure for **information blindness**, the tendency to stop absorbing data when there's too much of it. Duhigg's cases are Cincinnati's South Avondale Elementary turnaround and Chase Manhattan's debt collectors.

### Information blindness and overload

More data should make decisions easier; in practice, being surrounded by data often makes deciding *harder*. The 401(k) study (~800,000 workers) is the proof: at 2 plan options, 75% enrolled; at 25 options, still 72%; but past ~30 plans, sign-ups collapsed â€” every ten extra funds dropped participation 1.5â€“2%, until at 60 plans only 53% enrolled. The right choice never changed; the *volume* of information caused people to give up and shove the brochures in a drawer. "The quality of people's decisions gets better as they receive more relevant informationâ€¦ then their brain reaches a breaking point when the data becomes too much."

### How the brain turns information into knowledge: scaffolding

We absorb information well only when we can break it into smaller pieces â€” "winnowing" or **scaffolding** into mental folders and subfolders (the wine-list example: red or white? cheap or expensive? this Chardonnay or that one?). Experts differ from novices mainly in how many folders they carry and how fast they route information into them. When data arrives with no obvious way to start sorting it (60 undifferentiated 401(k) plans), the brain defaults to a binary: engage, or ignore entirely. Overload pushes it toward *ignore*.

### Disfluency: make it harder so it sticks

The fix is to **force an operation on the data**. South Avondale's teachers had been drowning in dashboards and spreadsheets they never opened. The turnaround came when they were *banned* from the slick tools and ordered into "data rooms" to transcribe test scores onto index cards by hand, draw graphs on butcher paper, and run their own impromptu experiments. "Rather than simply receiving information, teachers were forced to engage with it" â€” they made the data disfluent: harder to process at first, but far stickier once understood. The principal: "there's a difference between finding an answer and understanding what it means."

The mechanism (NYU's Adam Alter): performing *some operation* is what matters â€” use a new word in a sentence and you remember it; read text in a hard-to-read font and you process it more carefully. The initial difficulty makes you think more deeply. Chase's best debt collectors (Tampa) succeeded not from the computer-fed data on each debtor but from forcing themselves to actively work the information into a tailored approach.

## How to apply

1. **Don't just consume â€” operate on the data.** Before trusting that you've "reviewed" a report or dashboard, do something *to* it: rewrite it in your own words, recompute a number, build the spreadsheet yourself, turn it into questions to answer.
2. **Make it disfluent on purpose.** Transcribe by hand, sketch the graph yourself, restate the finding as a sequence of binary choices. The small bit of labor is precisely what converts passive looking into learning.
3. **Build a scaffold for overload.** When information is too plentiful, impose a folder structure: reduce the choice to two or three categories, then sub-categorize, so each step is a manageable decision instead of one overwhelming mass.
4. **Test your preconceptions against the data.** Like the South Avondale teachers running classroom experiments, frame information as hypotheses to check ("do scores improve with smaller reading groups?") rather than facts to file.
5. **Distrust frictionless dashboards for genuine learning.** Polished tools that hand you the answer can produce information blindness; if a metric must actually change behavior, route it through an operation that forces engagement.

## Common failure modes

- **Information blindness from overload** â€” adding more reports/options/dashboards and assuming better decisions follow; past a threshold, people disengage entirely.
- **Passive consumption** â€” reading or skimming data without performing any operation on it; nothing sticks and behavior doesn't change.
- **Over-polished tooling** â€” making data so easy and pre-digested that no engagement (and no real understanding) ever occurs.
- **No scaffold** â€” facing a large undifferentiated mass with no folder structure, so the brain defaults to "ignore."
- **Confusing access with understanding** â€” assuming that because the answer is findable in seconds, it's understood; "finding an answer" and "understanding what it means" are different.
