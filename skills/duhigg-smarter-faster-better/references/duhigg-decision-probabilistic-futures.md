## The framework

> "Probabilistic thinking â€¦ is the ability to hold multiple, conflicting outcomes in your mind and estimate their relative likelihoods." (Chapter 6, Decision Making)

Good decisions come from **forecasting the future probabilistically** rather than fixating on a single expected outcome. The skill is to **envision multiple, often contradictory futures, assign odds to each, and update those odds (Bayesian-style) from experience** â€” including, deliberately, from failures. Duhigg builds this around poker champion **Annie Duke** and the **Good Judgment Project (GJP)**, Philip Tetlock's forecasting tournament.

### The future is many futures, not one

The GJP taught ordinary volunteers to beat intelligence analysts at prediction. Its central lesson: "The future isn't one thing. Rather, it is a multitude of possibilities that often contradict one another until one of them comes true." For the 2012 French election, trainees were taught to hold three contradictory scenarios for Sarkozy (67%, 25%, 45% of the vote) and **combine** them into one estimate (a simple equal-weighted average â†’ ~46%) â€” a deliberately simplistic illustration of a deep idea: contradictory futures can be merged into a single calibrated prediction, often visualized as a probability *curve* across outcomes. Merely exposing people to this probabilistic training raised forecast accuracy by as much as 50%.

We resist this because "we only live in one reality," so forcing ourselves to think in multiple futures is unsettling â€” it makes us contemplate things we hope won't happen. That discomfort is the work.

### Bayesian thinking: start with a base rate, then update

Annie Duke's poker edge isn't math; it's **Bayesian cognition**. She begins each hand with a **base rate (prior)** â€” assumptions about an opponent based on type ("a forty-year-old businessman will take lots of risks; a twenty-two-year-old in a poker T-shirt learned online and plays tight"). The difference between this and prejudice is that she **continuously updates** the assumption from observed behavior: if the businessman bluffs well, maybe he's a pro inviting underestimation. "I spend a lot of time updating my assumptions because, if they're wrong, my base rate is off." Humans are naturally good Bayesian predictors when their priors are well-calibrated.

### Calibration requires learning from failure, not just success

The accuracy of your futures depends on the realism of your assumptions, which depends on your *experiences*. The trap: success is easier to look at, so we over-sample winners and ignore the humbled. The best entrepreneurs are "obsessed with spending time around people who complain about their failures." Calibrating a base rate "requires learning from both the accomplished and the humbled" â€” talking to thriving *and* failing colleagues, sitting in crowded *and* empty theaters. So when a deal falls through or a friend is fired, ask why; mine the disappointment for the data that sharpens your next forecast.

## How to apply

1. **Spell out multiple, contradictory futures.** For a decision, force yourself to list several distinct ways it could turn out â€” including the ones you hope won't happen â€” rather than committing to one expected story.
2. **Assign odds and combine them.** Estimate a probability for each scenario; if you have no reason to weight one more heavily, start with equal weighting and average. Think in distributions/curves, not point predictions.
3. **State a base rate first, then update.** Begin with the prior (how do situations/people of this type usually go?), then deliberately revise it as evidence arrives. Track the difference between your prior and what you're now seeing.
4. **Separate what you hope from what's likely.** Make the uncomfortable distinction explicit. The aim isn't certainty; it's knowing which assumptions are solid and which are flimsy.
5. **Harvest failures for calibration.** Actively seek out failures â€” yours and others'. After a bad outcome, ask precisely what happened instead of "next time will be better." Feed those lessons into the next round of forecasts.

## Common failure modes

- **Single-future fixation** â€” planning around one expected outcome, so any deviation blindsides you.
- **Avoiding uncomfortable scenarios** â€” refusing to assign odds to futures you don't want, which leaves your forecast systematically optimistic.
- **Prejudice masquerading as a prior** â€” setting a base rate and never updating it; the only thing separating useful Bayesian priors from bias is relentless updating.
- **Survivorship bias in calibration** â€” learning only from successes and the people still standing; your base rates drift rosy and your predictions degrade.
- **Demanding certainty** â€” treating "I can't be 100% sure" as a reason not to decide, instead of using probabilities to decide better under irreducible uncertainty.
