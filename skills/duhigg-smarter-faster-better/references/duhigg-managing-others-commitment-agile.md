## The framework

> "The person closest to a problem [has] primary responsibility for figuring out how to solve it." â€” Chad Fulgham on rebuilding the FBI's Sentinel (Chapter 5, Managing Others)

The best way to manage people and decisions is to **push decision-making power down to the people closest to the problem**, inside a culture built on **trust and ownership** rather than control. Duhigg braids two cases: Toyota's NUMMI plant and the "commitment culture" research (versus "star," "engineering," "bureaucratic," and "autocratic" models), and the FBI's rescue of its catastrophically late Sentinel software using **lean/agile** methods.

### Commitment cultures win

Stanford's Baron and Hannan tracked the founding "blueprints" of ~200 tech startups. The **commitment model** â€” hire slowly for cultural fit and self-direction, avoid layoffs, invest in training, prize employee loyalty over quick profit â€” was the only consistent winner: *not one* commitment firm in the study failed, they reached IPO fastest, had the highest profitability, and ran leaner with fewer middle managers. The glamorous **star model** (pack the smartest people in one room) produced some huge wins but failed in record numbers, wracked by infighting because everyone wants to be the star. Commitment cultures succeed because **trust** emerges among workers, managers, and customers, so people work harder, stay through setbacks, and don't carry insights to competitors.

### Push decisions to the front line (lean/agile)

At Toyota's NUMMI plant, the radical move was letting any worker pull the **andon cord** to stop the entire assembly line, and having managers take cues from workers rather than the reverse. Trusting front-line workers with real authority â€” and the obligation to use it â€” transformed a notoriously dysfunctional GM plant. "Once you're entrusted with that kind of authority, you can't help [but] feel a sense of ownership."

The FBI's Sentinel was the bureaucratic anti-pattern: thousands of pre-written specifications, hundreds of pages of rules, approvals from countless distrustful officials, projects canceled after months of work â€” a 2010 report projected six more years and $396M. Chad Fulgham, applying **lean manufacturing / agile programming**, cut the team from 400+ to ~30 and shipped it in sixteen months for ~$20M. The rules: everyone must make suggestions; anyone can call a time-out; **whoever is closest to a problem owns solving it**; daily stand-ups; demos every two weeks with no micromanaging (leaders could *suggest*, not command). Because you can't plan everything in advance â€” problems and breakthroughs are unpredictable â€” authority had to live with the people doing the work.

## How to apply

1. **Hire for self-direction and fit, slowly.** A commitment culture depends on people who excel without being directed. Hire deliberately so you can trust people with autonomy later; don't staff for raw star-power and hope it coheres.
2. **Push the decision to whoever is closest to the problem.** Identify who has the most direct, current contact with the issue, and give them primary responsibility *and* the authority to act â€” not just a recommendation to escalate.
3. **Give people a real "andon cord."** Build mechanisms for any team member to stop the line, flag a problem, or call a time-out without permission. The authority to halt or redirect creates ownership.
4. **Replace up-front mega-planning with iterative cycles.** When the path is uncertain, use short cycles (stand-ups, two-week demos) instead of exhaustive specifications. Let the design adapt to what the front line learns.
5. **Have leaders suggest, not command.** Catalog leadership input as suggestions evaluated by the owner of that work. Forbid micromanagement; protect against layoffs and invest in training to keep the trust intact.

## Common failure modes

- **The star trap** â€” concentrating the smartest people and rewarding individual stardom; produces infighting and high failure rates because everyone competes to be the star.
- **Control-from-above / bureaucratic planning** â€” trying to specify everything in advance (the original Sentinel); guarantees rigidity, canceled work, and missed reality on the ground.
- **Delegating responsibility without authority** â€” telling the front line they "own" a problem while still requiring layers of sign-off; ownership without real decision power is theater.
- **Treating trust as a cost center** â€” cutting training, defaulting to layoffs, prioritizing quick profit; this dissolves the trust that makes commitment cultures outperform.
- **Distrust among leaders** â€” internal power struggles (the FBI's original state) that drive defensive over-specification and kill front-line initiative.
