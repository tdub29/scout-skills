## The framework

> "If we are constantly telling ourselves stories and creating mental pictures, that beam never fully powers down â€¦ so when it has to flare to life in the real world, we're not blinded by its glare." (Chapter 3, Focus)

Sustained, high-quality attention is a skill built on **mental models** â€” the habit of narrating to yourself a story of what you expect to happen, so that when reality diverges, your attention snaps to the discrepancy instead of seizing on whatever is most obvious. The cautionary tale is **Air France Flight 447**, where pilots with weak mental models, suddenly handed control after hours of autopilot, fell into cognitive tunneling and flew a working plane into the ocean.

### The threat: cognitive tunneling and reactive thinking

Our attention is a spotlight that we relax when automated systems (autopilots, computers, routines) handle monitoring for us â€” a useful energy-saving default. But when an emergency hits, the spotlight has to "ramp up all of a sudden and, at first, it doesn't know where to shine," so the brain forces it onto **the most obvious stimulus right in front of us**, even when that's the wrong choice. That is **cognitive tunneling**: over-focusing on the immediate or easiest-to-grasp thing at the cost of common sense. **Reactive thinking** is its cousin â€” responding automatically and habitually rather than deliberately. Flight 447's copilot Bonin tunneled onto the wrong instrument, latched onto a single mental model ("I'm in TO/GA, right?"), and ignored every fact that contradicted it ("I'm climbing, okay, so we're going down") until impact ("But what's happening?").

### The defense: build robust mental models by narrating your expectations

People who manage attention well (Duhigg's exemplar is Darlene, the NICU nurse who spotted a dying baby others missed) share a habit: they **tell themselves stories about what's going on as it happens** and **forecast what they expect to see**. Darlene had a vivid mental picture of what a healthy baby looked like; when the real infant's mottled skin and distended belly clashed with that picture, her attention was *snagged* by the mismatch. Because she was constantly narrating, her spotlight "never fully powered down."

The MIT study of a recruiting firm's superstars showed the same pattern at work: the most productive people obsessively **generated theories** â€” about why accounts succeeded, why clients were happy, how management styles played out. They were always explaining the world to themselves, which is the same engine: building and updating mental models so new information has a structure to land in.

## How to apply

1. **Narrate before you act.** Before a meeting, drive, surgery, or shift, tell yourself a concrete story of what you *expect* to happen and what you'll do. Specificity is the point â€” picture the conversation, the screen, the next three steps.
2. **Forecast so you can be surprised.** The value of the expected-story is that reality can *diverge* from it. A vivid prediction turns a subtle anomaly into a salient mismatch your attention will catch.
3. **Talk to yourself / write it down in noisy or automated settings.** When a system is monitoring for you (dashboards, autopilots, routine ops), deliberately keep your spotlight warm: ask "what should I be seeing right now? what would tell me something's wrong?"
4. **When the unexpected hits, resist the first obvious stimulus.** Recognize the tunneling reflex. Before reacting, ask what your mental model predicted and which facts *contradict* the story you're tempted to grab.
5. **Generate theories habitually.** Like the superstars, keep explaining the world to yourself â€” why did that go well, why is this client unhappy. The theories are scaffolding that makes future information faster to absorb.

## Common failure modes

- **Outsourcing attention to automation** â€” letting computers/autopilots/routines monitor for you with no mental model running; the spotlight dims and you're blinded when it must flare.
- **Latching onto the first mental model and ignoring disconfirming data** â€” Bonin's fatal error; once you commit to a story, actively hunt for facts that *break* it.
- **Tunneling on the obvious** â€” fixating on the most immediate or easiest-to-grasp signal (the blaring alarm, the wailing easiest detail) rather than the most important one.
- **Vague expectations** â€” a fuzzy "it'll probably be fine" is not a mental model; without a specific predicted story, there's nothing for an anomaly to clash against.
- **Confusing busyness with focus** â€” handling ten projects reactively beats out the disciplined few-projects-plus-theories pattern of the genuinely productive.
