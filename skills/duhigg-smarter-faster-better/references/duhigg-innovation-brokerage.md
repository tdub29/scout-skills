### Innovation = combining old ideas in new ways

"Creativity is just connecting things" (Steve Jobs). The most "creative" people are **intellectual middlemen / idea brokers** who transfer knowledge between groups. Sociologist Ronald Burt's study of 673 managers found the ideas most consistently rated creative came from people who moved concepts across divisions â€” "between-group brokers" whose ideas are more credible because they can point to where the idea already worked. *West Side Story* itself is a broker move: *Romeo and Juliet* relocated to 1950s New York gang warfare. The Post-it (a failed-adhesive chemist annoyed by hymnal bookmarks), cellophane (protecting tablecloths from wine), and infant formula (a tired father suspending nutrients in powder) are all old ideas dropped into new settings.

Critically, **there is no "creative personality."** Studies show almost anyone can become a broker â€” *if pushed the right way*. Disney pushes its people to mine **their own emotions** as raw material (the songwriters cracked Elsa by asking "what would it feel like to be judged your whole life for something you can't control?" â€” a feeling Kristen Anderson-Lopez knew personally). The lesson: pay attention to how things make you *feel and react*, and use that as creative fodder.

### Creative desperation: a little panic helps

Effective brokers "aren't cool and collected. They're often worried and afraid." Gary Klein's research suggests roughly 20% of creative breakthroughs are preceded by anxiety. Deadline pressure, the clash of people from different backgrounds, and the stress of collaborators pushing for more all generate **divergent thinking** â€” the ability to see something new because you're forced to look at an idea from someone else's point of view. A small amount of tension is fuel.

## How to apply

1. **Treat innovation as import-export.** Don't wait for an original bolt. Ask: where has a problem like this already been solved â€” another industry, discipline, era â€” and what can I carry over? Brokered ideas are more credible *and* more likely to work.
2. **Mine your own emotions and experience as material.** Notice how a situation makes you feel and react; that reaction is often the raw insight. Push past the clichÃ© to "something real."
3. **Use a little tension on purpose.** Deadlines, diverse collaborators, and constructive pressure trigger divergent thinking. Don't engineer it away in the name of comfort.
4. **Watch for the consensus trap.** When everyone suddenly agrees and the team feels relieved, that's the warning sign you've stopped generating options. Deliberately reintroduce a small disturbance (a new voice, a reframed role, a forced alternative).
5. **Be willing to kill your darlings.** Separate your attachment to prior effort from the quality of the current solution. If you're clinging to the first idea because of sunk work, surface that explicitly and force fresh options.

## Common failure modes

- **Waiting for genius** â€” assuming you need to be a "creative type"; the evidence says brokering is learnable and almost anyone can do it when pushed.
- **Staying in one domain** â€” looking for solutions only inside your own field, so you never broker the proven idea sitting one industry over.
- **Sanitizing away all tension** â€” removing every deadline and disagreement in pursuit of a calm process, and losing the divergent thinking that pressure produces.
- **Clinging to the first solution** â€” over-valuing what you've already built (the IKEA-effect of your own ideas) and refusing to let it go even when stuck.
- **Mistaking consensus for completion** â€” treating the relief of everyone agreeing as success, when it often signals the team has stopped exploring.
