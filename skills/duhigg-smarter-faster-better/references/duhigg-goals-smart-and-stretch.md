## The framework

> "The reason why we need both stretch goals and SMART goals is that audaciousness, on its own, can be terrifying â€¦ And so, for a stretch goal to become more than just an aspiration, [you] often need â€¦ the discipline of SMART goals." (Chapter 4, Goal Setting)

Good goal-setting **pairs two kinds of goals that fail when used alone**: a **stretch goal** (audacious, ambitious, gives direction and inspiration) and a set of **SMART goals** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Timeline-based â€” the concrete near-term steps that make the stretch goal actionable). Duhigg contrasts GE's SMART-goal culture under Jack Welch with the stretch goals Welch later layered on top, and shows what goes wrong with either half by itself.

### SMART goals: concrete, near-term, disciplined

SMART goals (developed from Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's research) force vague aspirations into provable plans. In Latham's 1975 typist study, a fifteen-minute conversation that turned "type well" into a specific, measured, realistic, time-bound target lifted output from 95 to 112 lines per hour and the gains held months later. "Specific, high goals lead to a higher level of task performance than â€¦ vague, abstract goals such as the exhortation to 'do one's best.'" SMART works because making a goal specific and proving it achievable *forces you to figure out the steps* â€” it's "the difference between hoping something comes true and figuring out how to do it."

### The failure of SMART alone: timidity and tunnel vision

The same need-for-closure that makes SMART satisfying also makes it dangerous in isolation. The GE assistant who wrote SMART memos for tasks she'd *already finished* just to file them in her "Done" folder illustrates the trap: SMART goals "can cause a person to have tunnel vision, to focus â€¦ on getting immediate results." People with only SMART goals "seize on the easiest tasks, become obsessed with finishing projects, and freeze on priorities" â€” crossing things off the list becomes more important than asking whether you're doing the right things. SMART without stretch breeds **timidity**.

### The failure of stretch alone: overwhelm and paralysis

A genuinely audacious goal (Welch's order to cut aircraft-engine errors by 70%, or a runner aiming far below their current time) sparks innovation and elevates aspiration *because* the only way to hit it is to rethink the whole approach. Numerous studies (3M's Scotch tape and Thinsulate among them) tie stretch goals to breakthrough. **But** the caveat: a stretch goal that's audacious can also be *terrifying and paralyzing* â€” it's often unclear how to even start. Stretch without SMART **overwhelms**. The fix is to break the audacious aim down into SMART sub-goals â€” the "athletic equivalent of breaking a stretch goal into SMART components."

## How to apply

1. **State the stretch goal first.** Begin with the audacious, direction-setting ambition (GE's "Work-Outs" deliberately captured the big aim *before* worrying about feasibility, to encourage bigger thinking). Don't pre-shrink it to what feels safe.
2. **Immediately decompose it into SMART sub-goals.** For the stretch aim, define specific, measurable, realistic, time-bound next steps. This converts "terrifying aspiration" into "today's concrete task" and is what keeps the stretch goal from being mere fantasy.
3. **Write to-do lists that hold both layers.** Put the stretch goal at the top of the list and the SMART first-step beneath it, so each near-term task is visibly in service of the larger ambition (this also supplies the "why" from the motivation chapter).
4. **Audit your list for tunnel vision.** Periodically ask: am I doing the easiest items to feel done, or the items that move the stretch goal? If your "Done" folder is full of trivial completions, you have SMART-only disease.
5. **Re-stretch when SMART goals get comfortable.** When near-term targets are routinely met with ease (GE's complacent factories), reintroduce an audacious goal to shake people out of short-term optimization.

## Common failure modes

- **SMART-only** â€” productive-feeling busywork; timidity; obsession with finishing and crossing off, while never questioning whether the work matters.
- **Stretch-only** â€” an inspiring banner that no one knows how to start on; overwhelm, anxiety, and paralysis instead of action.
- **Fake-achievable SMART goals** â€” setting targets you've effectively already met (the "Done" folder trick) so the satisfaction is real but the progress is nil.
- **Letting closure drive priorities** â€” choosing goals because they're easy to mark complete rather than because they advance the ambition.
- **Never revisiting the stretch** â€” meeting SMART goals indefinitely without raising the ceiling, which calcifies into short-term thinking.
