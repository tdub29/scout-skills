## The framework

> "We had to manage the *how* of teams, not the *who*." â€” Abeer Dubey, Google (Chapter 2, Teams)

What makes a team effective is **not who is on it** but **the norms by which its members interact**. Duhigg tells this through Google's **Project Aristotle**, which studied 180+ Google teams for over 150 hours and found that raw talent, seniority, and personality mix barely predicted performance. Group norms did. And among all norms, the one that mattered most was **psychological safety**.

### Norms beat membership

Project Aristotle initially looked for the perfect *mix of people* and found nothing consistent â€” the best teams had wildly different compositions. The breakthrough came when they shifted to **group norms**: the unwritten rules of how a team behaves. The same data that made no sense at the individual level "started making sense" at the norm level. Norms determine whether members feel safe or threatened, energized or drained, encouraged or discouraged. Julia Rozovsky's draining Yale study group (tussles for leadership, constant proving, passive-aggressive critique) versus her energizing case-competition team (enthusiasm for each other's ideas, withheld criticism, freedom to lead or hang back) is the contrast.

### Collective intelligence comes from two behaviors

Anita Woolley's "collective intelligence" research found that a group's smarts is a property of the *group, not its individuals* â€” the right norms raise mediocre thinkers; the wrong norms hobble brilliant ones. Two behaviors showed up in every good team:

1. **Equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking.** Everyone speaks in roughly the same proportion. It need not be equal minute-to-minute, but by the end "everyone had spoken roughly the same amount." When one person or a clique dominates, collective intelligence collapses.
2. **High average social sensitivity.** Members are skilled at reading how others feel from tone, posture, and expression (measured by the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test). They notice when someone is upset or left out and ask what others are thinking.

### Psychological safety is the umbrella norm

Amy Edmondson's term, which Google adopted, names the climate these behaviors create: a shared belief that the team is **safe for interpersonal risk-taking** â€” you can question, admit mistakes, propose half-formed ideas, and disagree without fear of being punished or humiliated, while trusting that others aren't trying to undermine you.

*Saturday Night Live* is Duhigg's vivid case: a cast of combative, egotistical comedians who nonetheless protected one another's feelings, because Lorne Michaels was "ostentatious in his demonstrations of social sensitivity" â€” making sure everyone got to pitch ("Do we have pieces for the girls this week?"), drawing in quiet voices, and modeling the listening he expected. People disagreed and clashed but were careful how far critiques went and didn't escalate.

## How to apply

1. **Diagnose at the norm level, not the roster.** Before reshuffling people, ask: what are this team's actual norms? Who speaks, who interrupts, what happens when someone admits a mistake or floats a weak idea?
2. **Engineer equal airtime.** Track turn-taking. Round-robin in meetings, explicitly invite quieter members ("who hasn't been on in a while?"), and stop the loud minority from monopolizing. The goal is balanced totals, not rigid equality.
3. **Model and reward social sensitivity.** Leaders set the norm: acknowledge feelings, ask what people think, notice when someone's left out, summarize others' points before adding your own. Teams with safe norms had leaders who modeled listening.
4. **Make it safe to take interpersonal risks.** React to mistakes and dissent with curiosity, not punishment. Frame work as a learning problem ("we've never done this before, we need everyone's brains") rather than an execution problem where errors are failures.
5. **Allow controlled conflict.** Psychological safety is not conflict-avoidance. SNL clashed constantly; the norm was that critique didn't become personal and nobody escalated. Disagreement plus protectiveness is the target.

## Common failure modes

- **Hiring/firing your way to a great team** â€” chasing the perfect roster while ignoring the norms; "managing the who, not the how." The book's central error.
- **Letting one or two voices dominate** â€” even with brilliant people, lopsided turn-taking destroys collective intelligence.
- **Confusing psychological safety with niceness or no-conflict** â€” safety means risk is *safe*, not that disagreement is suppressed. Suppressed conflict is its own failure.
- **Efficiency over safety in the short run** â€” it is faster to cut off debate and decide unilaterally; doing so repeatedly erodes the safety that produces better outcomes over time.
- **Leaders who don't model it** â€” norms cascade from leader behavior. A leader who interrupts, dismisses, or punishes mistakes installs the opposite norm regardless of stated values.
