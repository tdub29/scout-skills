## The framework

> "Your Ability Chain is only as strong as its weakest Ability Factor link." (*Tiny Habits*, Chapter 3)

In B=MAP, the most reliable lever is **Ability** â€” making the behavior easier. Motivation is fickle; ability is something you can engineer. Fogg's central maxim here: **simplicity changes behavior.** But "simplicity" isn't generic â€” it's specific. A behavior is hard because of a specific weak link, and you find it by asking the **Discovery Question:**

> **"What is making this behavior hard to do?"**

The answer always involves at least one of **five Ability Factors**. They form a chain, and the chain is only as strong as its weakest link:

1. **Time** â€” Do you have enough time to do the behavior?
2. **Money** â€” Do you have enough money to do it?
3. **Physical effort** â€” Are you physically capable of doing it easily?
4. **Mental effort** â€” Does it require a lot of creative or mental energy / hard thinking?
5. **Routine** â€” Does it fit your current routine, or does it force you to rearrange your life?

"Hard to do" doesn't only mean *very* hard â€” it means *any* amount of hard that's enough to keep you from doing the behavior.

## The weakest-link demonstration

Fogg's own flossing case: a seven-minute workout "sounds easy," but break it down â€” Time is fine (seven minutes fits a day), Money is fine (free at home), Routine may be fine â€” yet **Physical effort** is the weak link (the apps push you hard), and that single weak link can derail the habit. For Fogg's flossing: time, money, mental effort, and routine were all *strong* links, but **physical effort** was weak â€” his teeth have "close contacts," so the floss shredded and fought him. That one wobbly link was enough for him to "blow off flossing for months at a time."

Crucially, the fix wasn't more motivation or self-blame â€” it was **shoring up the specific weak link**: after sampling about fifteen types, he found a thin floss that slid between his teeth. The weak link strengthened, the behavior got easy, and the habit formed.

This is why diagnosis beats brute force: you can waste enormous motivation on a behavior whose real problem is a single cheap-to-fix ability factor.

## Make it easier: the three moves (the Breakthrough Question)

After the Discovery Question, ask the **Breakthrough Question: "How can I make this behavior easier to do?"** There are three answers:

1. **Increase your skills** â€” get better at the behavior so it costs less effort.
2. **Get tools and resources** â€” change the environment or buy the right gear (the thinner floss; a faster app) so the weak link goes away.
3. **Shrink the behavior** â€” make the behavior itself smaller. This is the most reliable and the one Tiny Habits leans on.

### Shrinking the behavior â€” two ways

- **Starter Step.** Do only the *first tiny piece*. Want to walk daily? The Starter Step is just putting your walking shoes on. That's the whole required behavior. It's "mental jujitsu" â€” the Starter Step itself is success, and it often pulls the larger behavior along behind it. (Sarika put a pan on the stove as the Starter Step to cooking breakfast â€” that was all, the first few days.)
- **Scaling Back.** Do a *miniature version* of the whole behavior â€” walk to the mailbox instead of around the block; one wall push-up instead of a workout. When a setback hits (a cold), you can still do the scaled-back version "stuffy nose and all," so consistency survives.

Either way works. The point is to land the behavior firmly above the Action Line so it happens and roots itself; you can grow it later.

## How to apply

**To diagnose a behavior that won't stick:**
1. Ask the Discovery Question: *what is making this hard to do?*
2. Walk the five factors â€” Time, Money, Physical effort, Mental effort, Routine â€” and find the **weakest link**.
3. Don't assume the obvious link. People assume "time"; often it's mental effort (too much deciding) or routine (it doesn't fit) or physical effort.

**To make it easier:**
1. Ask the Breakthrough Question and pick a move: build skill, add tools/resources, or shrink the behavior.
2. For habits, default to **shrink** â€” find a Starter Step or scale back until the behavior is bad-day-proof.
3. Re-check the Action Line: is the shrunk behavior now easy enough to clear it with the motivation you actually have?

**To design for others (or a product):** apply the same chain to *their* context, not yours. A behavior easy for you may have a weak Money or Physical-effort link for them. Reducing required effort, steps, time, and decisions is the most reliable way to raise completion â€” the same logic Eyal applies in the Hook Model's Action phase (`eyal-hook-model.md`).

## Common failure modes

- **Treating ability as one thing ("just make it simple").** Simplicity is specific. Without finding the actual weak link you'll "simplify" the wrong factor and nothing changes.
- **Assuming the weak link is time.** Time is the default scapegoat; often the real blocker is mental effort or routine fit.
- **Solving a motivation problem when it's an ability problem (and vice versa).** Fogg didn't lack motivation to floss â€” he lacked easy floss. Misdiagnosis wastes effort.
- **Starting too big.** A big behavior with shallow roots topples in the first storm; refusing to shrink keeps the behavior below the Action Line on bad days.
