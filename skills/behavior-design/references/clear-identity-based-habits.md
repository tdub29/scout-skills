## The framework

> "Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become." (*Atomic Habits*, Chapter 2)

Most habit attempts fail because we try to change the wrong thing. Clear maps **three layers of behavior change**, like the layers of an onion:

1. **Outcomes** â€” what you *get* (lose weight, publish a book, win a championship). Most goals live here.
2. **Processes** â€” what you *do* (a new gym routine, a meditation practice, decluttering a desk). Most habits live here.
3. **Identity** â€” what you *believe* (your worldview, self-image, judgments about yourself). Most beliefs and biases live here.

"Outcomes are about what you get. Processes are about what you do. Identity is about what you believe." All three layers matter; the problem is the **direction of change**.

## Change identity, not just outcomes

Most people change from the **outside in**: they start with an outcome ("I want to be skinny") and a process ("if I diet, I'll be skinny") but never touch the belief underneath. Identity-based habits invert this â€” you change from the **inside out**, starting with **who you wish to become**.

The two smokers illustrate it: one declines a cigarette with "No thanks, I'm trying to quit" (still a smoker fighting their identity); the other says "No thanks, I'm not a smoker" (the identity has already shifted). The second statement signals that smoking is no longer who they are.

> "Behavior that is incongruent with the self will not last." (Ch 2)

You may want more money, but if your identity is someone who consumes rather than creates, you'll be pulled toward spending. **The ultimate form of intrinsic motivation is when a habit becomes part of your identity** â€” "It's one thing to say *I'm the type of person who wants this*. It's something very different to say *I'm the type of person who is this*." Pride locks it in: once you take pride in an aspect of your identity, you fight to maintain the habits that support it.

Identity is a double-edged sword. The same mechanism produces limiting self-talk â€” "I'm not a morning person," "I'm horrible at math" â€” that makes you resist actions because "that's not who I am." The biggest barrier to positive change at any level is **identity conflict**: good habits that clash with your self-image will fail.

## Habits as votes; identity as "repeated beingness"

> "Your identity is literally your 'repeated beingness.'" (Ch 2)

Your identity *emerges out of* your habits. Every belief about yourself is learned through experience, and your habits are how you **embody** that identity. Whatever your identity is now, you believe it because you have **evidence** (proof) of it: go to church for twenty years and you have evidence you're religious; study biology every night and you have evidence you're studious.

So each habit is "like a suggestion: Hey, maybe this is who I am." **Every action is a vote** for the type of person you wish to become. No single vote transforms your beliefs, but as votes accumulate, so does the evidence of a new identity. Crucially, you don't need a unanimous vote â€” just a majority. A few votes for a bad habit don't ruin the election; "your goal is simply to win the majority of the time."

The reframes:
- The goal is not to read a book â€” the goal is to **become a reader**.
- The goal is not to run a marathon â€” the goal is to **become a runner**.
- The goal is not to learn an instrument â€” the goal is to **become a musician**.

## How to apply

The two-step process to change identity:
1. **Decide the type of person you want to be.** If you only know the outcome you want (six-pack abs, double your salary), work backward: "Who is the type of person that could get the outcome I want?" Writing a book â†’ "someone consistent and reliable." Your focus shifts from the outcome to *being that kind of person*.
2. **Prove it to yourself with small wins.** Cast votes. The friend who lost 100 pounds asked all day, "What would a healthy person do?" â€” and eventually became that person.

"Identity change is the North Star of habit change." The first question is not *what* or *how* but **who**. Let your values and identity drive the feedback loop (habits shape identity; identity shapes habits), rather than letting results drive it.

## Common failure modes

- **Outside-in only** â€” chasing an outcome with a process while the old identity quietly sabotages the new plan.
- **Clinging to a fixed identity** â€” limiting beliefs ("I'm not a X person") that veto useful actions. (See `clear-advanced-goldilocks-review.md` for keeping identity flexible.)
- **Demanding a unanimous vote** â€” treating one lapse as proof you're "not that person," instead of aiming for the majority.
- **Reward that conflicts with identity** â€” celebrating a workout with ice cream casts votes for two opposed identities; pick reinforcers that confirm who you want to be.
