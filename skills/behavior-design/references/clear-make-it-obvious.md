## The framework

> "The two most common cues are time and location." (*Atomic Habits*, Chapter 5)

The 1st Law of Behavior Change tunes the **cue** stage of the habit loop: make the cues of good habits obvious and visible. It has four tactics â€” the Habits Scorecard, implementation intentions, habit stacking, and environment design â€” plus the inversion (make bad cues invisible).

## Awareness first: the Habits Scorecard

You can't change a habit you don't see. "The process of behavior change always starts with awareness." The **Habits Scorecard** is a simple exercise: list your daily habits, then mark each "+" (good), "â€“" (bad), or "=" (neutral). The marks depend on your goals â€” there are "no good habits or bad habits, there are only effective habits" â€” so the test is: "Does this habit cast a vote for or against my desired identity?" Make no changes at first; just notice, without judgment.

A complementary tool is **Pointing-and-Calling**: say the action and its outcome out loud ("I'm about to eat this cookie, but I don't need it"). Verbalizing raises a nonconscious habit to a conscious level, adding weight so you don't mindlessly slip into the routine.

## Implementation intentions

> "I will [BEHAVIOR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]." (Ch 5)

An **implementation intention** is a plan made beforehand about *when and where* to act. In the British exercise study, the control and motivation groups exercised at 35â€“38 percent; the group that filled in "I will exercise for 20 minutes on [DAY] at [TIME] in [PLACE]" hit **91 percent**. The general format is "When situation X arises, I will perform response Y."

The insight: many people think they lack motivation when what they really lack is **clarity** â€” it's not obvious when and where to act. An implementation intention sweeps away vague aims ("I want to work out more") into a concrete plan, so when the moment comes there's no decision to make â€” you just follow the plan. (Tip: starting on the first day of a week, month, or year helps, because hope â€” and motivation â€” is higher.)

## Habit stacking

> "After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]." (Ch 5)

**Habit stacking** is a special form of implementation intention: instead of pairing the new habit with a time/location, you pair it with a **current habit** that already happens reliably. Clear credits the method to BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits program. It exploits the connectedness of behavior â€” each action becomes the cue for the next (a positive version of the Diderot Effect). Examples: "After I pour my morning coffee, I will meditate for one minute"; "After I take off my work shoes, I will immediately change into my workout clothes."

You can chain stacks into routines and even insert a habit into the middle of an existing routine (Wake up â†’ Make bed â†’ *Place a book on my pillow* â†’ Take a shower). The secret is selecting the right cue: it must be **highly specific and immediately actionable** ("When I close my laptop for lunch, I will do ten push-ups next to my desk," not the vague "When I take a break for lunch"), and it must have the **same frequency** as the desired habit (don't stack a daily habit on a Monday-only cue).

## Environment design

> "Environment is the invisible hand that shapes human behavior." (Ch 6)

People often choose products "not because of what they are, but because of where they are." Anne Thorndike's hospital cafeteria added water next to every drink station without saying a word to anyone â€” soda sales fell 11.4 percent, water rose 25.8 percent. Vision is the dominant sense (â‰ˆ10 of 11 million sensory receptors), so **visual cues are the greatest catalyst of behavior**: apples in a bowl on the counter get eaten; apples in the crisper rot unseen.

So make the cues for good habits obvious: pill bottle next to the faucet, guitar stand in the middle of the living room, workout clothes laid out. You are not the victim of your environment â€” you can be its architect. Where possible, give each context one dominant use so its cues pull a single behavior.

## Inversion: make it invisible

The inversion of the 1st Law is **make bad cues invisible** â€” the most practical way to break a bad habit is to **reduce exposure to the cue** that triggers it. People with high self-control aren't superhuman; they "spend less time in tempting situations." Leave the phone in another room; unfollow accounts that trigger envy; move the TV out of the bedroom. "Self-control is a short-term strategy, not a long-term one" â€” "it's easier to avoid temptation than resist it." Often, "remove a single cue and the entire habit fades away."

## How to apply

1. **Run the Habits Scorecard** to surface the cues already firing.
2. **Write an implementation intention** ("I will [behavior] at [time] in [location]") or, better, a **habit stack** anchored to a reliable, specific current habit.
3. **Redesign the environment** so good-habit cues are visible and unmissable.
4. **For a bad habit, invert:** make its cue invisible by removing it from the environment.

## Common failure modes

- **Vague cues.** "In the morning" or "when I take a break" aren't events; the cue must be a precise, reliably occurring action.
- **Frequency mismatch.** Stacking a daily habit onto a weekly cue.
- **Relying on willpower over environment.** Trying to resist an ever-present cue instead of removing it.
- **Skipping awareness.** Trying to change habits you haven't yet made conscious via the Scorecard or Pointing-and-Calling.
