## The framework

> "A behavior happens when the three elements of MAP â€” Motivation, Ability, and Prompt â€” come together at the same moment." (*Tiny Habits*, Chapter 1)

The Fogg Behavior Model reduces every human action to three variables and one rule:

**B = MAP** â€” a Behavior occurs only when **M**otivation, **A**bility, and **P**rompt converge at the same instant.

- **Motivation (M):** your desire to do the behavior.
- **Ability (A):** your capacity to do the behavior (how easy it is right now).
- **Prompt (P):** the cue that tells you to do it now.

Fogg's own demonstration: in 2010 he donated to the Red Cross from the elliptical machine. **M** â€” he wanted to help earthquake victims. **A** â€” replying to a text was trivially easy. **P** â€” a text message asked him to. All three converged, so the behavior happened. Remove any one â€” make it a phone call requiring a credit card in his car (kills ability), or junk mail he never opens (kills prompt) â€” and the behavior dies. The campaign raised over $21M in a week because it engineered M, A, and P together.

The model is **universal**: good habits and bad habits, easy and hard behaviors, run on the same three parts. "Behaviors are like bicycles. They can look different, but the core mechanisms are the same." This matters because it means a stuck behavior is a *design* problem, not a *character* problem â€” you stop blaming willpower and start adjusting the dials.

## The Action Line (the motivationâ€“ability tradeoff)

Plot motivation on the vertical axis and ability (ease) on the horizontal axis. A curved **Action Line** separates "behavior happens when prompted" (above) from "behavior fails when prompted" (below).

- The **more motivated** you are, the harder a behavior you'll do when prompted (a mother lifting a car off her child).
- The **harder** a behavior is, the **less likely** you'll do it â€” and you need more motivation to clear the bar.
- **Motivation and ability are teammates and compensate** for each other: if one is weak, the other must be strong to land above the Action Line.
- **Behaviors that become habits reliably fall above the Action Line** â€” and the easier a behavior is, the more likely it becomes a habit. "The easier a behavior is to do, the more likely the behavior will become habit."

Key design insight: motivation is unreliable â€” it spikes and crashes ("the Motivation Monkey"). Ability and prompts are far more controllable. So Behavior Design leans on **making things easy** and **engineering prompts**, not on pumping up motivation.

## Why the prompt is the trigger

> "No prompt, no behavior." (Chapter 1)

Motivation and ability are *continuous* â€” you always have some level of each in the background. A **prompt is like lightning**: it comes and goes. Without a prompt, your motivation and ability are irrelevant in that moment because nothing fires the action. Phone rings â†’ you answer; you don't hear it â†’ you don't, no matter how willing and able you are.

This makes the prompt the **first lever** for both building and breaking habits:

- **To build:** attach a reliable prompt (Fogg uses an existing routine as an "Anchor" â€” see `fogg-tiny-habits-recipe.md`).
- **To break:** **remove the prompt** first. Fogg disrupted his hotel-snack habit not by killing motivation (he loves salty snacks) or by force, but by hiding the goody basket in a closed cabinet â€” out of sight, no prompt, behavior gone. "Prompts are the low-hanging fruit of Behavior Design."

## How to apply

**To analyze why a behavior happens (or fails):**
1. Name the specific behavior and the exact moment.
2. Score each element: Is there a prompt? Is motivation present? Is it easy enough to do?
3. The missing/weak element is your culprit. No prompt â†’ nothing fires. Below the Action Line â†’ it's too hard for the motivation available.

**To design a wanted behavior:**
1. Make it as **easy** as possible (the most durable lever â€” see `fogg-ability-simplify.md`).
2. Attach a **reliable prompt** (anchor it to an existing routine).
3. Treat motivation as a bonus, not the plan.

**To disrupt an unwanted behavior, attack in this order:**
1. **Remove the prompt** (easiest, most reliable).
2. **Make it harder** (degrade ability â€” phone across the room, delete the app).
3. **Reduce motivation** (hardest and least reliable).
   Katie's 4:30am scroll: motivation was baked-in (Facebook feels good), so she couldn't demotivate. She made it harder (phone in the kitchen) and removed the prompt (old-fashioned alarm clock instead of phone alarm). Behavior gone; her dormant exercise habit returned on its own.

**To detect the model at work on you:** when you can't stop a behavior, find the dot far above the Action Line â€” usually high motivation + high ease + a reliable prompt (the phone alarm that becomes a scroll prompt). The fix is rarely "try harder."

## Common failure modes

- **Blaming character or willpower.** The whole point of B=MAP is that a failed behavior is a missing or weak *element*, not a moral failing. Diagnosing "I have no discipline" instead of "I had no prompt" guarantees you fix the wrong thing.
- **Reaching for motivation first.** Motivation is the least reliable dial. Designs that depend on staying motivated collapse the moment motivation dips.
- **Ignoring the prompt.** People obsess over wanting it and being able to do it, then forget there was no cue to do it *now*. No prompt, no behavior.
- **Treating a snapshot as a trajectory.** B=MAP describes one behavior at one moment; chaining behaviors over time (B1 â†’ B2 â†’ B3) needs each link to clear its own Action Line.

## Ethical guardrail

B=MAP is value-neutral â€” it builds flossing habits and scrolling compulsions identically. When you engineer M, A, and P for *another person* (especially via a product prompt), you are steering behavior below their deliberation. Pair this model with the Manipulation Matrix (`eyal-manipulation-matrix.md`): identify the behavior you're prompting, ask whether it materially improves their life, and ask whether you'd accept that prompt yourself.
