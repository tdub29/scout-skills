## The framework

> "What distinguishes the Hook Model from a plain vanilla feedback loop is the hook's ability to create a craving." (*Hooked*, Chapter 4)

The third phase of the Hook rewards the user by solving the problem that motivated the action â€” but the reward must be **variable**. Predictable feedback loops don't create desire (your fridge light turning on doesn't make you keep opening the door); add unpredictability â€” a different treat appears each time â€” and intrigue is created.

The neuroscience: the **nucleus accumbens** (the brain's craving center) spikes dopamine **in anticipation** of a reward, not on receiving it (Knutson's fMRI gambling study; Olds & Milner's lever-pressing mice). What compels us is "the stress of desire" â€” the need to relieve the craving. **Variability multiplies the effect**, suppressing the judgment/reason regions and activating wanting and desire. Skinner's pigeons pressed a lever far more often when the food pellet came on a *random* schedule than on a fixed one â€” intermittent reward dramatically increases the frequency of the action.

Without variability we habituate and disengage: a child delighted by a new puppy stops finding it thrilling once its behavior becomes predictable. "To hold our attention, products must have an ongoing degree of novelty."

## The three types of variable reward

Habit-forming products use one or more of these:

1. **Rewards of the Tribe (social rewards)** â€” we seek to feel accepted, attractive, important, and included. Driven by connectedness and **social learning theory** (Bandura): we copy behaviors we see rewarded, especially in people like us. Examples: Facebook's endless variable feed and the variable count of Likes/comments (a reward for *creators* too); Stack Overflow's upvotes, points, and badges conferring tribe status; League of Legends' Honor Points. The variability of *how much* social validation you'll get is the engine.
2. **Rewards of the Hunt** â€” the search for *resources* (material goods or information). Rooted in "persistence hunting" â€” humans evolved to chase. Examples: the variable scroll of a feed or search results (you keep hunting for the next good thing), Twitter's timeline, Pinterest's bottomless boards, slot machines (the literal hunt for money), bargain-shopping.
3. **Rewards of the Self** â€” intrinsic gratification: mastery, completion, competency, consistency. Fueled by **intrinsic motivation** (Deci & Ryan's self-determination theory â€” the desire for competence). Examples: video-game leveling/unlocking (World of Warcraft progression), the drive to clear an email inbox to zero, finishing a jigsaw puzzle for no prize but completion. Adding mystery to the goal makes the pursuit more enticing.

## Finite vs infinite variability

A critical distinction for *sustained* engagement:

- **Finite variability** â€” an experience that becomes **predictable after use**. Once the mystery is exhausted, engagement wanes. *Breaking Bad* enthralls until you know the ending; a single-player game ends; a TV series concludes. Businesses with finite variability aren't inferior, but they "operate under different constraints": they must **constantly churn out new content** to feed the appetite for novelty (Hollywood and gaming run a "studio model" of bets on the next hit).
- **Infinite variability** â€” an experience that **sustains variability with use**, so it stays unpredictable. World of Warcraft played *with other people* stays interesting because human behavior is hard to predict; **content creation** (Dribbble, and the user-generated streams of YouTube/Facebook/Pinterest/Twitter) is infinitely variable because there's always new user-made material.

> Products with infinite variability stand a better chance of holding attention; those with finite variability must constantly reinvent themselves to keep pace.

The general pattern: **other people** and **user-generated content** are the most reliable sources of infinite variability.

## How to apply

1. **Make the reward variable, not predictable.** A fixed, guaranteed reward is a feedback loop, not a hook â€” it won't create craving. Introduce uncertainty in *what* or *how much* the user gets.
2. **Pick the reward type(s) that fit the user's internal trigger.** Loneliness â†’ Tribe; uncertainty/seeking â†’ Hunt; desire for mastery/completion â†’ Self. Many strong products blend types.
3. **Engineer for infinite variability** if you want durable retention â€” lean on social interaction and user-generated content rather than a finite content library you must constantly refill.
4. **Preserve user autonomy.** Variable rewards work best when the user retains a "sense of control"; manipulative pressure that removes choice backfires.
5. **To detect it:** the bottomless scroll, the pull-to-refresh, the streaks, the unpredictable Like counts, the "you have new..." â€” name which reward type (Tribe/Hunt/Self) is being exploited and whether the variability is finite or infinite.

## Common failure modes

- **Predictable rewards.** No variability means no craving â€” the most common reason a "rewarding" feature doesn't retain.
- **Mismatched reward type.** Offering Self-rewards (badges, points) to users who came for Tribe (real social validation) produces hollow gamification that doesn't stick (the classic Peddler mistake â€” see `eyal-manipulation-matrix.md`).
- **Relying on finite variability without a content engine.** If your variability runs out and you can't churn new content fast enough, engagement decays.
- **Crushing autonomy.** Coercive variable-reward mechanics (dark-pattern loot boxes, manipulative scarcity) erode trust and invite backlash and regulation.

## Ethical guardrail

Variable rewards are the most directly addictive element of the Hook â€” the same mechanism as slot machines. Designing them obligates the Manipulation Matrix check (`eyal-manipulation-matrix.md`): does the variable reward serve a problem that materially improves the user's life, or does it just farm engagement (and "extract cash")? Engineering craving for a behavior the user wouldn't endorse on reflection is a Dealer move. Prefer rewards tied to genuine user value and preserve the user's sense of control.
