## The framework

> "Help people do what they already want to do." (Fogg Maxim #1, *Tiny Habits*, Chapter 2)

People fail at change because they pick the wrong behavior, not because they lack discipline. An **aspiration** ("reduce my stress," "be healthier") is not a behavior â€” and neither is an **outcome** ("lose 10 pounds"). You can't *do* an aspiration or an outcome; you can only do **specific behaviors** that, over time, lead to them. Behavior Design's job is to **match** a person to the *right* specific behaviors â€” ones high in both impact and feasibility â€” instead of guessing, copying an internet guru, or doing what worked for a friend (Fogg's three "wrong ways" to choose a behavior).

The output of this process is **Golden Behaviors**: two or three specific behaviors that are both **effective** (high impact toward the aspiration) and **realistic** (you can actually get yourself to do them). You design for those and set the rest aside.

This runs in two methods: **Magic Wanding** (generate options) and **Focus Mapping** (select the golden few).

## Step 1 â€” Magic Wanding (the Swarm of Behaviors)

Start from a clear aspiration written in a cloud (e.g., Mark writes "reduce my stress"). Then ask the magic-wand question:

> "If you could wave a magic wand and get yourself to do **any** behavior that would [achieve your aspiration], what would it be?"

For each answer, say only **"Great. What else?"** â€” don't evaluate, don't go deep. Keep generating to build a **Swarm of Behaviors** (the worksheet has ~10 boxes; more is better). Be wildly optimistic â€” you have magical powers, so list anything (move to Maui, get a weekly massage, play guitar ten minutes a day). Use three prompts to widen the swarm:

- What behaviors would you do **one time**? (download a meditation app, cancel cable)
- What **new habits** would you create? (stretch after every call)
- What habit would you **stop**? (no email after 7pm)

You can recruit others ("if you could get me to do any behavior that helps me ____, what would it be?"). Then make each wish **"crispy"** â€” specific enough to picture ("play fetch with my dog each evening at home," not "play with my dog").

## Step 2 â€” Focus Mapping (impact Ã— feasibility)

Focus Mapping is Fogg's favorite method â€” the best tool for matching yourself to Golden Behaviors. Write each swarm behavior on its own card, then plot on a 2Ã—2 landscape in **two rounds**:

**Round one â€” Impact (vertical).** Considering *only* effectiveness, ask of each card: *How effective is this behavior at achieving my aspiration?* Slide it up (high impact) or down (low impact). Ignore feasibility entirely in this round. (Mark puts "play guitar 10 min/day" high â€” it reliably lifts his mood; "leave work 15 min early" low â€” it might stress him more on deadline.)

**Round two â€” Feasibility (horizontal).** Now ask of each card: **"Can I get myself to do this behavior?"** This single question deliberately bundles *both* B=MAP dials â€” **motivation** ("Do I want to do this?") and **ability** ("Can I realistically do this in my actual life?"). Slide cards left (hard / unlikely) or right (easy / likely). Be **Real-You**, not Fantasy-You: imagine rolling out of bed 20 minutes before dashing out, not the idealized version who makes oatmeal at dawn.

The behaviors that land in the **high-impact + high-feasibility** corner are your **Golden Behaviors** â€” two or three of them. Those are what you design Tiny Habits for; everything else is set aside.

## Why this beats willpower

The feasibility question's *want* dimension is the key. **Match yourself to behaviors you already want to do, not ones you think you should do.** When motivation is already embedded in the behavior, you never have to bolt on motivational tricks later â€” you "take the Motivation Monkey out of commission." Other approaches pick a "should" behavior and then struggle to manufacture motivation forever; Behavior Design picks behaviors where the motivation is already present, so the habit is durable by design. (If dancing to BeyoncÃ© for five minutes is the exercise you *want*, make that the habit and forget the treadmill you dread.)

This is the operational meaning of **Maxim #1: Help people do what they already want to do.**

## How to apply

1. **Name the aspiration or outcome** clearly and revise it until it taps what really matters (you might think it's "be more mindful" when it's really "feel less rushed").
2. **Magic Wand** a wide Swarm of Behaviors â€” "Great, what else?" â€” then make each crispy/specific.
3. **Focus Map** in two rounds: impact first (fantasy allowed), feasibility second (Real-You only; "can I get myself to do this?").
4. **Pick 2â€“3 Golden Behaviors** from the high-impact + high-feasibility corner.
5. **Hand each Golden Behavior to the Tiny Habits recipe** (`fogg-tiny-habits-recipe.md`): shrink it, anchor it, celebrate it.

**To detect it / coach others:** when someone is "trying to get motivated" to do a behavior, that's the signal they picked a *should*, not a *want*. Re-run behavior matching to find a golden behavior they're already motivated to do.

## Common failure modes

- **Designing for an aspiration or outcome directly.** "Be healthier" is not actionable; you must decompose it into specific behaviors first.
- **Collapsing impact and feasibility into one judgment.** Doing both rounds at once kills good high-impact ideas prematurely and keeps low-impact easy ones. Separate the rounds.
- **Plotting feasibility as Fantasy-You.** Rating "make fresh oatmeal daily" as feasible because the idealized you would â€” Real-You won't, and the habit dies.
- **Picking shoulds over wants.** Choosing a high-impact behavior you dread guarantees a motivation battle later. Match to wants.
- **Guessing instead of mapping.** The three "wrong ways" â€” random guessing, internet inspiration, copying a friend â€” skip the matching step and produce poorly matched behaviors.
