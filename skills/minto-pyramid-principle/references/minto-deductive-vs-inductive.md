## The framework

> "These two forms of reasoning are the only patterns available for establishing logical relationships between ideas." (Ch 5, Deduction and Induction: The Difference)

Every horizontal grouping in the pyramid is held together by exactly one of two logics â€” never both at once. **Deduction** is a line of reasoning leading to a "therefore"; the point above summarizes it, resting heavily on the final step. **Induction** is a set of like ideas summarized by what their sameness implies. "The deductive points derive from each other; the inductive points do not" (Ch 5).

### How deduction works

A deductive argument does three things (Ch 5, How It Works â€” Minto avoids "syllogism/major/minor premise" as confusing):

1. Make a statement about a situation that exists in the world.
2. Make another statement that *comments on the subject or predicate of the first*.
3. State the implication of those two situations existing at the same time ("therefore").

> - Men are mortal.
> - Socrates is a man.
> - Therefore Socrates is mortal.

To summarize a deductive grouping you make a statement "resting heavily on the final point" ("Because Socrates is a man, he is mortal"). Arguments can be **chained** (skipping obvious steps) â€” permissible "provided your reader is likely to grasp and agree with the missing steps" â€” but with two hard limits: **no more than four points** in a deductive argument, and **no more than two "therefore" points chained** (Ch 5, When to Use It), or "the groupings will be too heavy to summarize effectively."

### How induction works

An inductive grouping "takes a set of ideas that are related simply by virtue of the fact that you can describe them all by the same plural noun" (Ch 5):

> - French tanks are at the Polish border.
> - German tanks are at the Polish border.
> - Russian tanks are at the Polish border.
> â†’ Inference: "Poland is about to be invaded by tanks."

To summarize, you "draw an inference based on your assessment of what is the same about the points." The two skills of good induction: **defining the ideas in the grouping** (finding the one plural noun that fits) and **identifying the misfits** (Ch 5, Inductive Reasoning). The inference must not "go beyond the grouping" â€” it must make a statement *specifically and only* about the items below it.

### How to tell them apart

> "If you are thinking deductively, your second point will always comment on the subject or predicate of the first. If it does not so comment, you should be able to classify it by the same plural noun as the first, to test that you have a proper inductive grouping." (Ch 5, How It Differs)

With inductive ideas you "hold the subject constant and vary the predicate, or hold the predicate constant and vary the subject." A telltale: *"Japanese businessmen are escalating their drive for the Chinese market"* + *"American businessmen areâ€¦"* is **inductive** (same predicate, varied subject); + *"American businessmen entering will stimulate them further"* is **deductive** (comments on the first). And if the items share nothing (China, Iceland, Peru with no common thread), they aren't related at all â€” "you are simply passing along news, and there is no place for news in a document whose purpose is to communicate your thinking."

### When to use each

**On the key line, prefer induction.** "Try to avoid using a deductive argument, and strive instead always to present your message inductively... because it is easier on the reader" (Ch 5, When to Use It). Deduction is "a useful way to think but a ponderous way to write" â€” it makes "a mystery story out of what should be a straightforward point." A deductively-structured report forces the reader to hold the A-B-Cs of what's wrong, match each to its cause, then match each to the fix, before learning what to do Monday morning.

The key insight: **deduction and induction are not different reasoning â€” only different layouts.** Your problem-solving produces a Recommendation Worksheet (findings â†’ conclusions â†’ recommendations); presenting it *deductively* means reading column by column ("why you should change, then how"), while presenting it *inductively* means turning the worksheet "90 degrees to the left" and putting recommendations on the key line with their support beneath ("that you should change, and why each change makes sense"). "As a rule of thumb, it is always better to present the action before the argument, since that is what the reader cares about."

**Use deduction on the key line only in two cases** (Ch 5):
1. The point is **alien** to what the reader expects ("Forget about cutting costs â€” sell the business"). He'll ask *Why? How? Are you sure?* before any action makes sense.
2. The reader **cannot understand the action without prior explanation** (e.g., risk-analysis method he must grasp before the steps mean anything).

Otherwise, **push deduction as low in the pyramid as possible.** "At the paragraph level deductive arguments are lovely... but inductive reasoning is always easier to absorb at higher levels." Deduction reaching you directly ("I am a bird, therefore I fly") is fine; deduction spread across ten pages between steps "loses its instant clarity."

### Deduction masquerading as induction

A grouping that looks inductive can hide a deductive chain â€” "low productivity led to high overtime, which led to uncompetitive prices" presented as three parallel "indicators." Whenever you have only one piece of evidence for something, "you are forced to deal with it deductively." Detect it by testing whether each point comments on the one before (deductive) or is merely the same kind of thing (inductive).

## How to apply

1. **Classify each grouping: deductive or inductive.** Does point 2 comment on point 1's subject/predicate (deductive), or is it another member of the same plural-noun class (inductive)?
2. **Default the key line to induction.** Put the recommendation/conclusion on top and the like-kind support beneath. Reserve deduction for the two exceptions (alien point; reader needs the reasoning first).
3. **Present action before argument.** Mentally "rotate the worksheet 90Â°": lead with recommendations, not the why-chain.
4. **Push deduction down.** Keep "therefore" chains at the paragraph level, close to where the steps sit, never spanning sections.
5. **Honor the deductive limits.** â‰¤4 points; â‰¤2 chained "therefores"; skip a step only if the reader will supply it.
6. **Cut the misfits and the news.** In an inductive group, drop items that don't fit the plural noun; if items share nothing, they don't belong in the document.

## Common failure modes

- **Deductive key line by default.** Forcing the reader to re-walk your entire problem-solving sequence before reaching the action.
- **Inference beyond the grouping.** A summary that claims more than the items support ("managers mismanage because they want to" from a list of mismanagement examples) â€” "certainly not; it's sloppy reasoning."
- **A "grouping" with no relationship.** Three true but unconnected facts (the China/Iceland/Peru list) â€” news, not thinking; no inference possible.
- **Over-chaining deduction.** More than four points or more than two stacked "therefores" â€” too heavy to summarize.
- **An "overstructured" deduction.** Including a third point that raises no question and isn't needed for clarity â€” a signal the inductive form would communicate more efficiently (Ch 3, Caveat 6: the warehouse example).
