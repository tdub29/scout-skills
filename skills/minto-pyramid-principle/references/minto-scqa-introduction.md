## The framework

> "This classic pattern of story-telling â€” Situation, Complication, Question, Answer â€” permits you to make sure that you and the reader are 'standing in the same place' before you take him by the hand and lead him through your reasoning." (Ch 2, The Introductory Flow)

The pyramid answers exactly one question. The introduction's job is to establish *that question* â€” and to do it as a short story the reader already half-knows, so the apex of your pyramid lands as the obvious answer. The introduction "tells the reader, in story form, what he already knows or could reasonably be expected to know about the subject... and thus reminds him of the question he has to which he can expect the document to give him an answer" (Ch 2).

### The narrative flow

Every good story "establishes a situation, introduces a complication, and offers a resolution. The resolution will always be your major point" (Ch 4, The Story Form). The four moves:

- **Situation (S)** â€” an established, noncontroversial truth about the subject. You "begin by establishing for the reader the time and place." It must be something the reader knows or will instantly accept; if you can't open with a statement about the subject, "either you have the wrong subject, or you're starting in the wrong place to discuss it" (Ch 4, Where Do You Start the Situation?). The general reader response you want: *"Yes, I'm sure that's true, but so what?"*
- **Complication (C)** â€” "what happened next in the story that inevitably leads to a Question." Not a "problem" in the everyday sense necessarily; it is the tension in the story. It is "the last thing known by the reader," which is why it sits next to the Question (Ch 4, What's a Complication?).
- **Question (Q)** â€” raised in the reader's mind by the Complication. There is "always only one to a document." If you seem to have two, they must be related ("Should we enter the market, and if so, how?" is really "How should we enter the market?").
- **Answer (A)** â€” the apex of your pyramid. It is the resolution of the story and the point that, once stated, raises the *new* question the key line answers.

### Why a story (and why "remind, not inform")

The introduction is meant **to remind, not to inform** â€” "nothing should be included that would have to be proved to the reader" (Ch 4). Telling the reader things he already agrees with, before things he might dispute, makes him receptive: "Easy reading of agreeable points is apt to render him more receptive to your ideas than confused plodding through a morass of detail." The story flow also "lends a feeling of plausibility" and "a sense of inevitable rightness to the logic of the writer's conclusion... in much the same way that a trial lawyer's opening statement seeks to give the jury a framework in which to receive the evidence to come" (Ch 4, end). Three rules: introductions *remind* (no exhibits, nothing to be proved); they *always contain the three story elements*; their *length depends on the reader's needs and the subject's demands* (Ch 4).

### The four common questions

Most documents answer one of four questions; the Complication is "usually a variation" on these (Ch 4, Exhibit 10 / Some Common Patterns):

| Situation | Complication | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Have a task to perform | Something blocks the task | What should we do? |
| Have a problem | We know the solution | How do we implement it? |
| Have a problem | A solution has been suggested | Is it the right solution? |
| Took an action | The action didn't work | Why not? |

In business these surface as: **giving direction** (What/How should we do it? â€” often *plants* the question rather than reminding), **seeking approval to spend money** (Should I approve? â€” supported by: resolution can't wait / this solves it / cost is offset by savings / other goodies), **explaining "how to"** (lay out the present process and the ideal; the differences become the key-line steps), and **choosing among alternatives** (structure the key line around the *criteria* used to judge, or around *objectives* if no option wins on everything â€” never around "alternative ways to solve the problem").

### Variants of the SCQA pattern

Once you know the S, C, and Q, you can **reorder them** in the writing to set the tone (Ch 4, Why That Order?). Begin your *thinking* with the Situation, but vary the *written* order:

- **Standard â€” S-C-Q-A.** Neutral, narrative.
- **Direct â€” A-S-C** (solution first). Confident, leads with the recommendation.
- **Concerned â€” C-S-Q-A** (complication first). Opens on the tension/risk.
- **Aggressive â€” Q-S-C** (question first). Provocative, challenges the reader.

The order changes the tone, never the elements. **Key-line points also get their own brief introductions** â€” each follows the same S-C-Q logic, written to remind the reader why *this* subject is relevant given where he's now standing in the document (Ch 4, Do I Need to Introduce the Key Line Points?).

## How to apply

1. **Write S, C, Q, A out as four lines first.** Start with the Situation (a truth the reader accepts), then ask "so what?" to surface the Complication, confirm the Question the Complication raises, and state the Answer (your apex).
2. **Verify the loop closes.** The Complication must *immediately* raise the Question you wrote; if not, you have the wrong Complication or Question â€” rework until they snap together (Ch 3, top-down procedure). "The purpose of the entire exercise is to make sure you know what Question it is you are trying to answer."
3. **Keep the introduction reminding, not informing.** No new claims, no evidence, no exhibits â€” only what the reader already knows or will accept.
4. **Enforce one question.** If two questions appear, relate them ("â€¦and if so, how?") or split the document.
5. **Choose the reorder for tone.** Default to S-C-Q-A; go direct/concerned/aggressive deliberately when the situation calls for it.
6. **Introduce each key-line point.** A sentence or two of S-C-Q to re-anchor the reader before each major section.
7. **Put historical chronology in the introduction.** The body can hold only ideas (logical relationships); "simple historical occurrences do not exist as the result of logical thought" and belong in the intro (Ch 3, Caveat 4).

## Common failure modes

- **Skipping the introduction.** Jumping straight to the key line; you then "end up structuring information that properly belongs in the Situation or Complication, and forcing yourself into a complicated and unwieldy deductive argument" (Ch 3, Caveat 3).
- **A purpose-statement instead of a story.** "The purpose of this memorandum is to pull together some ideas onâ€¦" followed by a numbered list of topics â€” comprehension drops versus the same content forced into narrative mold (Ch 2 example).
- **Putting new information in the introduction.** Including something the reader doesn't know "will cause you to distort his Question" (Ch 3, Caveat 5).
- **Two questions, one document.** Splits the reader's attention; relate them or split the doc.
- **Headings like "Findings"/"Issues."** No scanning value; an "Issues" section is almost never needed â€” issues derive from the analysis (see `minto-problem-definition-structuring.md`).
