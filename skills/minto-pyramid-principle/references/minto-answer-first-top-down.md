## The framework

> "Controlling the sequence in which you present your ideas is the single most important act necessary to clear writing. The clearest sequence is always to give the summarizing idea before you give the individual ideas being summarized. I cannot emphasize this point too much." (Ch 2, Ordering from the Top Down)

You build a pyramid from the bottom up (grouping sentences into paragraphs, paragraphs into sections), but you must **deliver** it from the top down â€” governing thought first, then the support. The two directions are not contradictory: "the clearest written documents will be those that consistently present their information from the top down, in a pyramidal structure, even though the original thinking will have been done from the bottom up" (Ch 2, Thinking from the Bottom Up).

### Why top-down, not bottom-up, delivery

The reader can only take sentences "one at a time," and "he will assume that those ideas that appear together logically belong together" (Ch 2). If you give the ideas before the summary, he is forced to *guess* the relationship â€” and "people being as diverse in background and understanding as they are, they rarely put exactly the same interpretation on your groupings as you do." Minto's beard/moustache-in-Zurich demonstration shows a reader inventing three different wrong theories before the writer finally states the point. Give the framework first "to make sure he knows what to look for. Otherwise he is likely either to see an unintended relationship, or worse, none at all."

### Ordering to match how the mind receives ideas

A reader has "only a limited amount of mental energy." Some is spent recognizing words, some seeing relationships between ideas, and "whatever is left comprehending their significance" (Ch 2). Bottom-up delivery burns that energy on reconstruction: "to sequence them so that the mind has to go backward and forward to make connections is simply bad manners, and most readers react by refusing to do so." Top-down delivery spends the energy where it matters. The summarizing idea, stated first, tells the mind "what to think about the ideas, and thus prepares his mind to receive them more easily."

### The "magic number 7" / chunking rationale

There is a hard ceiling on what the mind can hold at once. Minto cites George A. Miller's "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two": "the mind cannot hold more than about seven items in its short-term memory at any one time" (Ch 2, The Magical Number Seven). "A convenient number is three, but of course the easiest number is one."

The grocery-list demonstration is the mechanism in miniature: nine unstructured items asked for on the way out the door â€” most people remember two. The same nine, *categorized into the sections of the supermarket*, become recallable, because the mind now holds three categories instead of nine items. "Instead of remembering each of the nine items, you remember only the three categories into which they fall. You are thinking one level of abstraction higher, but because the thought is at a higher level, it suggests the items below it" (Ch 2, The Need to State the Logic).

This is the entire engine of the pyramid: chunk upward into summaries so the reader carries a handful of high-level ideas, each of which *unpacks* into its supporting detail on demand. Minto recommends, in practice, **no more than four or five points per grouping** (Ch 6) â€” beyond that, some of the ideas are probably more closely related to each other than to the rest, and forcing the subgrouping reveals an insight you'd otherwise miss.

### Bottom-up thinking is real â€” but private

"You cannot know precisely what you think until you have been forced to symbolize it" (Ch 2). So you *discover* your structure by grouping from the bottom up â€” but that discovery process is for you, not the reader. The reader gets the finished, top-down product. ## How to apply

1. **State the answer before the support, at every level.** The apex before the key line; each key-line point before its sub-points. If you catch yourself building toward a conclusion, move the conclusion up front.
2. **Lead with the summarizing idea, never the category.** "He did it because they are the same kind of people" beats "He did it for three reasons" â€” the first has content, the second is dead air (Ch 7; see `minto-mece-grouping.md` on intellectually-blank assertions).
3. **Chunk to three-to-five per group.** If a grouping exceeds about five items, look for subgroupings; the mind can't hold more, and the subgrouping usually surfaces a sharper point.
4. **Think bottom-up privately, present top-down publicly.** Use a worksheet or list to *find* the structure; deliver only the finished pyramid.
5. **Resist the deductive instinct on the top line.** You solved the problem deductively (here's what's wrong â†’ here's the cause â†’ here's what to do), but don't force the reader to re-walk it. Present the recommendation first; reserve the "why" for below or for the rare case where the reader needs it first. See `minto-deductive-vs-inductive.md`.

## Common failure modes

- **Burying the lede.** Background, method, and reasoning first; the actual recommendation on page 6. The reader has spent all his energy before reaching the point.
- **Naming the category instead of giving the answer.** "There are three issues" / "We have five problems" â€” intellectually blank; the reader still doesn't know what you think.
- **Overstuffed groupings.** Seven, eight, ten parallel bullets. Past the magic number, comprehension collapses; chunk them.
- **Presenting your problem-solving order as your communication order.** Re-enacting your whole analysis "as if you're saying, 'I worked extremely hard to get this answer, and I'm going to make sure you know it'" (Ch 5). The reader cares about Monday morning, not your journey.
- **"Get it all down first, structure later."** "The minute you express an idea in writing, it tends to take on the most extraordinary beauty... you'll love it once you see it typed, no matter how disjointed the thinking really is" (Ch 3, Caveats). Structure first.
