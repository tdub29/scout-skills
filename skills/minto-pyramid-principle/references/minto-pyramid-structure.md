# Minto â€” The Pyramid Structure (the core principle)

## The framework

> "The ideal structure of the ideas will always be a pyramid of groups of ideas tied together by a single overall thought. Within that pyramidal structure, the ideas will relate vertically â€” in that a point at any level will always be a summary of the ideas grouped below; and horizontally â€” in that the ideas will have been grouped together because together they present a logical argument." (Introduction to Part One)

Clear writing is not a matter of better sentences. It is a matter of *order*: "If a person's writing is unclear, it is most likely because the ordering of the ideas conflicts with the capability of a reader's mind to process them" (Intro, Part One). The single form that fits the mind is a pyramid â€” every document supports **one** governing thought at the apex, with all other ideas arrayed beneath it in summarizing layers.

### Why the mind demands a pyramid

The mind automatically imposes order on whatever it receives â€” "it tends to see any sequence of things that occur together as belonging together" (Ch 2, Sorting into Pyramids). Given a stream of ideas it will *group and summarize* them whether or not the writer helps. So the writer's job is to do that grouping in advance and hand the reader the result presorted. "Any grouping of ideas is easier to comprehend if it arrives presorted into its pyramid" (Ch 1).

### The two relationships inside the pyramid

A pyramid has exactly two kinds of internal relationship (Ch 2, The Substructures Within the Pyramid):

1. **Vertical â€” the question/answer dialogue.** Each box holds an *idea*, which Minto defines as "a statement that raises a question in the reader's mind because you are telling him something he does not know" (Ch 2). Stating a point raises a question (*Why? How? Why do you say that?*); the level immediately below answers it. You "continue the question/answer dialogue until you have communicated all the ideas." This is what holds attention: the reader is "forced to respond logically to your ideas."

2. **Horizontal â€” the logical grouping.** The set of ideas answering a given question must hang together as either an inductive grouping or a deductive argument (one or the other, never both at once) â€” covered in `minto-deductive-vs-inductive.md`. They sit "sideways" because together they form one argument.

A third element â€” the **introductory flow** (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer) â€” is not part of the pyramid proper; it is the narrative that establishes the *one question* the pyramid answers. See `minto-scqa-introduction.md`.

### The three pyramid rules

Whatever the content, a valid pyramid obeys three rules (Ch 1, page 9â€“10):

1. **Ideas at any level must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them.** Abstracting upward to a summary *is* the act of thinking; the point of a paragraph summarizes its sentences, the section summarizes its paragraphs, the document summarizes its sections.
2. **Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea** â€” describable by one plural noun (reasons, steps, problems, changes). If the first is a reason, the rest must be reasons.
3. **Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered** â€” there is a specific reason the second idea comes second. The four possible orders: deductive (premise/premise/conclusion), chronological, structural, comparative (degree). See `minto-mece-grouping.md`.

Rules 2 and 3 make rule 1 *possible*: you can only draw a clean summary out of a properly formed grouping.

### Why the reader needs the answer first

The vertical relationship runs *downward*: a point at the top raises a question that the level below answers. That logic only works if the governing thought is delivered first â€” otherwise the reader has no question to attach the supporting ideas to, "scrabbling about trying to find a relationship, coming to the conclusion that there is none, and giving up in disgust" (Ch 2). This is why the pyramid is delivered top-down even though it was built bottom-up. See `minto-answer-first-top-down.md`.

## How to apply

1. **Find the single governing thought.** Ask: what is the *one* point this document makes? If you can't state it in one sentence, the pyramid has no apex â€” keep abstracting upward from your material until you can.
2. **Trace the vertical dialogue.** Under the apex, ask the question the reader will now have (*Why? How?*). The answers become your **key line** â€” the second level of the pyramid. Each key-line point raises its own question, answered on the line below, until the reader has no more logical questions.
3. **Check each grouping against the three rules.** Is the parent a true *summary* of its children (rule 1)? Are the children the *same kind of idea*, labelable by one plural noun (rule 2)? Are they in a *defensible order* (rule 3)?
4. **Confirm horizontal logic.** Each grouping must be one clean inductive grouping or one deductive argument â€” not a mix. Route to `minto-deductive-vs-inductive.md`.
5. **Slot it before you write.** "The key to clear writing is to slot your ideas into this pyramidal form and test them against the rules before you begin to write" (Ch 1). A broken rule signals a flaw in the thinking â€” fix the structure, not the prose.

## Common failure modes

- **No single thought at the top.** A pile of points with no apex that summarizes them. The reader can't tell what you're claiming.
- **"Findings" / "Conclusions" headings.** Category labels with "no scanning value" (Ch 3) â€” they name the *kind* of idea instead of stating the idea. Replace with the actual point.
- **Answering before raising.** A section ("Our Assumptions") that answers a question the reader hasn't been led to ask yet; the information has to be re-read at the point where it's relevant (Ch 2). Refrain from raising questions before you're ready to answer them, and from answering before you've raised them.
- **Mixing the two relationships.** Treating a horizontal grouping as if it were a vertical chain (or vice versa), so the summary doesn't actually summarize.
- **Fixing prose instead of structure.** The original and rewritten TTW memos "differ very little in the way the language is worded. The clarity of the second document comes from the pyramidal ordering of the ideas" (Ch 3). Reorder first.
