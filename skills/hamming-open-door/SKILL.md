# Hamming: The Open Door

## When to use

The moment: someone is about to optimize a schedule, a piece of finished work, or a body of know-how purely for personal output or personal control, without asking what that optimization costs them in relevance or in credit. The tell is a decision that reads as purely internal (how do I get more done, how do I keep control of this) when it is actually a decision about exposure to the outside world.

Cues that this is the right skill:

- Deciding whether to close the door (block the calendar, mute notifications, go heads-down) or stay reachable to colleagues, even though staying reachable will visibly cost output.

- Noticing a string of finished projects that were technically excellent but aimed at problems nobody upstream cared about by the time they shipped.

- Just finished a good piece of work and feeling the instinct to keep it under tight personal control rather than let others pick it up and run with it.

- Feeling that being "the only one who can do X" is safe, without noticing that this safety is exactly what is blocking a promotion or a move to more important work.

- Wanting a recurring habit that keeps you pointed at what is actually happening in your org or field, not just what is on your own roadmap.

- Weighing whether to tell people about an idea early, while it is still rough, or sit on it until it is fully formed and defensible.

- Asking "am I closed off in a way that is quietly making me less useful," instead of the safer, self-flattering question "am I disciplined."

- Watching a colleague get credit for extending your work and feeling that as a loss rather than as the system working correctly.

Don't trigger for:

- Pure deep-work capacity questions: how to protect blocks of concentrated time to build a hard skill or ship a specific artifact, with no visibility tradeoff in view. That is newport-so-good territory (career capital, deliberate practice); it optimizes for skill acquisition, not for staying pointed at the right problem.

- Generic time-management or calendar hygiene ("how do I stop overbooking myself," "how do I batch my meetings so I get more done"). Hamming's point here is not efficiency, it is what accessibility buys you that isolation structurally cannot.

- Straightforward problem-selection questions with no access or sharing component. If the only question is "which problem is worth attacking," that is hamming-important-problems, not this skill.

- Generic networking or relationship-building advice ("how do I build my professional network," "how do I make more contacts"). This skill is about a specific accessibility-for-relevance trade and a specific share-versus-hoard decision on real work, not about accumulating contacts as an end in itself.

## The framework

**Core thesis.** What looks like a good working condition, maximum personal output, tight personal control of your results, is often not good for you. Hamming's own words:

> "Thus what you consider to be good working conditions may not be good for you! ... working with one's door closed lets you get more work done per year than if you had an open door, but I have observed repeatedly later those with the closed doors, while working just as hard as others, seem to work on slightly the wrong problems, while those who have let their door stay open get less work done but tend to work on the right problems!"

He could not prove cause and effect, only the correlation. Hamming names an institutional-scale version of the same failure: he considered the Institute for Advanced Study, with its total protection from interruption, to have "ruined more great scientists than any other place has created," because most who arrived there kept polishing the same problems that had made them famous long after those problems stopped mattering. Only a few, like von Neumann, kept enough contact with the outside to stay current. The same logic extends past the literal office door to how you treat a finished piece of work: hoard it to look indispensable and you block your own advancement, because you become the only one who can do what you already did.

Two signatures to recognize before you even open the framework's five sub-parts:

- **Closed-door signature.** High personal output, rare meetings, few interruptions, projects that are individually excellent but that keep missing what the team or field actually needed by the time they land.

- **Open-door signature.** Lower personal output, frequent informal contact, work that stays aimed at what currently matters, and a habit of letting other people finish, extend, or take credit for pieces of the result.

Neither signature is the goal. The goal is choosing which one you need right now, deliberately, and being able to say why.

### 1. The open-door/closed-door tradeoff

Treat "how much do I close off" as a live, continuous decision, not a default you fell into. Closing the door is not free even when it raises your weekly output, because it removes the ambient signal about what is actually changing around you. Opening the door is not free either, it costs you hours and focus. Neither setting is virtuous by itself. The question is which one you need, given how stale your current sense of "the right problem" might already be.

In practice this shows up as a visible pattern before it shows up as a stated belief: someone whose output is high and whose meetings are rare, but whose projects keep missing what the team or the market actually needed next.

### 2. Open door leads to open mind, open mind leads to open door

> "I suspect the open mind leads to the open door, and the open door tends to lead to the open mind; they reinforce each other."

These are not independent variables you can tune separately. Interruptions, hallway conversations, and being reachable are how new information about priorities, half-formed clues, and shifting needs actually reaches you. An open mind that never has an open door starves for lack of input. An open door with a closed mind is just noise. Treat accessibility as an input to your judgment about what to work on, not merely a cost line charged against your output.

Because the two reinforce each other, you cannot fix a stale open mind by thinking harder in isolation. The fix runs through the door: increase actual exposure first, and let the mind catch up.

### 3. Do the work so others can build on top of it

> "Again, you should do your job in such a fashion others can build on top of it."

Finishing something is not the same as finishing it in a form that stops with you. Before calling a piece of work done, ask what shape would let a colleague pick it up, extend it, or adapt it without needing you personally in the loop. This is a design decision made at delivery time, not a nice-to-have added later if someone happens to ask.

In practice this means writing the thing down, naming the interface, or walking one other person through it, before you move on, not "whenever someone asks."

### 4. Tell your ideas freely; credit accrues through adoption, not secrecy

> "If you are to get recognition then others must use your results, adopt, adapt, extend, and elaborate them, and in the process give you credit for it. I have long held the attitude of telling every one freely of my ideas, and in my long career I have had only one important idea 'stolen' by another person. I have found people are remarkably honest if you are in your turn."

Recognition requires that other people actually use your results: adopt them, adapt them, extend them, elaborate on them, and credit you as they go. Sitting on an idea until it is airtight, or telling only a trusted few, starves it of the one mechanism that turns a result into a reputation. Hamming's own career-long tally: telling everyone freely cost him exactly one stolen idea, against everything he gained from wide adoption. Go first, and most people are honest back.

In practice this means the default answer to "should I mention this before it's finished" is yes, not "once it's safe."

### 5. Indispensability blocks your own promotion

> "Do not in the process try to make yourself indispensable; if you do then you cannot be promoted because you will be the only one who can do what you are now doing! I have seen a number of times where this clinging to the exclusive rights to the idea has in the long run done much harm to the individual and to the organization."

Making yourself the sole person who can operate a piece of work feels like job security. It is the opposite. If you are the only one who can do what you are currently doing, the organization cannot move you off it no matter how ready you are for something bigger. The fix is structural: build the handoff into the work itself (see point 3 above), not into your intentions.

In practice this means treating "nobody else can run this" as a red flag to fix, not a badge to protect.

## How to apply

Work the steps in order the first time through. After the first pass, steps 2 and 7 become the recurring cycle: check the staleness signal, then re-set the ratio.

1. **Name the current default.** State plainly whether your calendar, inbox, and habits are currently set to closed-door (protected, low-interrupt, high output) or open-door (reachable, high-interrupt, lower output). Most people have drifted into one setting without ever deciding.
   - If you cannot state it in one sentence, that itself is the finding: you have no policy, only a habit.

2. **Check the staleness signal, not the output signal.** Ask when you last got a piece of information from an unplanned conversation, a hallway comment, or an unscheduled interruption that changed what you thought was important. If you cannot remember, that is evidence your door has been closed longer than your judgment about priorities can safely tolerate.

3. **Set a deliberate ratio, not an absolute state.** For a defined stretch (a week, a quarter), decide what fraction of time stays genuinely open, reachable, interruptible, available for the unplanned, versus protected.
   - Write the ratio down as a policy, for example: "reachable most afternoons, protected mornings," or "open door by default, one protected block per week for the single hardest task."
   - A policy you can state in one sentence is one you can actually audit later; a vague intention to "be more available" is not.

4. **Before closing anything out as finished, run the handoff check.** Ask who else could extend this if you disappeared tomorrow, and what is missing that would stop them.
   - Add the missing piece, documentation, a walkthrough, a clean interface, before calling the work done, not after someone asks for it.
   - If nobody else could pick it up even with the missing piece added, that is itself a finding: say so explicitly rather than letting the gap stay invisible.
   - Name the piece out loud (a doc, a diagram, a recorded walkthrough) rather than leaving "make it handoff-ready" as an abstract goal.

5. **Actively give the idea away before it feels ready.** Tell colleagues or the relevant group about a half-formed idea or an early result rather than waiting for a polished version. The cost is a small risk that someone else runs with it first. The much larger risk is the idea going nowhere because nobody but you ever heard of it.
   - Name the specific person or group who should hear about it this week, not "eventually, once it's solid."

6. **Audit your own indispensability.** List anything you currently do that only you can do. For each item, ask whether that exclusivity protects something genuinely valuable (safety, quality) or just protects your position. If it is the latter, build the bridge that lets someone else do it too.
   - Pick the top item on the list and name one concrete next action that reduces the exclusivity, not just a vague intention to "document more."

7. **Revisit the ratio on a fixed cadence.** Treat the open-door setting as something to re-decide periodically, not something set once. Hamming used a standing Friday-afternoon block to step back and ask the larger questions; conditions change, and a ratio that was right last quarter may now be starving you of signal or starving your output.
   - Put the actual date of the next review on the calendar before moving on; a cadence with no next date is not a cadence.

**Final sanity check.** If the plan you produced only maximizes either output or control, and never trades either one away, you have not actually applied this framework. A correct output states two things plainly: the visibility/access policy (the ratio from step 3, and what changes about it), and the share-and-hand-off decision on the specific piece of work in front of you (what gets given away, to whom, and what was added so they can actually use it). If either half is missing, the application is incomplete.

A minimal completed output looks like this:

- **Access policy:** "Open door Tuesday through Thursday afternoons for the team; Monday and Friday mornings protected for the one hard problem on my list."
- **Hand-off decision:** "The pipeline script goes to the team wiki this week, with a five-minute walkthrough for whoever inherits it, so I stop being the only person who can rerun it."

## Common failure modes

Watch for these patterns; each one takes half of Hamming's trade and uses it to excuse skipping the other half.

- **Rationalizing calendar clutter as the open door.** Filling a schedule with meetings that carry no signal value and calling it "staying accessible." The point of the open door is exposure to information that changes your sense of what matters, not attendance for its own sake. A packed calendar with no new information reaching you is not this framework, it is just busyness.

- **Using "protect deep work" to justify isolation while ignoring the relevance cost.** Closed-door time is legitimate, but Hamming names its price explicitly: drift toward the slightly wrong problem. Choosing to close the door without ever checking whether you have drifted is the failure, not the closing itself.

- **Hoarding a finished result to look indispensable.** Keeping sole command of a system, a dataset, or a piece of institutional knowledge because it feels like leverage. It is leverage against your own promotion, not for it, in Hamming's own repeated observation.

- **Sharing ideas too late to matter.** Waiting for full polish before telling anyone defeats the purpose. By the time it is polished, the window for someone else to build on it, or for you to get credit for originating it, may have closed. Share the rough version with the right people; do not save it for a finished unveiling to everyone.

- **Treating this as a fixed personality trait instead of a revisitable policy.** "I'm just an introvert, closed-door is who I am" or "I'm generous, I already share everything" both skip the actual decision. This framework asks for a specific, revisitable ratio and a specific handoff check on specific pieces of work, not a self-description.

- **Confusing wide sharing with sloppy work.** Telling people your ideas freely does not mean shipping unreviewed or unverified work. The generosity is about access and credit-sharing, not about lowering your own bar for what you put in front of others.
