
# Sun Tzu: Five Constant Factors & Seven Comparisons

## When to use

Trigger when the decision is **whether conditions favor engagement** â€” before resources are committed, not after battle is joined. Concrete cues:

- Entering a new market, category, or geography and needing a structured "who starts ahead?" assessment
- Comparing your organization to a competitor, incumbent, or coalition partner on fundamentals â€” not feature lists
- Board or leadership asks "do we have the alignment and discipline to win this?"
- Pre-deal diligence where *organizational* readiness matters as much as financials
- A reorg or leadership change prompts "did we just improve or weaken our competitive posture?"
- Two sides preparing to negotiate from strength â€” who actually holds the high ground?
- Post-mortem on a lost deal: "we had better product but still lost" â€” often a factors problem, not a product problem
- User mentions Sun Tzu Chapter 1, "laying plans," "five constant factors," or "seven considerations"

Don't trigger for: tactical playbooks mid-engagement (use `sun-tzu-terrain-nine-situations`), intelligence gaps about a specific rival (use `sun-tzu-know-enemy-self`), generic SWOT without Sun Tzu structure (use `competitor-teardown`), launch timing and channel plans (use `launch`), abstract mental models (use `model-thinking`), or any request involving real-world violence or military operations.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Sun Tzu opens *The Art of War* by insisting that war â€” read here as **any high-stakes competitive commitment** â€” is governed by five constant factors that must enter every deliberation. He who knows them wins; he who ignores them fails. The factors are not a checklist of nice-to-haves; they are the structural conditions under which strategy becomes possible or impossible.

The seven comparisons operationalize the audit: side-by-side scoring on the factors and derived conditions (leadership ability, discipline, training, reward/punishment consistency). Sun Tzu's claim is blunt â€” by these seven, victory or defeat can be **forecast** before engagement. The skill translates ancient military categories into organizational and market vocabulary while preserving Sun Tzu's logic: alignment, timing, ground, leadership, and systems determine outcomes more reliably than optimism or brute spend.

Giles's translation uses "Moral Law" for the Tao of cohesion â€” the principle that people follow a leader regardless of personal danger because the mission and governance are credible. In business: do employees, partners, and customers move with you when the move is hard?

### The five constant factors (translated for competitive strategy)

**1. Moral Law â€” alignment and cohesion**

The organization (and its extended ecosystem) is in complete accord with leadership on the mission. People follow through difficulty because they believe the cause and the leader are legitimate. In competitive terms: internal alignment, partner loyalty, customer trust, and cultural coherence that survives stress.

Diagnostic questions:
- Would key teams execute a painful pivot without passive sabotage?
- Do partners and suppliers treat your commitments as credible under pressure?
- Is there a shared narrative that survives bad quarters?

**2. Heaven â€” timing and external conditions**

Night and day, cold and heat, seasons â€” the forces outside direct control that favor or punish action. In business: macro cycle, regulatory window, technology S-curve, seasonality, interest rates, category maturity, public sentiment.

Diagnostic questions:
- Is the external clock working for or against this move *right now*?
- Are we attributing skill to a tailwind, or blaming execution for a headwind?
- Can we wait one season and improve Heaven without losing Earth?

**3. Earth â€” market structure, distance, and logistics**

Distances, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes, the chances of life and death. In business: market access, distribution topology, capital intensity, geographic friction, supply-chain vulnerability, switching costs, channel control, regulatory moats.

Diagnostic questions:
- Who holds the "narrow passes" (platforms, standards, licenses)?
- Is our supply line â€” talent pipeline, cash runway, data access â€” secure?
- Does geography or structure make our advantage real or illusory?

**4. The Commander â€” leadership virtues**

Wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage, strictness. Sun Tzu's commander is not charismatic alone â€” they combine judgment, honesty, care for the force, moral courage, and enforceable standards.

Diagnostic questions:
- Does leadership see the whole field or only their function?
- Are hard truths surfaced, or punished?
- Will the leader enforce discipline on high performers who break process?

**5. Method and discipline â€” organization and systems**

Marshaling the army in proper subdivisions, rank clarity, maintained roads for supplies, controlled expenditure. In business: org design, decision rights, operating cadence, budget discipline, KPI integrity, onboarding and training systems.

Diagnostic questions:
- Can the org deploy resources to the right place at the right time?
- Are roles and escalation paths clear under stress?
- Is spend controlled, or does every initiative become a new silo?

Sun Tzu's summary: these five heads must be familiar to every general; knowing them brings victory, ignoring them brings failure.

### The seven comparisons

When determining conditions in the field, compare both sides:

1. **Which sovereign/executive is imbued with Moral Law?** â€” Who has stronger stakeholder alignment and mission legitimacy?
2. **Which general/leader has most ability?** â€” Who has the better decision-maker in this domain?
3. **With whom lie advantages from Heaven and Earth?** â€” Who owns timing plus structural/market position?
4. **On which side is discipline most rigorously enforced?** â€” Where do rules actually bind behavior?
5. **Which army/organization is stronger?** â€” Morally (esprit) and materially (scale, resources).
6. **On which side are officers and men more highly trained?** â€” Capability, practice, readiness for this *specific* contest.
7. **In which army is there greater constancy in reward and punishment?** â€” Merit recognized, failure addressed â€” predictably.

Sun Tzu: by these seven, forecast victory or defeat. The general who heeds counsel and acts on it conquers; who ignores it suffers defeat.

### Circumstances beyond the rules

Sun Tzu warns against "bookish theoretic" rigidity. While heading the profit of counsel, avail yourself of helpful circumstances beyond ordinary rules. As circumstances are favorable, modify plans. Deception (ss. 18â€“25) belongs to execution, not this audit â€” but the audit must leave room for opportunistic adjustment when the map says "slight edge" not "clear win."

All competitive interaction is based on managing information asymmetry â€” appearing weak when strong, etc. â€” but **this skill stops at the audit**; it does not coach deception tactics for harm.

### Temple calculations â€” many calculations lead to victory

Sun Tzu closes Chapter I with the temple metaphor: the general who wins makes many calculations in the temple before battle; who loses makes few. "Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat: how much more no calculation at all!"

In business: the five-factor audit *is* the calculation. Skipping it is not boldness â€” it is the path Sun Tzu associates with defeat. The audit should be repeated when Heaven shifts (macro turn), Commander changes (new CEO), or Earth moves (platform policy, regulation).

### Scoring rubric (1â€“5 per factor)

Use consistently across engagements so audits compare over time:

| Score | Meaning | Evidence bar |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Critical weakness â€” likely decisive against you | Documented failures, attrition, lost deals traceable to this factor |
| 2 | Material disadvantage | Multiple negative signals; no compensating strength |
| 3 | Parity â€” neither side owns this factor | Mixed evidence or symmetric constraints |
| 4 | Material advantage | Repeatable wins attributable to this factor |
| 5 | Decisive advantage â€” difficult for rival to close in contest horizon | Structural moat, multi-year lead, crisis-tested cohesion |

A side with two scores of 1 cannot compensate with three scores of 5 on unrelated factors â€” Sun Tzu treats the system as integrated.

### Deception principles (reference â€” execution layer)

Chapter I ss. 18â€“25 lists tactical deception: seem unable when able, inactive when using force, near when far, etc. These belong to **execution after the audit**, coordinated with `sun-tzu-know-enemy-self`. The audit asks honestly about factors; deception applies when engaging, not when scoring Moral Law (deceiving yourself destroys the audit).

Business-ethical bounds: competitive signaling, negotiation posture, and launch timing are in bounds; fraud, false claims, and counterfeit products are out of bounds.

### Worked example (abbreviated)

**Engagement:** Mid-size B2B SaaS (Side A) entering vertical already served by entrenched incumbent (Side B).

| Factor | Side A | Side B | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moral Law | 3 | 4 | Incumbent customers trust brand; A's team aligned but unproven in vertical |
| Heaven | 4 | 2 | New regulation favors A's architecture; incumbent's legacy stack penalized |
| Earth | 2 | 5 | Incumbent owns channel partnerships â€” narrow passes |
| Commander | 4 | 3 | A's GM is domain expert; B's VP is generic enterprise |
| Method | 3 | 4 | B has mature CS and sales ops; A faster but scrappier |

Seven comparisons: A wins Heaven, Commander; B wins Moral Law, Earth, strength, training, constancy â€” **forecast B advantage** unless A balks B's plans (regulatory wedge) rather than sieging accounts. Limiting factor for A: Earth (channels). Recommendation: alliance before direct enterprise assault.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Name the engagement.** Define the competitive move (market entry, pricing war, partnership bid, regulatory fight, talent war, M&A defense). Name Side A (usually the user's org) and Side B (primary rival or counterparty). If multiple rivals, pick the decisive one for the seven comparisons; note others in appendix.

2. **Score the five factors for each side.** Use a 1â€“5 scale per factor: 1 = critical weakness, 3 = parity, 5 = decisive advantage. Require evidence per score â€” no default 3s. One paragraph of justification per factor per side.

3. **Run the seven comparisons.** For each comparison, declare Advantage A, Advantage B, or Parity. Tie each verdict to specific evidence from step 2, not generic industry wisdom.

4. **Forecast overall advantage.** Count comparison wins. Sun Tzu's logic: lopsided comparison wins imply forecast victory; lopsided losses imply forecast defeat; narrow splits imply contingent outcome â€” engagement only with a plan to shift factors.

5. **Identify the limiting factor for Side A.** Which single factor, if improved one notch, most shifts the seven comparisons? This is the highest-leverage pre-engagement investment.

6. **Assess Heaven sensitivity.** Run a brief scenario: if external conditions flip (recession, regulation, platform policy change), which scores move? Flag engagements that are only viable in one weather pattern.

7. **Counsel retain or dismiss.** Sun Tzu's harsh HR lesson: the leader who will not act on the audit should not retain command of the initiative. Translate to: if leadership won't fix the limiting factor before committing resources, recommend delay or disengage.

8. **Note circumstantial modifiers.** List 2â€“3 favorable circumstances to exploit and 2â€“3 unfavorable ones to hedge â€” without rewriting the audit into a tactical plan.

9. **Manipulation-risk check.** Flag if the user is using the audit to rationalize a decision already made ("we have to launch anyway"). If Moral Law scores are low but the user wants to proceed, say so explicitly â€” Sun Tzu treats alignment failure as structural, not cosmetic.

10. **Deliver the output artifact.** Produce the tables below filled in â€” not prose alone.

## Common failure modes

- **Scoring product features instead of factors.** "We have better API" is not Method and discipline unless it reflects org capability. Re-score using organizational and structural evidence.

- **Treating Moral Law as "culture deck."** Posters and values statements are not cohesion. Ask what happens when layoffs, price increases, or partner betrayals stress the system.

- **Ignoring Heaven because "we can't control macro."** You cannot control weather; you can choose not to sail into a storm. Timing denial is how organizations waste quarters.

- **Commander worship.** One brilliant CEO does not fix broken Method. Sun Tzu lists commander as one of five â€” not five of five.

- **False discipline scores.** Startups often score 5 on agility but 1 on discipline; incumbents the reverse. Name which kind of discipline the *contest* requires.

- **Skipping the seventh comparison.** Inconsistent rewards and punishments destroy esprit faster than weak training. Include recent high-profile promotions and firings as evidence.

- **Using the audit to justify violence or unethical conduct.** Sun Tzu in business context does not license harm, fraud, or illegal competitive behavior. Deception in source text means information advantage in negotiation and market signaling â€” not fraud.

- **Collapsing into generic SWOT.** The five factors are not Strengths/Weaknesses re-labeled. Keep Sun Tzu's categories strict so the audit stays comparable across engagements.

- **Audit once, never revisit.** Sun Tzu's factors change â€” new CEO, new funding environment, lost anchor customer. Re-run when Commander or Heaven shifts materially.

- **Single-side audit.** The seven comparisons require Side B. If unknown, name hypotheses explicitly and mark comparisons as low confidence â€” then route to `sun-tzu-know-enemy-self` for intelligence plan.

- **NGO/nonprofit distortion.** Moral Law is not "mission purity" alone â€” coalition cohesion, donor alignment, and field staff trust still score here; Earth includes logistics of distribution and regulatory access to beneficiaries.

- **Conflating with `launch`.** Launch calendars, channel mix, and week-by-week GTM are out of scope. This skill answers "are we structurally positioned to win?" not "what's the launch tweet schedule?"

- **Conflating with `model-thinking`.** Mental models (inversion, second-order effects) are generic. Five factors are Sun Tzu-specific and produce comparable scorecards â€” use this skill when the user wants Sun Tzu or structured competitive positioning, not a brainstorming lattice.
