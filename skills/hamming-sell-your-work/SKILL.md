# Hamming: You Must Sell Your Work

## When to use

Reach for this skill at the moment a piece of work is finished, or nearly finished, and attention turns (or should turn) to what happens next: who hears about it, who adopts it, whether it survives contact with an organization that automatically resists new things. This is not a document-formatting skill and not a prose-quality skill. It is the higher-altitude claim that presenting the work at all, deliberately and as a practiced discipline, is required alongside doing the work, on the same footing as the technical effort itself, not a bonus activity for people who happen to enjoy it.

Trigger on:

- The user built or finished something good, and nobody is picking it up, using it, or citing it.
  For example: a tool, model, process, or paper sits unused months after completion while the user assumes quality alone should have carried it.
- The user says or implies "the work should speak for itself" or "if it is good, people will notice."
  This is the exact belief Hamming calls out by name as wrong, and the skill exists to correct it directly.
- The user has a result, paper, tool, or process ready but has made no plan for a talk, report, or informal pitch about it.
  The technical work is done; the distribution work has not even been scoped, let alone started.
- The user is puzzled that an idea they had earlier shows up later, credited to someone else, or gets "rediscovered."
  This is Hamming's specific failure case: not being beaten to the idea, but being beaten to the presentation of it.
- The user is trying to get buy-in, adoption, or funding for something already built, not asking how to build it.
  The technical question is closed; the open question is entirely about reaching an audience.
- The user is preparing for a talk, demo, report, or hallway conversation and wants to know how seriously to prepare for it.
  The answer this skill gives is: as seriously as the work itself, because presentation is not a lesser task bolted onto the real one.
- The user is thinking about their own visibility or reputation inside an organization, not just the work's visibility.
  Selling the work and selling yourself run on the same track and fail for the same reasons when skipped.
- The user asks something like "why doesn't anyone know about this" or "how do I get this adopted."
  Treat this as a request for a presentation plan, not a request for a better version of the work.

Don't trigger for:

- Structuring a specific document, memo, or deck once the decision to write it is already made. That is minto-pyramid-principle's job: pyramid structure, SCQA, MECE groupings, ordering the argument inside the report this skill already decided was needed.
  If the user already knows they are writing a report and just needs it organized, hand off; only stay here if "should I even write one, and for whom" is still open.
- Improving sentence-level wording, clarity, rhythm, or voice. That is prose-editing or technical-writing, and it operates one level below the question this skill answers.
  Wordsmithing a paragraph that nobody has decided to circulate yet is solving the wrong problem first.
- Deciding what order to prove or demonstrate a new method or technique in, or how to sequence the first hard test of it. That is hamming-heroic-task-first; it governs proof, this skill governs distribution of a result once it exists.
  A method that has not yet been proven has nothing to present; this skill applies once there is a result worth carrying to an audience.
- Choosing which problem to work on in the first place, or building the standing list of important, attackable problems. That is hamming-important-problems; this skill starts downstream of that choice, after a result already exists and needs an audience.
  Confusing the two produces a plan for presenting work that has not been done yet, which is not what this skill is for.

## The framework

Hamming's claim has an internal shape: a thesis about why presentation is mandatory, a named set of forms to master, a method for building skill in those forms, a line that keeps the discipline honest, and an extension of the same logic from the work to the person doing it.

### Core thesis: good work is necessary, not sufficient

Hamming states this bluntly: "many scientists and others think good ideas will win out automatically and need not be carefully presented. They are wrong; many a good idea has had to be rediscovered because it was not well presented the first time, years before!" New ideas face automatic institutional resistance, and to some extent justly, since an organization cannot be in continual ferment and change. "Change does not mean progress, but progress requires change," and an organization only responds to change that has been made legible to it through some form of presentation. That resistance does not dissolve because the idea happens to be correct; correctness is not the currency an organization trades in, legibility is.

Getting a result adopted is therefore a separate, masterable skill from producing the result in the first place, and neglecting it is a recurring, correctable failure of discipline, not bad luck or an unfair market. Treat "sell the work" as a required deliverable on the same footing as the work itself, not an afterthought contingent on spare time, temperament, or whether the user privately considers themselves a "presentation person." Nobody is exempted by talent in the underlying work; the presentation still has to happen, on purpose, every time.

Hamming places this claim in the closing chapter of the book on purpose, after chapters on courage, important problems, and tolerating ambiguity. Having the right problem and the right result is not the end of the story he is telling; a result that never gets presented never completes the arc from private discovery to shared, credited contribution.

### The three master forms

Hamming names exactly three forms to master, and is explicit that "all three are essential":

1. **Formal presentations.** Scheduled talks, lectures, conference sessions, structured demos, anything with an announced audience and a fixed time slot. These reward rehearsal and a deliberate opening, middle, and close, and they are the form most people already recognize as needing preparation.

2. **Written reports.** Documents meant to be read, archived, and referenced after the fact, often by people who were never in the room and never will be. These carry the work forward in time, past the memory of anyone who attended a talk, and are frequently the only surviving record years later.

3. **Informal presentations, as they happen to occur.** Hallway conversations, an aside inside someone else's meeting, an unplanned question, an elevator pitch. This form cannot be scheduled in advance, only prepared for, so it is the one most people skip preparing for entirely, and the one where a good idea is most often lost for want of a ready, clear answer.

The recurring failure is picking a favorite and treating the other two as optional. A researcher comfortable writing but not speaking will under-invest in formal talks and impromptu pitches; a strong public speaker will under-invest in the written report that has to outlive the talk and reach people who were not present for it.

All three channels reach different people at different moments in an organization's decision cycle, and skipping one is exactly how a good idea sits unclaimed until someone else, who covered all three, presents it first and gets the credit for it. Treat competence in all three as a checklist to maintain, not a strength to lean on and two weaknesses to accept.

### Build the skill by privately critiquing every presentation you attend

Hamming's method for developing presentation skill is not a course or a book, it is a standing habit: "adopt the habit of privately critiquing all presentations you attend and also asking the opinions of others. Try to find those parts which you think are effective and which also can be adapted to your style." Concretely, this means:

- Noting, after every talk, report, or pitch (yours or someone else's), one specific technique that worked and one that did not.
- Asking other attendees what they thought, since a technique that landed for the presenter's intent may not have landed for the room.
- Deliberately stealing structural choices, not just content: how someone opened, where they placed the strongest point, how they closed, how they handled a hostile question.
- Including the smaller craft details most people never notice they are making, down to Hamming's own example that a good after-dinner speech needs three well-placed jokes: one at the beginning, one in the middle to re-engage a drifting audience, and the best one at the end so people remember at least one thing that was said.
- Adapting whatever is stolen to the user's own style rather than copying it wholesale; the point is a technique bank, not an impression of someone else's delivery.

The habit is cheap, continuous, and compounding. Every talk attended, every report read, every pitch overheard is raw material, whether it was good or bad, and the technique bank it builds is what gets drawn on later under deadline pressure, when there is no time left to invent an approach from nothing.

This is a lifelong habit in Hamming's account, not a phase to graduate out of once early-career presentations go well. Senior people who stop critiquing what they watch tend to be the ones whose talks and reports quietly stop improving, even as their technical work keeps advancing.

### Selling means force of clear presentation, not propaganda

The discipline is legitimate only because of this distinction, and it is worth holding onto precisely because it is the thing that makes "sell your work" different from spin. Hamming: "you must learn to sell your ideas, not by propaganda, but by force of clear presentation." And later, describing how he handled his own results: "I do not mean give it false importance, nor propagandize for it, nor pretend it is not what it is." Selling, in this framework, means recasting real work into its clearest and most fundamental form, the way Hamming recast a specific numerical-integration method once he realized the result would be scrutinized by skeptical outsiders, so that its actual value and its actual range of application became visible to people who had not done the work themselves.

It is not exaggeration, not manufacturing urgency, not hiding weaknesses, and not pretending a narrow result is a general one. If a claim needs inflating to sound impressive, the correct fix is a clearer, more honest statement of what the work actually does, not stronger adjectives wrapped around the same claim. Clarity is the whole mechanism; anything added on top of clarity to make a weak result look stronger is propaganda, not selling, and Hamming rules it out by name.

This is also the test to hand back to a user who worries that "selling" feels beneath them or dishonest: if the plan under consideration is entirely made of clearer statements of true things, it is selling in Hamming's sense; the moment it requires a claim that would not survive being checked, it has crossed into propaganda and should be cut, not softened.

### Sell yourself: reputation is earned before autonomy is granted

Selling is not limited to the result; Hamming applies the same logic to the person doing the work, using the plumber analogy: "You do not hire a plumber to learn plumbing while trying to fix your trouble, you expect he is already an expert." He is explicit that he did not have the freedom to choose his own problems either, for years, and had to earn it the same way: "I had to establish the reputation on my own time that I could do important work, and only then was I given the time to do it."

The sequence runs in one direction only. Demonstrated, presented competence comes first; autonomy to pick your own problems and set your own schedule comes after, as a consequence of that reputation, not as a starting entitlement. Waiting for autonomy before investing in visible, well-presented work reverses a sequence that Hamming's own career shows does not run in reverse. This is why self-promotion belongs inside the same discipline as presenting results, not off to one side as something separately optional: an unsold researcher and an unsold result fail for the same underlying reason, and fixing one without the other leaves the failure half-repaired.

Hamming pairs this with what he calls "the gentle art of educating your bosses," managing upward so the people who grant autonomy actually understand what has been demonstrated. Selling yourself is not limited to peers and outside audiences; it includes the specific, ongoing work of making your own management legible to the fact that you have earned more latitude, since they will not infer it unprompted any more than an external audience will infer the value of an unpresented result.

## How to apply

These steps assume a result already exists and are meant to run at the point where the user is deciding it is nearly done. Do not wait for the work to be fully polished before starting step 1; the presentation plan should be forming alongside the last stretch of the work, not after it.

1. When finishing, or nearing finished, any piece of work, stop and add "how will this reach people" as a literal line item on the plan, not an afterthought for after the work ships. Name who specifically needs to know, and in what form.
   Example: closing out a project plan with a "presentation" line next to the "testing" and "documentation" lines, not after them.

2. For each intended audience, pick which of the three forms actually fits: a formal presentation for a scheduled or high-stakes audience, a written report for an archival or asynchronous audience, an informal pitch for opportunistic moments that cannot be scheduled. If the work genuinely matters, plan for more than one form. A single channel is a single point of failure, and the people who matter most are rarely all reachable through the same one.

3. Draft the informal, hallway-length version first, even when a formal talk or written report is also coming. State what the work is and why it matters in the length of an unplanned conversation, out loud or on paper. If that statement will not come out clearly and quickly, the work itself is not yet understood clearly enough to present in any of the three forms, and that is worth discovering before the formal version is drafted, not during it.

4. Recast the result into its clearest, most fundamental statement before presenting it in any form. Ask directly: am I clarifying this, or am I dressing it up? If a claim needs qualifying language or hedging to survive scrutiny, fix the underlying claim, not the wording wrapped around it.

5. Before a formal talk or a written report goes out, pull two or three techniques from the standing critique log (an opening hook, a structural choice, where the strongest point was placed, how something closed, how a hard question got handled) and apply at least one of them on purpose. Do not present from a blank template when an already-adapted technique is sitting in the log unused.

6. Separately from any single piece of work, audit personal visibility on a recurring cadence, not just once. Is competence being presented regularly, or is the user waiting to be discovered? If more autonomy over which problems to pursue is wanted, treat presented, demonstrated competence as the price of admission for that autonomy, not a distraction from "real work" that competes with it.

7. After every talk, report, or pitch given, and after every one merely attended, log one thing that worked and one thing that did not. This is a recurring line added to a running log every time, not a one-time retrospective done after a single big talk.

8. Final sanity check before shipping: state, in one sentence each, what the formal-talk version, the written-report version, and the hallway version of this result would say. If any one of the three draws a blank, the work is not actually ready to ship, even though the technical part is finished, because the distribution plan is not.

### Worked example

A user has finished an internal tool that meaningfully speeds up a recurring analysis, and nobody outside their own team knows it exists.

- Step 1 to 2: the user names three audiences (their manager, the adjacent team that runs the same analysis manually, and a company-wide practices channel) and assigns a form to each: an informal pitch to the manager at the next one-on-one, a short written report for the adjacent team to read asynchronously, and a lightning-talk slot at the next practices meeting.
- Step 3 to 4: the hallway version comes out as "this cuts a two-day manual process to twenty minutes, here is the before and after," which is clear enough that it becomes the spine of both the report and the talk, recast rather than rewritten from scratch each time.
- Step 5: the user pulls a technique from their critique log, opening the practices talk with the twenty-minute result before explaining how it works, because a talk they logged three months earlier used that exact ordering to good effect.
- Step 6 to 7: the user treats the practices talk itself as a visibility move, not only a technical one, and logs afterward that leading with the result landed well while a slide of implementation detail lost the room.
- Step 8: before scheduling anything, the user can already state all three one-sentence versions, so the tool ships with its presentation plan intact rather than trailing behind it.

Contrast the failure variant: the same tool ships with no plan, the manager finds out about it by accident weeks later, the adjacent team never hears about it and keeps doing the analysis manually, and six months on someone else demos a similar tool at the practices meeting and gets treated as the person who solved the problem. Nothing about the underlying tool was worse in this version; only the presentation plan was missing.

## Common failure modes

Each of these is a way of quietly opting out of the discipline while still believing the work itself is enough. None of them are about the quality of the underlying work; all of them are about what happens, or fails to happen, after the work is done.

- **Believing good work sells itself.** Waiting for adoption instead of planning presentation as part of finishing the work. This is precisely the mechanism by which a good idea gets "rediscovered" later and credited to whoever presented it well the first time, exactly as Hamming warns.

- **Drifting into document-structure or wording questions.** Asking "how should I organize this report" or "how do I improve this sentence" before ever establishing whether a presentation plan exists at all. Those are real questions, but they belong to minto-pyramid-principle and prose-editing, and they only make sense once this skill's higher-altitude plan (which form, for which audience, saying what) is already decided.

- **Treating selling as propaganda.** Inflating results, hiding weaknesses, or presenting the work as something other than what it is. This is the exact behavior Hamming rules out by name, and it burns the credibility that future presentations depend on; a reputation for overselling makes the next honest claim harder to land, not easier.

- **Mastering only one of the three forms.** Usually the written report, because it feels lower-risk than standing in front of people live or fielding a question with no preparation time. All three are named as essential, not a menu to pick a comfortable one from.

- **Refusing to sell yourself.** Treating self-promotion as separate from, or beneath, professional discipline, then being puzzled that more autonomy is never extended. Reputation has to be established first, on the user's own initiative and often on their own time, before the freedom to choose problems follows from it.

- **Skipping the critique habit.** Sitting through talks or reading reports passively instead of logging what worked and what did not, so no technique bank ever accumulates, and every future presentation is prepared from scratch under time pressure instead of drawing on stored, already-adapted material.
