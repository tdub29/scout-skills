## The believability ladder (the most important rule)

Grandiose affirmations backfire for the people who most need them. In Wood, Perunovic & Lee (2009, *Psychological Science*), participants with **low** self-esteem who repeated "I'm a lovable person" ended up feeling *worse* than those who didn't â€” because the statement collided with their actual self-view and the contradiction got louder. High-self-esteem people got a small lift.

Takeaway: **claim only what the person can say without their gut objecting, then ladder up.** Rungs, lowest resistance to highest:

| Rung | Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Becoming | "I am becoming someone who ___." / "It's getting easier to ___." | The flat claim feels false |
| Choosing | "I am choosing to ___." / "I am learning to ___." | Want agency without overclaiming |
| Evidence-anchored | "I ___, because I already did it when ___." | There's a true past instance |
| Capacity / identity | "I am the kind of person who ___." | Trait shows up sometimes already |
| Flat present | "I am ___." | The user can say it and mostly mean it |

Calibration question: *"Could you say this out loud right now without your gut calling it a lie?"* If no â†’ drop a rung.

## Visualization (the "manifest" beat)

- End on the next concrete action the scene implies. Visualization that points at a real next step beats a vision board that points at nothing.
