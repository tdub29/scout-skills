## The framework

> An early retiree loses employer health coverage but isn't eligible for **Medicare until 65.** That gap â€” potentially decades â€” is one of the largest and most underestimated costs in a FIRE plan. The primary tool is the **ACA (Affordable Care Act) marketplace**, where **premium tax credits (subsidies)** can dramatically cut the cost â€” *if* you manage your income.

The subsidy you get is a function of your **MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income)** relative to the **Federal Poverty Level (FPL)**. The lower your MAGI (down to a floor), the larger the subsidy. This makes **MAGI management** the central healthcare lever for early retirees â€” and it directly conflicts with raising income through Roth conversions and capital-gain realization (see below).

## The 2026 subsidy cliff is back (time-sensitive)

The enhanced premium tax credits created by the American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) and extended by the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) **expired January 1, 2026.** With that expiration, **the 400% Federal Poverty Level subsidy "cliff" has returned.**

- **Under 400% FPL:** you qualify for premium tax credits on a sliding scale (more subsidy the lower your income).
- **At or above 400% FPL:** you get **$0 in subsidy â€” a hard cliff**, regardless of how expensive your premium is. There is no longer the ARPA-era cap that limited premiums to a percentage of income above 400% FPL.

The cliff is brutal because it's a step, not a slope. A dollar of income that pushes you just over 400% FPL can cost you the *entire* subsidy. Concretely: **a 60-year-old couple just over the line (~402% FPL â‰ˆ $85k MAGI) could face roughly $22,600/yr in unsubsidized premiums** â€” while the same couple a few hundred dollars under the line would pay a small fraction of that. Older pre-65 enrollees face the steepest premiums (age-rated), so the cliff hits them hardest.

## The core tension

> **Keeping MAGI under 400% FPL to preserve ACA subsidies** directly conflicts with **doing large Roth conversions or capital-gain harvesting in the same year** â€” because both raise MAGI.

This is the defining trade-off of the early-retirement years. The same low-income window that's ideal for cheap Roth conversions and 0% capital-gains harvesting (see `fire-withdrawal-tax-optimization.md`) is *also* the window where staying under the FPL ceiling preserves five-figure health subsidies. You frequently **cannot do both fully in the same year.** Options:

- **Throttle conversions** to stay under 400% FPL while collecting subsidies (smaller conversions, more years).
- **"Lumpy" years:** in some years go big on conversions/gains and accept no subsidy; in other years keep MAGI low and collect the subsidy. Plan the calendar deliberately.
- **Quantify both sides:** compare the tax saved by converting in a low bracket against the ACA subsidy lost by crossing the cliff that year. Sometimes the subsidy is worth more than the conversion arbitrage; sometimes not.

## Other bridge options

- **COBRA:** continue your former employer's plan for up to 18 months after leaving. Same coverage, but you pay the full premium (no employer share) â€” usually expensive, but useful as a short stopgap right after leaving work, especially mid-year before you can optimize ACA income.
- **HSA:** if you accumulated one (see `fire-accumulation-account-order.md`), it pays qualified medical expenses tax-free at any age â€” a direct way to cover deductibles and out-of-pocket costs in the gap. Note: you can't make new HSA contributions while on a non-HDHP ACA plan, but you can still spend the balance.
- **Spouse's plan / part-time job with benefits:** Barista FIRE (see `fire-number-and-variants.md`) often exists specifically to obtain employer health coverage and sidestep the ACA-MAGI problem entirely.
- **Short-term / catastrophic plans:** generally weaker coverage and not subsidy-eligible; treat as a last resort.

2. **Find the 400% FPL threshold for the household size** in the current year, and set a **MAGI target** comfortably under it if you want subsidies. (Re-check the FPL table every year â€” it moves.)
3. **Budget the MAGI ceiling across all income sources:** Roth conversions, realized capital gains, interest/dividends, and any earned income all count. Reserve the headroom deliberately.
4. **Decide the calendar:** smooth (stay under the cliff every year) vs. lumpy (alternate big-conversion/no-subsidy years with low-MAGI/subsidy years). Pick based on which produces more after-tax, after-premium wealth.
5. **Use COBRA as a short stopgap, HSA for out-of-pocket costs, and consider Barista coverage** if the cliff math is punishing.
6. **Track ACA rule changes year to year.** This area is unusually volatile â€” the cliff returned in 2026, and Congress can change it again. Re-verify the FPL %s, the cliff's existence, and the subsidy formula each enrollment season; advise the user to confirm at HealthCare.gov / their state exchange and with a CPA.

## Common failure modes

- **Assuming the ARPA enhanced subsidies still exist.** They expired Jan 1, 2026 â€” the 400% FPL cliff is back. Planning on the no-cliff regime overstates available subsidies.
- **Doing a big Roth conversion and a subsidy claim in the same year.** The conversion can push MAGI over 400% FPL and vaporize the subsidy â€” a five-figure mistake.
- **Forgetting capital gains count toward MAGI.** Realized gains (including 0% LTCG harvesting) raise MAGI and can breach the cliff just like conversions.
- **Ignoring the cliff's step shape.** Being $1 over 400% FPL costs the entire subsidy; a small income reduction near the line can be worth thousands.
- **Omitting healthcare from the FIRE number.** Pre-65 premiums are a major recurring expense; leaving them out understates the required portfolio.
- **Using last year's FPL table or subsidy rules.** Everything here changes annually â€” re-verify for the current year.
