# Wheeler: Prepare to Adapt (Planning, Not Plans)

## When to use

Trigger when the user is getting ready for a negotiation and wants to prepare in a way that survives contact with a counterpart who has their own agenda. Concrete cues:

- "How do I prepare for this negotiation / salary talk / vendor deal / dispute?"
- "What's my walkaway? My bottom line? My BATNA?"
- "How high should I aim â€” what's a realistic goal vs. a stretch?"
- "Should I even negotiate, or just take the offer? Is now the right time?"
- "I can't predict what they'll do â€” how do I plan around that?"
- "Last time I locked into a plan and it stopped working. How do I avoid that?"
- "What could go wrong here?" (premortem cue)
- The user has a script in mind and is treating it as fixed â€” the warning sign Wheeler writes against.

Don't trigger for: live in-the-moment improvisation once the negotiation is underway (route to `wheeler-improvisation-adaptive`), managing emotions at the table (`wheeler-emotions-momentum`), or non-negotiation interpersonal repair (route to the cc-*, stone-*, rosenberg-* families).

## The framework

### Core thesis: plans are worthless, planning is everything

Wheeler opens Part One with Jay Sheldon, who set out to *buy* a cable company, hit a deadlock on price, and â€” because he was agile, not scripted â€” flipped into being the *seller* and doubled his money (Ch 1). His counterpart, "sitting at the same table, looking at exactly the same facts, didn't imagine being a buyer until Jay proposed it." The lesson: preparation is indispensable, but the prepared mind has to stay loose.

Wheeler's signature framing borrows from Eisenhower: "Plans are worthless... Planning is everything" (Ch 8, "Critical Moments" callback; the principle threads through Part One). It is not a contradiction. A good planning process "sharpens objectives, exposes possible obstacles, and illuminates potential paths, even though the exact route may not be determined until the interaction is well under way." Plans can only be provisional. So you prepare to *adapt* â€” you do the homework precisely so you can improvise intelligently when reality diverges from the blueprint. Ambassador Brahimi's version: "Don't ask reality to conform to your blueprint, but transform your blueprint to adapt to reality" (Ch 1).

This is the opposite of the "cookie-cutter strategy" that crumbles in turbulence (the co-op window-bar lawsuit that ballooned from $902 to $100,000 because both sides clung to an obsolete plan, Ch 1).

### The deal triangle and the goal range (Ch 3, "Prospecting")

Wheeler rejects the one-dimensional "bargaining range" (a single bottom line on a taut line). Instead, map a **deal triangle** â€” the territory of workable outcomes â€” and locate three things on it:

1. **Baseline (your walkaway), defined broadly.** Don't put one stake in the ground. Identify a *set* of equivalent deals â€” better in some respects, worse in others â€” that all sit "on the knife edge between accepting and rejecting." Wheeler's three steps: (a) establish a benchmark deal (anchored to your BATNA â€” the better your fallback, the pickier you can be); (b) identify equivalent packages (the "bag of groceries" trade-off exercise â€” how many cans of soup for one more pint of raspberries?); (c) anticipate likely change. Karen's job offer is the worked example: a $100K benchmark, plus equivalent bundles trading salary for equity/vacation, plus contingencies (a promotion at her current job raises her baseline; two mortgages would lower it). Her baseline ends up "more like a brushstroke on a watercolor than a clear-cut line on a blueprint."

2. **A realistic target / value ladder.** Your baseline is only the floor. Treat intermediate outcomes as rungs on a value ladder so you can tell whether you're climbing or sliding back.

3. **A stretch goal (best-case thinking).** Set "something to reach for so that you don't settle too cheaply" â€” an outstanding result with maybe a 10% chance of materializing. Wheeler calls it "intentional wishful thinking": have high hopes *and* ponder what conditions would have to exist to make them true. Research he cites (Galinsky's promotion-priming) shows negotiators who set lofty goals get better deals and create more value â€” but unrealistic stretch goals can leave you empty-handed (the "maximizer vs. satisficer" tension, after Barry Schwartz). The discipline: think big *during* the deal, then accept what you achieved and move on.

Also estimate the **counterpart's baseline** â€” hard to do (they'll bluff or stay evasive), so look for clues, hold it loosely, and recognize that whoever has the clearer view of the deal space holds the information advantage.

### The prospect matrix (Ch 3): should you negotiate, and how hard?

Combine the *latitude* (probability of agreement: high/low) with the *longitude* (size of the potential gain vs. your fallback). That yields four cells: grade-A "abundance" (high odds, high payoff), grade-B "bonus" (high odds, lower payoff), grade-C "chancy" (long shot, big upside), grade-D "dead end." Each cell calls for a different posture and tells you how hungry to be. Wheeler's warning against the CEO with a 25-year unbroken record of reaching agreement: "If you never miss a flight, you're spending a lot of time at the airport." Always reaching agreement means you're either chasing only sure things or saying yes when you should walk.

### Prospective hindsight: the premortem (Ch 3)

Borrowed from decision scientist Gary Klein. Asking "Can we foresee problems?" produces a skimpy list because we're smitten with our own logic. Instead:

- **Premortem Part 1 (downside flash-forward):** "Imagine the negotiation is under way and there's been a major setback. What is it?" The shift from *whether* trouble might happen to *assuming* it did animates the vision and fosters watchfulness â€” so you spot trouble early and can get back on track or fall back to Plan B.
- **Premortem Part 2 (optimistic flash-forward):** "Imagine there's been a pleasant surprise. What is it?" Limit to ~10%-probability good news. This raises your sights and primes you to recognize opportunity.

Couple them ("a healthy schizophrenia" / "cautious optimism"). The cautionary case: Arvind Gupta blew a bargain property purchase with a buried lowball counteroffer; a single premortem question â€” "Something went disastrously wrong; what is it?" â€” would have surfaced the rejection risk and let him manage it.

### Plan B and commander's intent (Ch 4, "Plan B")

The Citibank Center block assembly is Wheeler's master class: the broker met each owner's different needs (paid movers, kept a restaurant open, arranged a condo for the church) parcel by parcel, while holding firm with potential holdouts â€” adaptive execution toward a fixed intent. Borrowing from Marine Corps doctrine, separate the **task** (what to do) from the **intent** (why). When circumstances make a specific task obsolete, you return to the underlying intent and find another way â€” exactly what lets a prepared negotiator improvise without losing the plot. Karl Weick's leadership distillation Wheeler endorses: *Situation* (here's what we face) / *Task* (here's what I think we should do) / *Intent* (here's why) / *Concerns* (here's what to watch for) / *Feedback* (now talk to me).

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **State your fundamental intent**, not just a target number. "What am I really trying to accomplish, and what must NOT happen?" (commander's intent). This is the anchor you return to when the plan breaks.

2. **Build the goal range.**
   - *Walkaway baseline:* anchor to your BATNA; then list 2-3 equivalent packages that all sit on the accept/reject knife edge. Define it broadly â€” a brushstroke, not a line.
   - *Realistic target:* the rung on the value ladder you genuinely expect to reach.
   - *Stretch goal:* a ~10%-probability outstanding result, plus the conditions that would make it real.

3. **Estimate their baseline** from available clues; hold it provisionally; note that you're likely uncertain and that uncertainty itself should make your strategy more provisional.

4. **Place yourself in the prospect matrix** (probability Ã— payoff) to decide *whether* and *how hard* to negotiate. Set a trip wire if you're in C/D territory.

5. **Run both premortems.** Downside: "It blew up â€” what happened?" â†’ watchfulness + Plan B triggers. Upside: "Pleasant surprise â€” what was it?" â†’ opportunity-spotting + raised sights.

6. **Anticipate likely shifts.** What new information, change in power, or change in your own goals could move the deal triangle? Pre-decide how you'd adapt.

7. **Pre-stage a Plan B** (a real alternative, like Sheldon's flip), and define the task-vs-intent separation so you can swap tactics without abandoning the goal.

## Common failure modes

- **Treating the plan as a script.** Wheeler's whole point: a rigid plan crumbles in turbulence (the co-op lawsuit). Prepare to adapt, don't prepare to recite.
- **A single bottom line instead of a baseline of equivalents.** Handcuffs you and blinds you to creative trades. Build the deal triangle and the goal range.
- **No stretch goal â€” anchoring on your own walkaway.** Satisficers settle cheap. Aim at what you could persuade *them* to accept, then compromise slowly.
- **An unrealistic stretch goal you then put on the table.** "Intentional wishful thinking" is for raising your sights, not for a take-it-or-leave-it demand that leaves you empty-handed.
- **Skipping the premortem / "bending the map."** Twisting reality to fit the plan. The optimistic Alps map only helped the soldiers because they didn't force reality to match it.
- **Asymmetric premortem.** Doing only the downside (defensive, prevention-primed, settles for minimums) or only the upside (ungrounded optimism, blind to obstacles). Run both.
- **No Plan B.** Without a real alternative you can't push, and you can't walk. Improving your fallback is often the highest-leverage prep.
- **Confusing plans with planning.** Don't mistake short-term means for long-term ends; keep returning to fundamental intent.
