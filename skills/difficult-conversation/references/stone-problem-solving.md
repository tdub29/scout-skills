# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” Joint Problem-Solving

## The framework

> "Now What? Begin to Problem-Solve." (Ch 11, lines 6781â€“6939)

After the Three Conversations have been worked through, the question becomes: now what? Stone/Patton/Heen draw from the Harvard Negotiation Project's *Getting to Yes* tradition: surface options together, evaluate against shared standards, decide.

> "Fundamentally, problem-solving consists of gathering information and testing your perceptions, creating options that would meet both sides' primary concerns, and, where you can't, trying to find fair ways to resolve the difference." (lines 6792â€“6795)

### The four moves

**1. It takes two to agree** (lines 6796â€“6815)

Neither party can impose a solution. Both must concur for an agreement to hold.

The practical implication: if you're stuck, you can turn the tables. "I'm not seeing how this works for me. Can you help me see something I'm missing? What would I need to be persuaded?" This shifts the listener from receiving-your-pitch to genuine collaboration on the problem.

**2. Gather information and test perceptions** (lines 6816â€“6876)

Before settling on a strategy, surface what you each don't know.

- "I'm not sure about X. Could we look into it?"
- "Here's what would persuade me. What about for you?"
- "Let's test our assumptions by trying [small experiment]."

Many difficult-conversation impasses dissolve once parties realize they were operating on assumptions that turn out to be wrong.

**3. Invent options to meet both sides' primary concerns** (lines 6882â€“6908)

After surfacing actual concerns (which often differ from initial positions), brainstorm options.

- Generate 3+ options before evaluating any.
- Each option should attempt to honor BOTH parties' primary concerns.
- Resist the urge to pick the first acceptable option.

Examples from the book: dog-exercise-vs-noise; weekend-work-vs-family-time; baby safety with cultural difference.

**4. Ask what standards should apply** (lines 6913â€“6939)

When options conflict, look for fair principles outside either party's preference:

- Industry/professional practice
- Local custom
- Precedent (how have similar things been handled?)
- Ethical principles
- The **principle of mutual caretaking** (lines 6927â€“6939) â€” that each party has obligations to the other beyond strict self-interest

Standards provide a way out of "your preference vs. my preference" by appealing to shared external frames.

## How to apply

After both sides have been heard:

1. **Confirm readiness.** "Are we ready to look forward together?" Don't move here while either side still has something to say or feel.

2. **Check: It takes two to agree.** Are you trying to impose? If so, return to listening. Are they? Name the dynamic.

3. **Gather information.** "What do we each need to know to figure this out? What are we assuming that we should test?"

4. **Surface concerns.** Each party names their PRIMARY concerns (often different from initial positions). What does each side need most?

5. **Brainstorm options.** 3+. Generate before evaluating.

6. **Apply standards.** When options conflict, what fair principles apply? Mutual caretaking â€” what does each side owe the other?

7. **Decide.** Once options and standards are surfaced, the decision is usually easier. If still stuck, [[cc-four-decision-methods]] can name HOW to decide.

8. **Commit.** Concrete action steps. [[cc-wwwf]] for execution.

## Common failure modes

- **Problem-solving too soon.** Before both sides feel heard. The first proposed solution gets rejected because one or both parties hasn't released into forward-mode yet.
- **Treating positions as concerns.** "I want a raise" is a position. The concern might be feeling valued, fairness with peers, financial security. Different concerns lead to different options.
- **Evaluating options before generating.** "Here's an option... wait, that won't work because..." kills options before others are considered. Generate first; evaluate after.
- **Skipping standards.** Going from options straight to "let's vote" without asking what fair principles apply. Often the standards-discussion reveals an obvious answer no one had named.
- **One party doing the problem-solving for both.** "Here's what I think we should do" without joint exploration. Even if the solution is good, the other party won't own it.
- **Mutual caretaking treated as platitude.** "We both want what's best." Often true but useless. Mutual caretaking is operational: what specifically does each side owe the other in THIS situation?

## Integrity check

Problem-solving frameworks can be used to bypass legitimate grievances by rushing to "solutions." Three guardrails:

- **Problem-solving comes AFTER the Three Conversations.** Don't use it to skip the feelings or identity work.
- **"It takes two to agree" runs both ways.** You can't impose; neither can they. If they're imposing, name it; don't accept it because the framework values agreement.
- **Standards can be gamed.** "Industry practice" can be cited selectively. The standards move requires genuine search for fair principles, not just cover for what one party wanted.
