# Wheeler: Creating Value & Breaking Impasse (Silk Purses and Closing)

## When to use

Trigger when the user is stuck in a deadlock or deciding how to finish a negotiation. Concrete cues:

- "We're deadlocked â€” how do I break it?"
- "How do I expand the pie / create value instead of just splitting it?"
- "We disagree about what'll happen in the future â€” how do I bridge that?"

- "How do I get past a win-lose fight?"
- "When do I walk away?"
- "Is this offer good enough, or should I push for more?"

## The framework

### Core thesis: differences are the raw material of value

Most stalemates come from the **mythical fixed pie** (Max Bazerman) â€” seeing negotiation strictly win-lose, so differences look like conflict rather than material for trades. Wheeler's reframe: "Just as differences of opinion make horse races, they're also the sources of value" (Ch 11). Value creation means finding outcomes that improve mutual welfare so one side's gains don't come at the other's expense.

### Five sources of value-creating trades (Ch 11)

1. **Valuation differences** â€” two diners both like pie Ã  la mode; one loves ice cream, one loves pie â†’ eat your favorite, swap plates.
2. **Economies of scale** â€” a bigger order may cost the supplier little more; buyer gets a better unit price, seller more profit.
3. **Time horizons** â€” a long-term sports contract gives the player security and lets the team lock in today's rate.
4. **Differing expectations** â€” "value is created anytime a pessimist sells to an optimist." If I think my business is maxed out and you see a bright future, you should buy it.
5. **Risk tolerance** â€” a wildcatter fears being wiped out by a dry well; a diversified company can bear that risk. Allocate risk to whoever values bearing it least.

### Contingent agreements: betting on the difference

When parties disagree about the future, don't argue it to a standstill â€” write the disagreement *into* the deal as a contingency or bet. Wheeler's vivid case: Stevens Aviation ("Plane Smart") vs. Southwest ("Just Plane Smart") settled a trademark fight with an **arm-wrestling match** (loser donates to charity; both got huge publicity) instead of six-figure litigation. The home-sale puzzle: a buyer wants the deal contingent on selling *his* house; you accept the contingency *but* add a twist â€” you keep your house on the market, and any new buyer's offer gives the first buyer 48 hours to match. Better for both than no deal. Contingent structures let each side act on its own forecast while bridging the gap.

### Breaking deadlock by reframing the problem

Wheeler's signature deadlock-breakers are reframes, not concessions:

- **Eleanor and Franklin** â€” a 1,400-page manuscript the first publisher said must be cut (author refused â†’ contract torn up). The second publisher (Norton) "just cut it in two." Two volumes, a Pulitzer and a bestseller. The first publisher was "stuck inside the box of thinking in terms of a single volume."
- **Bosnian/Serbian license plates** â€” neither side would adopt the other's alphabet; Holbrooke's reframe: "just use the ten letters the two languages have in common."
- **Recruiting a star (Barbara Corcoran)** â€” instead of negotiating compensation (substance), she changed the *process*: she had her loyal staff sell the diva on the firm. The candidate accepted because of the people. "She succeeded not because of what she negotiated but how she negotiated." (Roger Fisher: "Solutions aren't the answer. Process is paramount.")

The enabling mindset (Ch 11): defeat the fixed-pie bias and **naive realism** (seeing others' blind spots while blind to your own); hold the muse-and-editor balance (Nachmanovitch â€” don't let the critic edit the muse out of existence too early); adopt the **appreciative mindset** / "say yes to the mess" (Barrett) â€” bricolage, "MacGyvering" a solution â€” tempered by recognizing not everything is negotiable.

### When to walk away vs. close (Ch 10)

- **Push too far and you can lose it all.** Ben Evans negotiated a visiting offer up to tenure + top salary + bigger research budget, then asked to delay arrival a year â€” and got a two-sentence FedEx withdrawing the offer. The dean never said no; Ben missed the subtle warning ("Are we done?" meant "watch it"). A back-of-envelope **decision tree** (a sure-thing accept vs. a counter that could be better, worse, or fatal) would have shown how little there was to gain vs. all that could be lost.
- **But sometimes the over-push wins** â€” Derek Sanderson kept asking ("a limo for my girlfriend," "hire my father as a scout") and became the highest-paid athlete in the world. Wheeler's honest verdict: smart or just lucky he didn't exasperate them? You balance risk and reward; "the harder you press, the more you may gain â€” but also the more you stand to lose." J. P. Getty: "Never try to make all the money in a deal."
- **Knowing when you're done:** "The only way to know how far you can go without exploding a deal is by going a little farther than that." It's a gray area with hard-to-read signs.

### How to say yes (Ch 10)

Accepting isn't hitting "Confirm." Pick the form to fit your goal:
- **A. Gratitude** â€” "Yes, that's terrific! I know you extended yourself..." (strengthens the relationship; shows you know it's a generous offer).
- **B. Measured** â€” "I'd hoped to do a little better, but I can make this work." (lets them look good to their boss; signals you're at your limit).
- **C. Hedging** â€” "Excellent, we've got a deal â€” I'd really appreciate help on one last item." (locks the deal, keeps a small upside; converts the last issue from precondition to favor).
- **D. Conditional** â€” "Good! If you can guarantee delivery by month-end, we have a deal." (near-commitment, focuses on one final hurdle).

Always include thanks; avoid grudging "yes, but you owe me." When *others* say no, probe the underlying reason (Misino: "a successful negotiation is a series of small agreements"). The closing psychology: **loss aversion** (frame as avoiding a loss, not gaining a gain) and **choice overload** (offer A vs. B, not an alphabet soup â€” Iyengar's jam study). Anticipate last-minute blowups (the washing-machine and lightbulb closings) and hold something in reserve to throw in.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Check for the fixed-pie trap.** Are the "conflicts" actually *differences* you could trade on? List them.

2. **Mine the five value sources** â€” valuation, scale, time horizon, expectations, risk tolerance â€” and propose trades that give each side what it values most.

3. **If you disagree about the future, design a contingent agreement or bet** rather than arguing it out (earn-out, milestone, the 48-hour match, even an arm-wrestle). Let each side act on its own forecast.

4. **If deadlocked, reframe the problem, not the price.** Split it in two (Eleanor and Franklin), find the shared subset (ten common letters), or change the *process* (Corcoran). Defeat the fixed-pie bias and naive realism; don't let your inner editor kill the muse too early.

5. **At the finish, sketch a decision tree:** accept (sure thing) vs. push (better/worse/fatal). Ask honestly â€” what's the evidence there's more to win, or that you've done about the best you can? Watch for the subtle "watch it" signal (Ben Evans). Don't make all the money.

6. **Choose how to say yes** â€” gratitude / measured / hedging / conditional â€” to fit your objective, with thanks. Probe any "no" for its real reason. Use loss-aversion framing and limited choices to get their yes; anticipate last-minute blowups and keep a reserve sweetener.

## Common failure modes

- **Fixed-pie bias.** Treating differences as conflict, so you never look for joint gains. The first *Eleanor and Franklin* publisher's trap.
- **Arguing the future to a standstill.** When you and they disagree about what will happen, write it into a contingency or bet instead of debating it.
- **Reframing the price instead of the problem.** Concession battles where a structural reframe (split it, share the subset, change the process) would have dissolved the deadlock.
- **Letting the inner editor kill the muse.** Premature criticism shuts down creative options before they form.
- **Naive realism.** Spotting others' blind spots while blind to your own; assuming your frame is the only one.
- **Pushing one ask too far.** Ben Evans's withdrawn offer â€” gaining on the margin what's dwarfed by what you could lose. Sketch the decision tree; read the warning signs.
- **Trying to make all the money.** Getty's warning â€” a reputation for it costs you future deals; leave the other side something.
- **A grudging or graceless yes.** "Yes, but you owe me" sounds like whining; skipping gratitude weakens the relationship and implementation.
- **Overloading the close with choices, or framing as gain not loss.** Offer A vs. B; frame the cost of *not* agreeing.
- **Ignoring last-minute blowups.** Pressure built along the way erupts over a trivial item (washing machine, lightbulbs); confirm you're dealing with the decision-maker and keep a reserve item to close.
