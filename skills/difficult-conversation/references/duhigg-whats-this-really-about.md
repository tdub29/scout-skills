# Duhigg: What's This Really About?

## The Framework

> Within every conversation there is a quiet negotiation, where the prize is not winning, but determining what everyone wants. (Chapter 2)

Duhigg's practical conversation is not merely "solve the problem." Before people can solve, they must often negotiate what the conversation is for: advice or reassurance, decision or venting, candor or diplomacy, speed or carefulness, data or values.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Separate stated topic from conversation goal.**

"Should we submit an offer?" may really mean "Do you think this risk is safe?" "What do you think of Zoe's work?" may really mean "Can I say something critical without seeming cruel?"

**2. Notice practical clues and emotional/social leakage.**

Practical conversations show up through options, plans, evidence, deadlines, and tradeoffs. But they often carry emotional or social needs underneath: control, reassurance, fairness, status, belonging, or permission.

**3. Ask questions that reveal wants, not just facts.**

The useful question is not only "What happened?" but "What are you hoping will happen next?" / "What would make this conversation useful?" / "Are you looking for a decision, a recommendation, or a chance to think out loud?"

**4. Establish the conversation rules explicitly.**

People often need to know whether disagreement is welcome, whether candor is safe, who decides, and what counts as success.

## How To Apply

Create a conversation-negotiation brief:

1. **State the visible topic.** What does everyone think the meeting/conversation is about?
2. **Name the likely hidden negotiation.** Decision rights, emotional reassurance, risk tolerance, status, norms, or process.
3. **List each person's probable wants.** Use evidence, not mind-reading.
4. **Write two clarifying questions.** One about outcome; one about process.
5. **Draft a reset line.** "Before we solve, can we clarify what would make this useful?"
6. **Confirm the rules.** Decide how disagreement, timing, and final decision will work.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Solving the stated problem while missing the real want.** The patient asks for medical advice but needs control or reassurance first.
- **Assuming agenda equals purpose.** "Budget review" may mean choose, complain, align, or assign blame.
- **Skipping permission for candor.** People do not know whether disagreement is safe, so they either posture or go silent.
- **Over-negotiating content while ignoring process.** The group debates options without deciding who owns the call.
- **Confusing this with tactical bargaining.** The aim is mutual understanding of the conversation, not winning concessions.
