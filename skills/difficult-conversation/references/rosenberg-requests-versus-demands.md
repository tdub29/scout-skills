# Rosenberg â€” Requests vs. Demands

## The framework

A request invites; a demand coerces. To the listener, they often sound identical. The difference shows up in what you do if they say no.

> "When the other person hears a demand from us, they see two options: to submit or to rebel." (Ch 6, line 3562)

### The demand-detection diagnostic

Rosenberg's test (lines 3571â€“3614):

> "To tell if it's a demand or a request, observe what the speaker does if the request is not complied with." (line 3571)

- **It's a demand if** the speaker then **criticizes or judges**. (line 3581) Example: "You never help around here."
- **It's a demand if** the speaker then **lays a guilt trip**. (line 3602) Example: "After all I've done for you..."
- **It's a request if** the speaker **shows empathy toward the other person's needs**. (line 3613) Example: "I hear you can't tonight â€” what's getting in the way?"

The other person's experience: they sense the demand before you do. They feel they have only two options: submit or rebel. Both poison the relationship.

### What makes a request landable

A clear request has three properties (woven through Ch 6):

1. **Positive, action language.** Say what you DO want, not what you don't. "Stop interrupting me" is negative; "Will you wait until I finish before responding?" is positive.

2. **Specific and doable.** "Be more present" isn't doable; "Will you put your phone on the table while we have dinner?" is.

3. **Present-tense.** What can be done now, in this conversation, or as a clearly defined next action â€” not vague future hopes.

### Asking for reflection (a request sub-procedure)

When you've expressed something important, you can request a reflection â€” that the listener tell you what they heard. Three sub-rules (lines 3404â€“3443):

- **Ask:** "Would you tell me what you heard me say?" (line 3404)
- **Appreciate the attempt** even if their reflection is imperfect. (line 3427)
- **Empathize with resistance** if they don't want to reflect â€” don't demand. (line 3442)

This is itself a small request, and the same demand-vs-request test applies.

## How to apply

When you're about to ask for something:

1. **State the observation + feeling + need first** (NVC's first three components). Without these, the request lands as a demand even if its grammar is gentle. See [[rosenberg-nvc-four-components]].

2. **Make the request:**
   - In positive language. What DO you want?
   - With a specific action. Not "be supportive" but "tell me what you appreciate about my work this week."
   - Present-tense. "Right now," "this week," "next time X happens."

3. **Run the demand-detection test on yourself BEFORE asking.**
   - If they say no, will I criticize or judge? â†’ It's a demand.
   - If they say no, will I guilt-trip? â†’ It's a demand.
   - If they say no, can I be curious about their needs? â†’ It's a request.

4. **Be genuinely prepared for no.** Mentally walk through how you'd respond. If you can't tolerate no, don't ask yet â€” figure out why first.

5. **If they say no, get curious about their need.** "What's getting in the way for you?" That's the request-test passed in real time.

6. **Use the reflection-request when stakes are high.** "Would you tell me what you heard me say?" â€” and appreciate whatever comes back.

## Common failure modes

- **The hidden-demand request.** "Could you maybe possibly clean the kitchen?" with a punishing tone if not. The grammar is request-shaped; the consequence is demand.
- **The non-doable request.** "I want you to be more supportive" â€” what's the specific action? Without one, the listener can't comply even if willing.
- **Negative-language requests.** "Stop being so critical of me" â€” what should they DO instead?
- **Generic future tense.** "Be home earlier next week" â€” what counts? Earlier than what? Which day?
- **Skipping observation+feeling+need and going straight to request.** Listener has no context, only what looks like an order.
- **Punishing "no" while claiming it was a request.** "Fine, I won't ask again" with silent treatment for 3 days is a demand revealed.

## Compassion check (manipulation-risk)

The line between request and demand is also the line between connection and coercion. Three integrity tests:

- **Real openness to no.** Not theatrical openness ("Of COURSE you can say no, I'd just be DEEPLY disappointed"). Actual openness: their no is acceptable, and you'll explore what's behind it.
- **Don't use NVC grammar to coerce.** "I observe X, feel Y, need Z, would you be willing to W?" said with a knife in the tone is a demand in form-letter clothing.
- **Distinguish need from strategy.** "I need you to come home by 6" is a strategy. The need is connection, predictability, contribution. Strategies are negotiable. If only one strategy will do, you're closer to demand than request.
