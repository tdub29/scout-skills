## The framework

> "Expression: Speak for Yourself with Clarity and Power." (Ch 10)

After listening, the paired move is expressing â€” clearly. Not aggressively (lecturing); not weakly (hinting). Stone/Patton/Heen's framework has four layers.

### Layer 1: Entitlement

> "You're Entitled (Yes, You)." (Ch 10, lines 6012â€“6127)

> "My views and feelings are as legitimate, valuable, and important as yours â€” no more, but no less." (lines 6029â€“6030)

Many people skip the entitlement step. They either:
- Won't say what they think (silence yourself)
- Or say it apologetically until it can't be heard (water yourself down)

Both fail because the speaker doesn't believe they have the right to speak. The first move: claim entitlement. Your view counts. Not more than theirs â€” and not less.

### Layer 2: Speak the heart of the matter, start with what matters most

> "Speak the Heart of the Matter... Start with What Matters Most." (lines 6128â€“6170)

People often spend 10 minutes circling around what they actually need to say, hoping the listener will figure it out. Don't.

- Identify what matters MOST. The core concern. The thing that, if not said, leaves the conversation incomplete.
- Lead with it. Not last; first.

If the heart of the matter is "I'm worried this can't continue," start there. Don't tour the perimeter before getting to the center.

### Layer 3: Say what you mean â€” don't ease in with subtext

> "Say What You Mean." (lines 6171â€“6274)

Don't:
- Embed the message in jokes or sarcasm.
- Hint via body language and hope they read it.
- Bury the point in qualifiers so it never lands.
- Use questions when you mean statements ("don't you think you should have...?").

This is the "easing in" trap â€” softening so much that the message is undetectable. The speaker thinks they delivered it; the listener heard friendly chat.

### Layer 4: Three clarity guidelines

> Stone/Patton/Heen's three rules for clarity (lines 6321â€“6428):

**1. Don't present your conclusions as The Truth**

Your conclusion is a perception, not a fact about the world.

- Don't: "You're disorganized."
- Do: "From my view, the way the project is being managed feels disorganized to me â€” I might be missing context."

**2. Share where your conclusions come from**

Show the path from observation to conclusion. The listener can follow your reasoning and either confirm, correct, or update.

- Don't: "You don't care about the team."
- Do: "When you skipped the last three team meetings without explaining, I've started to wonder whether the team is a priority for you. I might be reading that wrong."

**3. Don't exaggerate with "always" and "never"**

> "Don't Exaggerate with 'Always' and 'Never.'" (lines 6396â€“6428)

These words make the statement easier to dismiss. "You're ALWAYS late" â€” they remember the one time they were on time and discount your whole point. Specific instances beat global claims.

- Don't: "You never listen to me."
- Do: "In the last three conversations, I've felt cut off when I was trying to share something important."

### The Me-Me AND

> "Use Me-Me And." (lines 6275â€“6320)

Sometimes your view itself is complex. Don't pretend it's simple.

- "I'm angry about what you did AND I still want this relationship to work."
- "I think you're wrong about X AND I really value your judgment on Y."

The AND inside yourself models the AND between you. It also prevents the listener from picking just one half of your view to respond to.

## How to apply

When you need to speak:

1. **Claim entitlement.** Internally check: my view counts, not more, not less. Don't open without this.

2. **Identify the heart of the matter.** The single most important thing. Write it as one sentence.

3. **Plan to lead with it.** Not after preamble. Start there.

4. **Run the three clarity guidelines on your draft:**
   - Are you stating conclusions as Truth? Add "from my view" / "my read is."
   - Are you sharing WHERE conclusions came from? Add the observations.
   - Are you using "always" / "never"? Replace with specific instances.

5. **Add Me-Me AND where your view is genuinely complex.** Don't simplify yourself.

6. **Don't ease in.** When the moment comes, say it. Plainly. With kindness, but with clarity.

## Common failure modes

- **Entitlement absent.** "I don't know if this matters, but..." â€” undercuts before you start.
- **Heart of the matter buried.** 5 minutes of context before the actual point. The listener has tuned out by the time you get there.
- **Easing in.** "Some people might wonder if maybe..." â€” the message is so wrapped it can't land.
- **Conclusion as Truth.** "You're being unreasonable." That's your conclusion, not a fact. Add "I think" / "my read is."
- **No origin shown.** "I think you don't care" without showing what observations led to that. The listener has nothing to engage with.
- **Always/never.** "You never X" provokes pushback to the form, not engagement with the substance.
- **False simplification.** Pretending your view is unmixed when it actually is. "I'm angry" without acknowledging the love that's also there. Listener gets a flat picture.

## Integrity check

The "clarity and power" framework can be misused to deliver harsh messages while claiming "I'm just being clear." Three guardrails:

- **Clarity â‰  harshness.** Stone/Patton/Heen's clarity is paired with respect for the listener's view. Brutal candor isn't what they're teaching.
- **Entitlement isn't dominance.** Claiming your view counts doesn't mean theirs counts less. The framework explicitly says "no more, but no less."
- **Speak after listening.** This framework is paired with [[stone-listening-skills]]. Don't deploy clarity-and-power as monologue.
