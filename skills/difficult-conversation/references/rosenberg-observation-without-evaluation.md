# Rosenberg â€” Observation Without Evaluation

## The framework

Krishnamurti's line, quoted by Rosenberg: "Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence." Most people fuse the two without noticing â€” and the listener responds to the evaluation, not the observation.

> "When we combine observation with evaluation, others are apt to hear criticism." (Ch 3, paraphrased; see chapter title line 1415)

The book's table "Distinguishing Observations From Evaluations" enumerates **seven specific evaluative-language traps** (Ch 3, lines 1591â€“1670):

### The 7 traps

1. **Use of verb "to be" without evaluator responsibility indication** (lines 1603â€“1612)
   - Evaluation: "You are too generous."
   - Observation: "When I see you give all your lunch money to others, I think you are being too generous."

2. **Use of verbs with evaluative connotations** (lines 1613â€“1618)
   - Evaluation: "Doug procrastinates."
   - Observation: "Doug only studies for exams the night before."

3. **Implication that one's inferences are the only possible ones** (lines 1619â€“1629)
   - Evaluation: "She won't get her work in."
   - Observation: "I don't think she'll get her work in" or "She said she won't get her work in."

4. **Confusion of prediction with certainty** (lines 1630â€“1640)
   - Evaluation: "If you don't eat balanced meals, your health will be impaired."
   - Observation: "If you don't eat balanced meals, I fear your health may be impaired."

5. **Failure to be specific about referents** (lines 1641â€“1651)
   - Evaluation: "Immigrants don't take care of their property."
   - Observation: "I have not seen the immigrant family living at 1679 Ross shovel the snow on their sidewalk."

6. **Use of words denoting ability without indicating evaluation** (lines 1652â€“1660)
   - Evaluation: "Hank Smith is a poor soccer player."
   - Observation: "Hank Smith has not scored a goal in twenty games."

7. **Use of adverbs and adjectives without indicating evaluation** (lines 1661â€“1670)
   - Evaluation: "Jim is ugly."
   - Observation: "Jim's looks don't appeal to me."

### The "always/never/whenever" trap

Words like *always, never, ever, whenever, frequently, rarely* often dress up evaluation as observation. "You're always late" â€” for what definition of always? "You never listen" â€” across what time window? Replace with the specific instances.

### The "I haven't done a thing today" antidote

Rosenberg quotes Wendell Berry's example of pure observation language. Practice naming what specifically happened (or didn't happen) over a defined window, with no character implications.

## How to apply

When you're about to make a statement of fact about someone's behavior:

1. **Write the sentence as you'd naturally say it.** Don't filter yet.

2. **Underline every word that comes from one of the 7 traps.** Is/are without "I think." Always/never. Loaded verbs (procrastinates, attacks, sulks). Adjectives describing them as a person (lazy, hostile, defensive). Ability-claims (poor, good, talented).

3. **Replace each underline with the specific observable thing.** What did you actually see? Quote what they said. Cite the times you observed it. Anchor to a window.

4. **Test: could a video camera have recorded this?** If no, it's still evaluation. ("Defensive" â€” no camera can see that. "Folded arms and said 'this is ridiculous' twice" â€” yes.)

5. **If you need to add an evaluation, use "I think" or "I believe."** This signals it's your judgment, not a fact about them. Compare: "Doug procrastinates" (fact-claim) vs. "I think Doug procrastinates" (claim-claim).

## Common failure modes

- **"I observed that you were being dismissive."** Smuggling an evaluation behind the word "observed." Adding "I observed" doesn't sanitize the predicate.
- **Using emotional words as if they were observations.** "I saw you attack me" â€” *attack* is the evaluation. "I saw you raise your voice and point at me" is the observation.
- **Specific-sounding but still evaluative.** "You arrived at 9:15 instead of 9:00 because you don't care" â€” first half is observation, second is a mind-read evaluation.
- **The 90/10 split.** Some people argue their statement is 90% observation and 10% evaluation, so it's fine. The 10% is what the listener hears.
- **Using observation as a weapon.** Loading up a pile of specific observations to support an unstated character verdict. The listener still hears the verdict.

## Compassion check (manipulation-risk)

Pure observation is the load-bearing factual basis for the rest of NVC. It's not a way to "score points" by being unimpeachably accurate while still attacking. If your specific observation is in service of a sentence whose subtext is "...therefore you're a bad person," the listener will hear the subtext.

Test: after stating the observation, what's the next sentence in your head? If it's a need ("I need predictability") or a feeling ("I felt confused"), good. If it's a verdict ("which proves you're irresponsible"), you've just deferred the evaluation by one sentence.
