# Wheeler: Emotions & Momentum (Presence of Mind)

## When to use

Trigger when the user's or the counterpart's emotions, mood, nerves, or the talks' momentum are the issue in a negotiation. Concrete cues:

- "How do I stay calm under pressure at the table?"
- "They pushed my buttons / I lost my temper / I caved emotionally."
- "I freeze up or go blank the moment a negotiation starts."
- "How do I prepare emotionally, not just on the numbers?"
- "How do I read their mood? How do I not confirm their worst fears?"
- "How do I keep my composure / not let them see I'm nervous?"
- "The negotiation lost momentum / stalled â€” how do I get it moving again?"
- "How do I balance being firm and being warm?"

Don't trigger for: scripted tactics for labeling the *counterpart's* emotions (route to `voss-tactical-empathy` / `voss-labeling`), relationship-repair feelings work (`stone-feelings-conversation`, the rosenberg-* family), or non-negotiation/clinical emotional support.

## The framework

### Core thesis: channel emotion, don't suppress it; master paradox

Wheeler rejects the read of *Getting to Yes* that says "separate the people from the problem" means ignore emotion. "Negotiation is about more than money or terms... People want to feel respected." Your passions "both reflect and influence your relationships." The problem isn't *having* emotions; it's when "your emotions have you" and derail the process. The goal is to put feelings to creative use as signals (Ch 5).

The defining move of master negotiators is **presence of mind**: holding apparent opposites at once.

- **Calm AND alert** â€” poise to defuse heat, vigilance to spot early signs of trouble.
- **Patient AND proactive** â€” trust takes time, yet only by probing do you learn how high the value ladder goes or when to switch to Plan B.
- **Practical AND creative** â€” hardheaded realists who still make lemonade.

These three dualities rest on a deeper pairing: **assurance AND humility** â€” confidence you can handle the task, plus acceptance that not everything is knowable or controllable (Csikszentmihalyi: self-assured yet "their egos seem curiously absent"; Brahimi: "arrogant... but also very, very humble"). It's not switching between them at different times â€” it's being both at once.

### Diagnose the three anxiety sources (Ch 5)

Wheeler's ZMET study of veteran negotiators found everyone harbors mixed feelings and that anxiety clusters around three concerns:

1. **Uncertainty** about what the counterpart will accept, how to engage them, and whether agreement is even possible. ("It's chess. It's thinking multiple moves down the line.")
2. **Relationship quality** â€” worry they won't be ethical, cooperative, or respectful (the "appears to be a handshake but they're holding guns" image).
3. **Performance anxiety** â€” fear of being outfoxed or appearing foolish (the "banana peels on the pavement" image; "if I come apart, everything comes apart").

Naming which one is firing tells you what to address.

### Know your hot buttons and don't confirm their fears

Triggers vary: insults wound some and bounce off others; some have infinite patience, others fray when talks drag. Look honestly at *when* in the process you falter (early? while closing?) and *how* (blowing up vs. quietly withdrawing). Conversely, manage the *other* side's emotions: "It's rarely in your interest to confirm their worst fears." Even hardball collectors must sense how hard to press before an explosion or walkout (the Tom Wolfe *A Man in Full* work-out scene). Donald Dell's poise â€” defusing the screaming chairman with "But Pierre, Arthur has a much better serve than you do" â€” relaxed the whole room.

### Emotional preparation: the six questions (Ch 5)

A fitness routine you run before the talk:
1. **What do you want to feel going in?** (and 2. **Why?**) â€” usually a balance: relaxed/focused/confident *and* alert/on edge.
3. **What can you do beforehand to get into that state?** Decompress (don't cram); a "let me call you back in three minutes" buffer; visualization; minimize distractions; promotion-priming (write the positive behaviors you'll use).
4. **What can throw you off balance?** Your hot buttons.

### Going to the balcony and beginner's mind

Bill Ury's **"go to the balcony"** (Ch 6 crossover): be fully engaged *and* a detached observer in the last row at once â€” which reveals that what you said may not be what they heard. **Mindfulness / beginner's mind** (Suzuki: "in the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's mind, few") means registering the first stirrings of your own anger without nurturing or suppressing it â€” noticing it like passing scenery. ### Sustaining and regaining momentum

Momentum is emotional fuel for a deal. Lofty goals are self-fulfilling (Galinsky promotion-priming; Jerry Weintraub's *Ocean's* casts â€” "everyone else is on board"). In the tobacco litigation, "that momentum brought the tobacco companies to the bargaining table" (Ch 1). Positive emotional spirals (comping a shared laugh) build momentum; negative ones (anxiety, low expectations) become self-fulfilling drags. When momentum dies, the mid-table reset moves (break, breathe, shift level) are how you restart it; asserting some control over the process is itself re-energizing.

1. **Name your target emotional state** â€” usually a paradox (calm *and* alert). Anchor it to assurance + humility.

2. **Diagnose which anxiety is firing** â€” uncertainty, relationship, or performance â€” so you treat the real one.

3. **Run the pre-negotiation routine.** Decide what you want to feel and why; decompress beforehand; visualize the balance; promotion-prime (list the positive behaviors you'll use); cut distractions.

## Common failure modes

- **Trying to suppress emotion ("separate people from problem" misread).** Ignoring core emotional concerns risks stalemate even when offers meet tangible interests. Channel feeling instead.
- **Treating the dualities as trade-offs.** "A little calm and somewhat alert" misses the point â€” be both at once.
- **Skipping emotional preparation.** Running the numbers and the draft contract but arriving emotionally unready.
- **Not knowing your hot buttons.** You get baited and underperform; the other side reads your "noise" before you do.
- **Confirming the counterpart's worst fears.** Pressing past the point of explosion or walkout; failing to relax a tense room.
- **Feeding the internal critic / pleading "concentrate."** Cure it with active outward focus (bounce-hit), not willpower.
- **Letting emotions "have you."** Nurturing anger or going blank rather than registering the feeling and moving on (beginner's mind).
- **Taking the break too late.** Reset before you need it; performing weary or irritated costs you.
- **Letting momentum die untended / low expectations.** Negative spirals are self-fulfilling; lofty goals and positive spirals are too â€” use the reset moves to restart stalled talks.
- **Aiming only to feel "relieved."** A sign the process is an ordeal; aim for "satisfied" with both outcome and performance.
