## The framework

### Core thesis: a dance of moves and countermoves

Wheeler treats the back-and-forth as choreography â€” "the dance of negotiation, how what one person says influences whatever his counterpart says next" (Ch 8). Every move conditions the next. The two layers Wheeler dissects are the **opening** (the first moments, where roles and tone get set) and the **critical moments / concession pattern** (the sequence of offers and the irreversible choices inside it).

### Openings: engaging, framing, norming (Ch 8)

In the first moments, three things happen simultaneously and are hard to undo:

- **Engaging â€” the WHO.** Identity, roles, relationships. We label friend/foe and gauge power "ultrafast," and the nature of negotiation tilts us toward negative reads (ambiguous comments heard as hostile). Katie Stevenson's story: the same people in the same room felt warm until she realized "a cloud was passing over the sun" â€” she'd entered a negotiation, and her own guardedness threatened the very deal her openness had created. Paradoxically, *seeing* something as a negotiation can make agreement harder.
- **Framing â€” the WHAT.** How the parties define the task: partnership, conflict, or haggle? Rarely stated explicitly; usually negotiated implicitly.
- **Norming â€” the HOW.** Tone and pace of the process.

Wheeler's videotaped real-estate pairs make it concrete. **Pair A** ("Welcome to my office" â†’ immediate jousting) fell into contradict-and-challenge, defined each other as foes, and used "I/you" language that widened the gap. **Pair B** opened with shared framing ("the business at hand that was dumped on *both* of us"), used "Yes, and" building, called each other by name, favored "we/us," mirrored posture, and depersonalized with humor ("I love it from your point of view") before asking "What's behind the company-policy line?" Same issues, opposite trajectories â€” set in the first two minutes. The master rule: **"Begin negotiation with an eye to how you want to finish"** and use openings to engage, frame, and norm (Ch 8 key points). Don't rush; small talk reduces tension. Avoid small ego battles that cost the war.

### Anchoring: the opening number shapes everything

The first offer pulls the whole exchange toward it. Wheeler's most vivid case is **Daniel Yin's kidnap-ransom negotiation** (Ch 9). Coached that the kidnappers demand the family name the first number, Daniel â€” who could raise ~$1M â€” opened deliberately *low* at "$50,000 right now." His reasoning: *any* first offer, however lavish, would be rejected ("he can get more"), so a high opener (say $500K) would signal the family could pay 10x that and anchor the kidnapper's expectations sky-high. The low anchor kept the conversation alive while shaping perceived deal space. He then "increased my offers only a little at a time" to "keep him interested but at the same time really anchor his expectations" â€” and laced in personal proof-of-life questions to control the tempo. The lesson generalizes: anchor consciously, justify the number, and deliver it in a way that keeps the dance going rather than ending it.

### Pace and pattern of concessions: signaling through the sequence

The *sequence* of offers is itself a message. The **Brims coffee-shop case** (Ch 10) is Wheeler's cautionary tale on both sides:
- The Westerly owner jumped from ~$250K to **$500K** in one leap. That dramatic move "revved up Brims's expectations" â€” it signaled there was lots more room. "A more measured sequence of proposals would have sent Brims a signal that there were limits to how high he was willing to go." If you must make a blowout offer, make explicit you won't consider counters.
- Brims, intoxicated by the jump, wanted to counter at $650K ("if he doubled in two months, he'll pay more") â€” and nearly blew up a fabulous deal.

The principles: move in small, decreasing increments; let the *pattern* communicate that you're approaching your limit; honor reciprocity (people repay moves in kind â€” and a huge unilateral jump invites a huge grab); and beware the reflex to "split the difference" or settle at point C where baselines barely cross, squandering value higher in the deal triangle.

### Shadow negotiation: moves and turns over power and respect (Ch 9)

Beneath the substance runs Kolb and Williams's **"shadow negotiation"** â€” a dance "about defining power, roles, and relationships." Chris let the fast-talking lawyer David run out of oxygen, then quietly took charge of the conversation's direction â€” a *turn* so subtle he may not have noticed. Critical moments often arise when parties work badly on these levels: a fight over a noncompete clause may really be about the seller's need for respect. **Outbursts and rescues:** Steve Jobs raged "You're ripping us off!"; Bill Gates's calm "correction move" ("we both had a rich neighbor named Xerox...") defused it without defending. Larry David's outburst-quitting-SNL and the "pretend it never happened" Monday â€” sometimes irreversible-seeming moments aren't. Key points: recognize what can and cannot be accomplished right now; know how to say no without ending the negotiation; use moves and turns to shift power imbalances; set up the endgame.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Decide how you want to finish, then design the opening backward.** What relationship, frame, and tone serve that ending?

2. **Engage / frame / norm deliberately in the first moments.** Engage as collaborators ("we/us," names, a shared task "dumped on both of us"); frame the task the way you want it solved; set tone with a little humor or small talk. Avoid early ego battles and contradict-and-challenge.

3. **Set your anchor consciously.** If you open, pick a number that shapes their perception of the deal space (Daniel Yin's logic: a high opener can signal you can pay far more). Attach a justification. Deliver it to *keep the dance going*, not to end it. If they open, recognize their anchor and don't let it silently reset your range.

4. **Plan the concession cadence.** Move in small, shrinking increments. Let the decreasing pattern signal you're nearing your limit. Don't make a giant unilateral jump (Brims) that revs up expectations; if you must, state that no counters will follow.

5. **Use reciprocity intentionally.** Trade move for move; a concession should pull one back. Don't reflexively split the difference or settle where baselines barely cross.

6. **Read the shadow negotiation.** Notice the power/role/respect moves under the substance. Use quiet "turns" (like Chris) to redirect; use a "correction move" (like Gates) to defuse an outburst without defending; know how to say no without ending it.

7. **Produce the dance plan:** opening (engage/frame/norm) Â· anchor (number + justification + delivery) Â· concession cadence (size, pace, what each move signals) Â· the shadow-negotiation read and any move/turn to make.

## Common failure modes

- **Opening with jousting (Pair A).** Contradict-and-challenge locks in a foe relationship that's hard to discard. Open as collaborators on a shared problem.
- **"I/you" framing.** Widens the gap; "we/us" signals partnership. Use names.
- **Rushing past the opening.** "Let's get through these clauses quickly" before any rapport reads as pushy and sets a brittle tone.
- **Anchoring too high without thinking through the signal.** A lavish opener can tell the other side you can pay far more (Daniel Yin's reason for going low).
- **A dramatic concession jump (Brims).** Revs up the other side's expectations and signals unlimited room. Move in small, decreasing steps.
- **Ignoring reciprocity / over-grabbing after their big move.** A huge unilateral jump invites a huge grab â€” and nearly blows up a great deal.
- **Reflexively splitting the difference / settling at point C.** Squanders value sitting higher in the deal triangle.
- **Treating a substance fight as only about substance.** The noncompete-clause fight that's really about respect; missing the shadow negotiation stalls everyone.
- **Saying no in a way that ends the talk, or defending against an outburst instead of making a correction move.** Know how to refuse without slamming the door (Gates vs. Jobs).
- **Believing every misstep is irreversible.** Larry David's Monday: sometimes you just pick the dance back up.
