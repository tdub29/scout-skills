# Rosenberg â€” Translating "Have-To" to "Choose-To"

## The framework

Rosenberg's three-step exercise for reclaiming agency from the language of obligation. The premise: most "have-to"s are actually "choose-to"s with a need hiding underneath. Naming the choice and the need either restores meaning to the task or reveals it shouldn't be on your list at all.

> "Translating 'Have to' to 'Choose to'" (Ch 9, line 5472)

### Step 1: List your have-to's

> "What do you do in your life that you don't experience as playful? List on a piece of paper all those things that you tell yourself you have to do. List any activity you dread but do anyway because you perceive yourself to have no choice." (lines 5474â€“5477)

Write the list. Don't pre-edit. Include the obvious obligations (taxes, dishes, meetings) and the subtle ones (reply-all to that email, that weekly call, that thing you always do because someone expects it).

### Step 2: Replace "have to" with "I choose to"

> "After completing your list, clearly acknowledge to yourself that you are doing these things because you choose to do them, not because you have to. Insert the words 'I choose to...' in front of each item you listed." (lines 5490â€“5492)

For each item, write: "I choose to ____."
- "I choose to do my taxes."
- "I choose to attend the Friday status meeting."
- "I choose to reply to that one customer's emails within an hour."

Feel the shift â€” even slight â€” when the obligation becomes a choice.

### Step 3: Find the need behind the choice

> "After having acknowledged that you choose to do a particular activity, get in touch with the intention behind your choice by completing the statement, 'I choose to... because I want...'" (lines 5501â€“5503)

For each: "I choose to ____ because I want ____."
- "I choose to do my taxes because I want to avoid penalties and stay in good standing."
- "I choose to attend Friday status because I want to stay aware of what my team is doing."
- "I choose to reply within an hour because I want to be trusted."

Now you have a list of choices with motivations. Some items will read: *yes, I genuinely value the need this serves â€” I'm in.* Other items will read: *the need I'm meeting here is fear of someone's disappointment, or social approval, or avoiding a conversation â€” and I'm not OK with that being my motivation.*

### The discovery

Items in the second category are candidates to **stop doing**, or to do differently. Either drop them, or find a different way to meet the underlying need. Items in the first category get permission to be done with renewed agency â€” the resentment falls away because the choice is conscious.

### Why this beats "just suck it up"

"Have to" creates resentment and depletes energy even when the task is small. "I choose to because I want to ____" preserves agency, even on hard tasks. Same task, different metabolism.

## How to apply

Allocate 30 minutes. With pen and paper:

1. **List 10-20 "have-to"s** â€” anything you do that you don't experience as playful or chosen. Big and small. Daily and weekly.

2. **Read each one aloud with "I choose to" in front.** Notice which ones feel honest (yes I do choose this) and which ones feel false (no, I really feel forced).

3. **For each "I choose to ___", complete "because I want ___".** Be honest about the want. It might be: to avoid conflict, to be liked, to not get fired, to keep someone happy, to feel competent. Not all wants are noble â€” but they're yours.

4. **Sort into three piles:**
   - **Reaffirm:** The need behind the choice is one you endorse. Do the task with restored agency.
   - **Renegotiate:** The need is legitimate but the strategy is wrong. Find a different way to meet it.
   - **Release:** The motivation is one you'd rather not be operating from (avoiding disapproval, fear of conflict). Drop the task or change it.

5. **Watch the language for the next week.** Each time "I have to" rises, swap it: "I choose to ___ because I want ___." Even small repetitions accumulate.

## Common failure modes

- **Refusing to do the exercise on the grounds that "I really do have to."** Almost nothing on your list is literal force. The exercise reveals choice underneath the language of force.
- **Performing the swap without feeling it.** "I choose to do taxes" said robotically. The shift has to land in your body, not just your phrasing.
- **Skipping the "because I want" step.** "I choose to" alone is incomplete â€” you need the need under the choice.
- **Listing only big items.** The small grinding obligations ("I have to reply right away," "I have to be at every soccer game") are where most of the leakage is.
- **Using the exercise to abandon real responsibilities.** "I choose not to pay child support" â€” choice doesn't dissolve obligation to others. The exercise is for distinguishing genuine choices from automatic compliance, not for ditching commitments to others.
- **Treating the result as permanent.** The list changes over time. Some "I choose to"s become "no longer choosing." Some "I'm releasing this" become "I'm choosing it again with different framing."

## Compassion check (manipulation-risk)

This exercise's misuse pattern is using "I choose to" rhetoric to justify dropping commitments that affect others. Three guardrails:

- **Choice is for the agent, not the relationship.** "I choose not to call my mother anymore" is your choice; the relationship still has the impact. The exercise reveals your choice; it doesn't dissolve what others depend on.
- **Don't use it to dismiss valid demands.** Some "have-to"s really are obligations (taxes, parenting, prior commitments). The exercise reframes them â€” it doesn't excuse you from them.
- **Watch for "I choose to because I want to feel superior."** If the need under a choice is harmful to others, the exercise has surfaced something worth changing in YOU, not licensing.
