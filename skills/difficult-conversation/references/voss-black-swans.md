# Voss: Black Swans

## When to use

Trigger when a negotiation is stuck despite competent tactics, OR when the stakes are high enough that "good enough" isn't enough. Concrete cues:

- A deal that should have closed by now is mysteriously stalled
- The counterpart's behavior "doesn't make sense" given what you think their interests are
- Major life decisions (custody, business sale, M&A, hostage scenarios)
- A high-value vendor / supplier / employee where the standard moves haven't surfaced their real position
- The user senses the counterpart wants something they aren't saying
- "I keep losing and I can't figure out why"
- A negotiation against someone whose worldview is very different from the user's (different culture, religion, profession, generation)
- Pre-negotiation preparation for an unusually important deal where deep discovery is worth the time
- The user has been told "they're crazy" or "they're being irrational" â€” Voss's signal that a Black Swan is present

Don't trigger for: low-stakes interactions (Ackerman + calibrated questions suffice), casual conversations, or non-negotiation contexts. This is the *high-stakes, deep-discovery* skill in the Voss family. The other Voss skills (mirroring, labeling, calibrated questions, Ackerman) operate on what's in the room; this skill is for what *isn't* in the room.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Voss borrows Donald Rumsfeld's epistemological taxonomy: known knowns, known unknowns, unknown unknowns. Applied to negotiation:

- **Known knowns:** facts both parties know (the price, the counterpart's name, their stated position, the deadline)
- **Known unknowns:** things you know you don't know (their boss's view, their alternative options, their walk-away point)
- **Unknown unknowns â€” Black Swans:** information you didn't know to look for, often game-changing once surfaced

Voss's central claim: **every meaningful negotiation contains at least three Black Swans.** Pieces of information that â€” if discovered â€” would completely reshape the leverage. The Black Swan isn't usually the price or even the position; it's *why* the counterpart holds the position they hold, what they're really afraid of, what they secretly want, or what worldview is filtering their judgment.

The reason "they're being irrational" is the Voss tell: when the counterpart's behavior doesn't fit your model of their interests, you don't have all the information. The behavior almost certainly *is* rational in their model â€” you just haven't found the Black Swan that makes it so.

### What Black Swans typically are

Voss categorizes Black Swans loosely into four types:

1. **Hidden interests.** The counterpart wants something they aren't saying â€” sometimes something they aren't admitting to themselves. A vendor "negotiating hard for price" might actually want to lose the deal because they're leaving for a competitor. A customer "pushing back on terms" might really be hiding budget pressure they're embarrassed to disclose.

2. **Hidden constraints.** The counterpart can't do what they're claiming they can do â€” but won't admit it. Legal restrictions, internal politics, personal shame, board mandates, regulatory issues. They argue from a position that isn't real.

3. **Ill-informedness.** The counterpart is operating on bad data. Wrong numbers, outdated assumptions, misread of the market. Once you reveal the real data, their position collapses or shifts.

4. **Worldview / "religion."** The framework â€” moral, professional, cultural, spiritual â€” through which the counterpart filters every decision. A military counterpart processes through honor and duty. A religious counterpart processes through faith. A trader processes through P&L. Arguing inside their worldview rather than yours unlocks moves that look impossible from the outside.

Voss treats worldview ("their religion" â€” his term) as the most powerful Black Swan because it dictates which arguments will land and which won't, regardless of the merits.

### How to find Black Swans

Voss's methods:

1. **Insist on face-to-face.** Black Swans rarely surface in email or even phone calls. The body language, the pauses, the half-finished sentences, the social-dynamic awkwardness â€” these are where the hidden information leaks. In-person if possible; video as a backup.

2. **Listen for what doesn't make sense.** When the counterpart's behavior, claim, or response doesn't fit your model, that's the signal. Don't dismiss as "they're being irrational" or "they don't understand." Lean in. The contradiction is the Black Swan announcing itself.

3. **Use calibrated questions to widen the surface.** "What else should I know?" / "What's the biggest challenge you face?" / "What about this situation am I missing?" â€” questions whose answers tend to surface hidden context. (Route to `voss-calibrated-questions`.)

4. **Listen for "their religion."** What worldview frames their decisions? What authority do they defer to (God, market, profession, family, country, code)? Adjust your arguments to fit *their* framework, not yours. Voss's Dwight Watson case: Watson was a Christian farmer with military service; Voss invoked "the Third Day" (Christ's resurrection) as the moment of decision. The framing was Watson's; the move was Voss's.

5. **Use the similarity principle.** People disclose more to those they perceive as similar â€” shared experience, shared values, shared background. Voss's hostage cases include negotiators emphasizing shared military service, shared faith, shared region. Honest similarity (not faked) lowers the barrier to disclosure.

6. **Use silence after questions.** Black Swans often leak in the elaboration that comes after an awkward pause. The 4-second silence after a calibrated question is the same silence that surfaces hidden information.

7. **Bring backup listeners.** Voss insists on having a second person on every negotiation. The primary negotiator focuses on the counterpart; the backup listens for tells, contradictions, and Black Swan signals the primary misses.

8. **Debrief afterward.** Many Black Swans only become visible in hindsight, when you replay the conversation with fresh eyes. Always debrief â€” write down anything the counterpart said that surprised you, anything that seemed inconsistent, anything you didn't follow up on.

### The three types of leverage â€” what Black Swans unlock

Once you have a Black Swan, it's only useful if you know how to *use* it. Voss identifies three types of leverage (folded into this skill because they're inseparable from Black Swans):

**Positive leverage.** Your ability to provide or withhold something the counterpart wants. If you discover a Black Swan revealing their hidden interest, you've gained positive leverage: you can offer (or threaten to deny) the thing they actually want.

Example: in salary negotiation, you discover the counterpart prizes title progression more than salary (their hidden interest). Positive leverage: offer a title upgrade in lieu of higher salary â€” both sides win.

**Negative leverage.** Your ability to threaten loss. If you discover a Black Swan revealing what they're afraid of losing (reputation, time, relationship, alternative deal), you can credibly threaten that loss.

Example: in a vendor negotiation, you discover the vendor's largest client is your competitor (hidden constraint they can't disclose). Negative leverage: imply you'll go public with concerns about their competitor's product if pricing doesn't improve. Use sparingly â€” negative leverage damages relationships.

**Normative leverage.** Use the counterpart's own rules / values / "religion" against their current position. If their stated position contradicts their stated values, the contradiction is leverage. This is the most subtle and most powerful form because it uses *their own authority* against them.

Example: in Dwight Watson's standoff, Voss didn't threaten Watson (negative) or offer him something (positive). Voss invoked Watson's own Christian faith ("the Third Day") and military code ("honor in standing down with dignity") â€” Watson's *own* normative framework â€” to make surrender feel honorable rather than defeat. Watson stood down.

Normative leverage works when the counterpart's stated values, professional code, or worldview can be invoked to support a move they'd resist if framed in your terms.

### Manipulation risk â€” the highest in the Voss family

Black Swan hunting is **information-asymmetric by design**. The user gathers information the counterpart didn't intend to share; the user then deploys leverage the counterpart can't see coming. This is the most ethically loaded skill in the Voss playbook.

The diagnostic gate:

1. **Consent.** Is the counterpart in a context where they expect deep discovery (a high-stakes negotiation, a legal proceeding, a hostage situation)? Or an unguarded context (a personal conversation, a casual meeting) where deep mining would be a betrayal?

2. **Truth.** Are the Black Swans you discover real ones the counterpart actually has? Or are you projecting hidden motives onto behavior that has simpler explanations?

3. **Disclosure asymmetry.** Are you exposing your own constraints / interests / "religion" at all, or only mining theirs? Pure extraction without disclosure produces an asymmetric relationship that damages trust over time.

4. **Leverage type.** Positive leverage (offering what they want) is usually ethical. Normative leverage can be ethical if you're invoking their stated values honestly, manipulative if you're invoking values they hold loosely to coerce them. **Negative leverage is the most dangerous** â€” Voss explicitly warns against deploying it as a default (the "paradox of power": the more you push, the more they resist). Use negative leverage only when other leverage types fail AND the relationship can tolerate it.

5. **Beneficiary.** If you successfully deploy the Black Swan you've found, will the resulting deal be one the counterpart can live with â€” or one they'll later regret?

Voss's own ethical posture (Ch 10): Black Swan hunting is about *understanding* the counterpart, not *exploiting* them. The frame: "I want to find what's actually going on for them so we can find a deal that works for us both." If the frame is "I want to find their weakness so I can extract," you've crossed into manipulation.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Diagnose: do you need Black Swan hunting?** Is the negotiation stuck despite competent tactics? Does the counterpart's behavior not fit your model? Are stakes high enough to warrant deep discovery? If yes, proceed. If no, route to lighter Voss tactics.

2. **Insist on face-to-face if at all possible.** Video at minimum. Email is the Black Swan graveyard.

3. **Map what you know going in.**
   - Known knowns (facts)
   - Known unknowns (things you know you don't know)
   - Suspected Black Swans (anomalies / contradictions / things-that-don't-add-up you've noticed)

4. **Generate the question list.** Calibrated questions designed to surface hidden context:
   - "What's the biggest challenge you face with this?"
   - "What else should I know?"
   - "What about this situation am I missing?"
   - "What would it take to make this work for you?"
   - "Who else needs to be okay with this?"
   - "What's behind this for you?"

5. **Listen for "their religion."** Throughout the conversation, listen for the framework they use to evaluate decisions. Cues:
   - Religious / spiritual language
   - Professional code language ("As an engineer, I have to..." / "Doctors don't...")
   - Family / cultural framing ("My father always said..." / "In our community...")
   - Economic / market framing ("The market won't support..." / "Investors expect...")

6. **Note inconsistencies.** Anything the counterpart says that contradicts something they said earlier, or contradicts how they're behaving, or doesn't fit your model. These are Black Swan signals.

7. **Use silence and the FM DJ voice.** The deeper information comes out in the second sentence after a pause, not the first. Don't fill the gaps.

8. **Bring a backup listener.** A colleague, partner, or advisor whose role is to listen for what you miss.

9. **Once you have a Black Swan, choose the leverage type:**
   - Positive (offer what they actually want)
   - Normative (invoke their own values honestly)
   - Negative (use sparingly, only when other types fail and relationship tolerates it)

10. **Deploy the leverage from inside their worldview, not yours.** The framing should sound like it comes from their values, their professional code, their religion. If it sounds like your framing, you've missed the move.

11. **Run the manipulation-risk gate.**
    - Consent / truth / disclosure-asymmetry / leverage-type / beneficiary
    - If any fails, recalibrate

12. **Produce the Black Swan hunt plan:**
    - Pre-negotiation: known/unknown/suspected map
    - Question list (calibrated)
    - Worldview-detection cues to listen for
    - Inconsistency log (filled during conversation)
    - Candidate leverage moves (positive / normative / negative) per discovered Black Swan
    - Manipulation-risk audit

## Common failure modes

- **Dismissing "they're crazy" instead of investigating.** Voss's tell: irrational behavior is rational behavior given information you don't have. Lean in.

- **Asking question lists in robotic sequence.** Calibrated questions surface Black Swans, but only when they feel conversational. Reading down a list reads as interrogation; defenses go up; nothing leaks.

- **Hunting Black Swans about their position while exposing none of yours.** Asymmetric disclosure builds resentment. The counterpart will eventually notice that you've learned everything about them and shared nothing. Reciprocal disclosure (your real constraints, your real interests) keeps the relationship intact.

- **Confusing absence of information with Black Swan.** Not every gap is a Black Swan. Some things are missing because they're not relevant. The Black Swan is *information that would change the deal if discovered* â€” not just any unknown.

- **Negative leverage as default.** Voss warns repeatedly: the paradox of power is that pushing harder produces more resistance. Deploy negative leverage only after positive and normative leverage have failed and the relationship can withstand it.

- **Normative leverage from your worldview, not theirs.** "You should care about this because [your value]" doesn't work. "This aligns with [their stated value]" does. The framing must come from inside their worldview.

- **Skipping face-to-face when remote is convenient.** Black Swans rarely emerge in async communication. If the stakes are high, the inconvenience of getting in the room is the price of entry.

- **No backup listener.** The primary negotiator is too busy talking to catch every signal. A second listener is non-optional for genuinely high-stakes negotiations.

- **Skipping the debrief.** Many Black Swans only become visible 24 hours later when you replay the conversation. Always write down anything that surprised you, contradicted prior info, or felt off.

- **Skipping the manipulation-risk gate.** This is the highest-risk skill in the family. The gate is non-optional.
