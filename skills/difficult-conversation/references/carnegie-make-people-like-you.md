# Carnegie â€” Six Ways to Make People Like You

## The framework

> "Part Two: Six Ways to Make People Like You." (line 3749)

Six small, executable disciplines. Each is simple individually; the combination is unusual enough that it produces strong relational outcomes consistently.

### Principle 1: Become genuinely interested in other people

> "Become genuinely interested in other people." (line 3751)

Famous insight: "You can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get other people interested in you."

The discipline: when meeting someone, your first question is "what do I want to learn about THIS person?" not "what do I want them to learn about ME?"

### Principle 2: Smile

> "Smile." (line 3753)

The cheapest, most-replicated influence move. The 1937 research Carnegie cites: smiles signal welcome, openness, lower threat. The effect is automatic, pre-cognitive, and cross-cultural.

The discipline: a real smile, not a posed one. The eyes participate. People detect the difference.

### Principle 3: Remember names

> "Remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language." (lines 3755-3756)

Names are the most-personalized identity signal. Using someone's name in conversation produces a small but consistent warmth-boost.

The discipline: actively remember names at introduction. Use mnemonics. Use the name immediately, twice, in the first minutes â€” locks it in for you and signals attention to them.

### Principle 4: Be a good listener; encourage others to talk about themselves

> "Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves." (line 3758)

Carnegie's strongest leverage. Most people are terrible listeners because they're rehearsing their next contribution. The rare person who genuinely listens is described, post-conversation, as "fascinating" â€” even though they barely spoke.

The discipline: ask questions that invite elaboration. When the person finishes, pause before responding (silent space invites more). Track what they care about; ask follow-ups.

### Principle 5: Talk in terms of the other person's interests

> "Talk in terms of the other person's interests." (line 3760)

Once you know what they care about, MOVE the conversation there. Don't redirect to your interests, expertise, or stories.

The discipline: when you do speak, pick the angle of your topic that intersects their interest. A real-estate agent meeting a teacher: lead with school district, not investment returns.

### Principle 6: Make the other person feel important â€” and do it sincerely

> "Make the other person feel important â€“ and do it sincerely." (line 3762)

The synthesis principle. Combines #1, #3, #4, #5. The aim isn't to flatter; it's to communicate (truthfully) that the person is worth your attention.

The discipline: notice what's distinctive about them, and acknowledge it. Their work, their thinking, the specific thing they did well. The sincerity test: are you doing this BECAUSE they're important to you, or to extract something?

## How to apply

When meeting someone new:
1. **Set intention to be interested (P1) before they speak.**
2. **Smile authentically (P2) at greeting.**
3. **Catch their name and use it twice (P3).**
4. **Ask an open question; listen fully (P4).**
5. **Find the intersection of your topic and theirs (P5).**
6. **Acknowledge something specific about them â€” what they said, did, or work on (P6).**

When trying to recover a stale relationship:
1. Stop talking. Genuinely re-explore who this person has become.
2. Ask about something they used to care about â€” see if it's still there.
3. Listen for what's new in their life; meet it where they are.

## Common failure modes

- **Performing interest without feeling it.** Asking questions while glazing over for the answer. The other person feels it instantly.
- **Smile as habit, not signal.** The constant social smile that doesn't reach the eyes â€” coded as untrustworthy.
- **Names used too often.** "Yes, John, I agree, John, that's smart, John" â€” the pattern becomes salesmanly.
- **Listening with rehearsal.** Listening primarily to identify your re-entry point. The eyes give it away.
- **Talking about their interests as a setup for your interests.** "Oh you like X? Well I did Y with X..." â€” bait-and-switch. Stay on their side longer than feels natural.

## Ethical guardrail

These principles can be deployed cynically as networking-manipulation. Three checks:

- **Genuine interest, not strategic interest.** If you're "interested" only because the person is useful to you, they'll detect it. Real interest doesn't filter by usefulness.
- **The like-them test for "feel important."** Are you communicating their importance because they ARE important to you, or because making them feel important serves you? The first is connection; the second is manipulation.
- **Long-term consistency.** Carnegie's principles work when they're who you are, not what you do. Performing the six principles around clients while ignoring family/employees produces a known caricature.
