# Wheeler: Improvisation & the Adaptive Mindset (Negotiation as Jazz)

## When to use

Trigger when the user is *inside* a live, unscripted negotiation and needs to respond to what's actually happening rather than execute a plan. Concrete cues:

- "They said something I didn't see coming â€” how do I respond?"
- "The negotiation has gone off-script / off the rails."
- "How do I think on my feet at the table?"
- "How do I read what's really going on between the lines?"
- "How do I steer the conversation without bulldozing them?"
- "I freeze up / overthink / lose the thread in the moment."
- "We're stuck in a routine that's going nowhere â€” how do I shake it up?"
- "How do I stay agile when I can't predict their moves?"

Don't trigger for: preparation before the talk (route to `wheeler-prepare-to-adapt`), managing the emotional swings themselves (`wheeler-emotions-momentum`), the concession sequence/anchoring (`wheeler-negotiation-dance`), or scripted tactical-empathy lines (the voss-* family).

## The framework

### Core thesis: negotiation is improvisation, and improvisation is a discipline

Wheeler's central claim (Part Two opener, quoting Wynton Marsalis and Richard Holbrooke): "Negotiation is like jazz. It is improvisation on a theme. You know where you want to go, but you don't know how to get there. It's not linear." A jazz combo of strangers creates something new "without an apparent plan or tight control over whatever happens next" by working with whatever the other players give them. Same at the table: you can't hand the other side sheet music.

But improvisation is **not** making it up randomly. "If it were, anyone could sit down at a piano and conjure great jazz." Improvisers prosper because they do three things especially well (Ch 6): pay close heed, know when to influence vs. adapt (solo vs. comp), and are proactive â€” even provocative. Wheeler frames the whole book around continuous cycles of **learning, adapting, and influencing** (Ch 1) â€” deliberate, interactive learning on three levels: the scope of the issues, the best means to resolve them, and the nature of the relationship.

### Move 1 â€” Pay heed (Ch 6)

Attention "well beyond active listening." Take in not just words but tone, posture, the expression that just flickered across a face. Herbie Hancock: "sometimes I'm listening with my toes." The enemy is internal chatter â€” while you script your next line, the interaction runs away from you. Formulaic "active listening" ("If I understand you correctly...") is at best acknowledgment, at worst patronizing. Real heeding means turning off the inner dialogue and absorbing the here-and-now, staying loose under pressure (the relaxed improv comic vs. the paralyzed amateur). Miles Davis's discipline: "not to play all the notes you could play, but to wait, let the space become part of the configuration." George Shultz heard a "last of the Mohicans" remark from a Soviet general, pressed gently, and discovered a man raised on James Fenimore Cooper â€” an opening that broke an impasse because he was paying heed.

### Move 2 â€” Comp (Ch 6)

In jazz, **comping** is accompanying â€” supporting and complementing the soloist. Strong negotiators take the lead *and* know when to relinquish it. Comping is not politely taking turns and not echoing; it's picking up "a seed of an idea that you can then nourish." When the other side floats three demands, two unworkable, the amateur attacks the two bad ones; the comper amplifies the one promising theme and builds on it ("Let's focus on that idea for a minute," "Maybe we could fold that into what we discussed earlier"). Frank Barrett (*Yes to the Mess*): musicians agree "to suspend judgment, to trust that whatever the soloist is doing right now will lead to something." This means abandoning the illusion of full control â€” paradoxically, micro-controlling each moment *lessens* your power. You may guide a conversation you barely speak in, leaving the other party feeling you accepted *their* proposal when you deftly got them to voice much of what you wanted. The improv "Yes, and" rule is comping in words (the collaborative real-estate pair in Ch 8 who build on each other vs. the antagonistic pair who contradict-and-challenge).

### Move 3 â€” Be provocative (Ch 6)

Barrett's "provocative competence": a willingness to abandon the status quo and venture partway into the unknown, because "otherwise nothing new can be created." Coltrane played in difficult keys because it "made me think." Provocation can be aimed at yourself (float a novel idea, be more or less confrontational than usual) or at the room (Gene Krupa's wild drum solo to "scare the band beyond their fear"). The governor: **Ed Sarath's 80/20 rule** â€” stay 80% in your comfort zone (so you can move forward without fear of irreversible mistakes) and 20% outside it (so you stay alert to danger and opportunity). Fear cramps your style ("if I force the play, I crush it"); a player who is completely comfortable just recycles past performances.

### Situational awareness: the OODA loop and sense-making (Ch 7)

Wheeler imports John Boyd's **OODA loop** â€” Observe, Orient, Decide, Act â€” as the micro-engine inside the larger learning/adapting/influencing cycle. Unlike a linear gather-then-decide model, in OODA your actions provoke reactions that become new data: a wheel within a wheel. *Observe* = heeding the other parties and assembling a mosaic; *Orient* = comparing what's actually unfolding against expectation, hunting for **mismatches**. Success comes from cycling faster than the other side, or disrupting their loop. Erin Egan's "social sonar" is sense-making â€” connecting dots, reading which "no" is firm (stated flat, no falter) and which is theater (protesting too much before a planned concession). Like a chess master who recalls a real board far better than a random one, experience teaches you to see pieces *in relation*.

### Double-loop learning and the design mindset

Beyond fast OODA cycles, Wheeler stresses **double-loop learning**: critically reflecting on whether you're even looking at the problem the right way. Major Lira in Iraq dropped his "Negotiation 1.0" linear checklist when it failed in a complex environment and reframed the whole approach â€” and his unit became a model. The **design mindset**: you shape both the substance *and* the process. "You negotiate how to negotiate each and every time you sit down" (Ch 8). And the master frame of the book â€” Tom Green's "making chaos my friend": all negotiations are chaotic because they unfold in fluid, unpredictable environments; the process is propelled by how the parties interact, so small moves can change everything. Don't fight the chaos; embrace it.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Quiet the inner chatter and pay heed.** Before responding, actually take in their words, tone, posture, and what just shifted. Let space exist; don't rush to fill it.

2. **Run an OODA pass.** *Observe* what they just did â†’ *Orient*: does it match what you expected, or is there a mismatch? A mismatch is signal â€” something you didn't know is in play. *Decide* the smallest useful next move â†’ *Act*, then watch how they react (new data).

3. **Choose a comp move.** Identify the one promising seed in what they said and amplify it ("Let's build on that") rather than attacking the parts that won't work. Use a light "Yes, and" to keep the flow constructive. Resist the urge to annotate every point.

4. **If stuck, get provocative â€” within 80/20.** Float a novel idea, reframe the problem, or change the energy. Stay 80% in your comfort zone so you can recover; push 20% out so something new can happen. Never force it (forcing crushes the play).

5. **Sense-make on their "no"s.** Is the refusal firm (flat, no falter) or theater (protesting too much before a likely concession)? Read relationships among the cues, not cues in isolation.

6. **Double-loop check.** Periodically step back: am I even framing this problem correctly? If the current frame keeps failing, change the frame (Lira's move), not just the tactic.

7. **Design the process, not just the deal.** Notice and shape the who/what/how of how you're negotiating (engaging, framing, norming). If haggling over details stalls, shift to principles or to process itself.

8. **Produce the read-and-respond plan:** the mismatch you just observed Â· the comp move (which seed to amplify) Â· a provocative move held in reserve if stuck Â· the sense-making read on their last "no" Â· any process-design adjustment.

## Common failure modes

- **Scripting your next line while they're still talking.** The interaction runs away from you; you miss the seed you should have comped. Pay heed first.
- **Mistaking active-listening formulas for heeding.** "If I understand you correctly..." is acknowledgment, not the deep attention improvisation requires.
- **Attacking the unworkable parts instead of comping the workable one.** Buckets-of-paint, brushes-back exchanges that leave both sides in opposite corners. Amplify the one good theme.
- **Micro-controlling every moment.** Paradoxically lessens your power. Suspend judgment, support the soloist, steer subtly.
- **Provoking outside the 80/20 band.** Too far out and you're in over your head; never out and you just recycle. Calibrate the 20%.
- **Forcing the move.** "If I force the play, I crush it." Stay loose; bracing against error causes the error.
- **Single-loop only.** Cycling OODA fast but never asking whether the *frame* is wrong (Lira's "Negotiation 1.0" trap). Reflect on the problem definition, not just the tactics.
- **Fighting the chaos instead of befriending it.** Treating unpredictability as failure rather than as the medium. Embrace it; small moves can open new deals (Sheldon's flip).
- **Ignoring the process.** You're always negotiating *how* to negotiate; leaving it unmanaged lets the other side set the tune.
