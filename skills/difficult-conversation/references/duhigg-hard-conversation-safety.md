# Duhigg: Hard Conversation Safety

## The Framework

> When discussing issues of identity, no one is allowed to blame, shame, or attack; it is okay to ask questions if they are asked in good faith. (Chapter 7)

Duhigg's Netflix and race-conversation examples show that hard conversations need structure. "Just be candid" is not enough when identity threat is high. Safety comes from preparing, naming norms, acknowledging discomfort, speaking from experience, and keeping people out of broad generalizations.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Prepare the conversation, not just the content.**

Ask: who should speak first, what emotions may arise, what obstacles are likely, what benefits justify the risk, and how will we respond when tension appears?

**2. State norms before the hard part.**

No blame, shame, or attack. Good-faith questions are allowed. Discomfort is expected. Mistakes may happen. The goal is understanding, not winning or public punishment.

**3. Speak from experience instead of generalization.**

"I experienced..." and "I felt..." reduce identity threat. "People like you always..." and "everyone knows..." make people one-dimensional.

**4. Acknowledge pain without minimizing or solving.**

When someone shares a painful identity experience, listen, acknowledge, and resist turning immediately to explanation, repair, or debate.

## How To Apply

Create a hard-conversation safety brief:

1. **Name the sensitive topic and why it matters.**
2. **List the likely identity threats.**
3. **Choose facilitation norms.**
4. **Plan the opening acknowledgment.**
5. **Decide who speaks first.** Duhigg notes that in some contexts the person with least power should begin.
6. **Write intervention lines for tension.**
7. **Define success as understanding plus next action, not emotional perfection.**

## Common Failure Modes

- **Radical candor without guardrails.** Fast, blunt cultures can go nuclear on identity topics.
- **Avoidance disguised as safety.** Silence prevents immediate discomfort but preserves the problem.
- **Generalization spiral.** Broad labels make people defensive and one-dimensional.
- **Burden asymmetry.** Some people do all the vulnerable explaining while others merely observe.
- **Fixing the pain too quickly.** Solving or minimizing can communicate that the discomfort is inconvenient rather than real.
