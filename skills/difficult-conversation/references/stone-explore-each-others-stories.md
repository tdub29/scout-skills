# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” Explore Each Other's Stories

## The framework

> "Stop Arguing About Who's Right: Explore Each Other's Stories" (Ch 2)

The What-Happened Conversation's core trap: assuming there's ONE story, with ONE truth, and only one of you sees it correctly. There are always at least two stories â€” both real, both partial.

### The two sources of different stories

**1. Different Information** (Ch 2, line 1350)

> "We and they have been exposed to different information."

You know things they don't: what you intended, what you've been thinking, what other commitments you have, what happened to you yesterday. They know things you don't: what they observed, what they were told, what they perceived your tone to mean, what context they're operating in.

The first task: surface what each of you knows that the other doesn't.

**2. Different Interpretation** (Ch 2, line 1400)

> "Even when we have been exposed to the same information, we interpret it differently."

Two people see the same event and make different meaning of it. Why? Two reasons:

- **Past experience.** Your history shapes what you notice and how you interpret it. Someone whose parents constantly criticized them will read neutral feedback as criticism.
- **Implicit rules.** You each have unstated rules about how things SHOULD be done. When the other person violates yours, you see "wrong." When you violate theirs, they see "wrong." Both think they're operating from common sense.

### Three moves to explore their story

> "Listen for their story. Inquire into their interpretation. Suspend judgment." (lines 1500â€“1570)

1. **Listen for what they know that you don't** â€” facts, context, history, perspective.
2. **Inquire into their interpretation** â€” "How did you read that?" "What did that mean to you?"
3. **Suspend judgment** â€” not "agree with them," just hold off on concluding they're wrong until you've heard.

## How to apply

When stuck in a "who's right" loop:

1. **Recognize the trap.** Notice the assumption you're holding: "their view is incomprehensible / irrational / dishonest."
2. **Ask what they might know that you don't.** Genuinely curious. "What's the context I might be missing here?"
3. **Ask how they're interpreting what happened.** "What did that look like from your side?" Not "let me tell you what really happened."
4. **Surface implicit rules.** "I'm realizing I had an assumption that... is that the same as how you see it?"
5. **Tell your own story openly.** What you know that they don't. How you're interpreting. What implicit rule you were operating from.
6. **Stay in the AND stance.** Both stories real. Both partial. Both worth understanding.

## Common failure modes

- **Asking "their story" as setup for refuting it.** "Tell me how you saw it" said as setup for "now let me explain why you're wrong." They feel it instantly.
- **Treating exploration as conceding.** Hearing their story doesn't mean agreeing. Most people resist exploration because they think it'll obligate them to capitulate.
- **Surfacing different information without surfacing different interpretation.** "I didn't know X" is one piece; "and we read the same facts differently because Y" is another.
- **Pretending you don't have a story.** "I have no opinion, I just want to understand yours." Disingenuous. Both stories exist; both should surface.

## Integrity check

This skill can be deployed cynically as "interrogation that looks like curiosity." Guardrails:

- **Genuine curiosity, not strategic.** Are you actually trying to learn? Or are you collecting information for the rebuttal you've already drafted?
- **Both stories surface, not just theirs.** If you're spending an hour exploring theirs and zero minutes on yours, that's tactical, not curious.
- **Different stories doesn't mean both equally valid.** Sometimes one person has more accurate information. Exploration helps surface that â€” not erase the distinction.
