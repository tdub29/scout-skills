# Crucial Conversations â€” ABC (Agree, Build, Compare)

## The framework

> "When you and others disagree, use the ABCs: Agree, Build, Compare." (Ch 8)

After [[cc-ampp]] has surfaced the other person's view, ABC is the framework for responding when you partially agree, partially want to add, and partially disagree. The structure forces you to acknowledge their valid points BEFORE adding your own â€” which is the single move most people skip.

> Most disagreements are violent agreements: people share 80% of the same view, then argue about the 20% as if it were the whole. ABC keeps the 80% visible.

### The three moves, in order

**A â€” Agree**

Find what's TRUE in what they said. Genuinely. State it back.

> "When you agree with the parts you agree with, you don't waste time arguing over points of violent agreement."

Sample language: "You're right that we missed the deadline." "I agree completely that the team is overworked." "I think your point about X is exactly right."

Why first: it signals you actually heard them. The listener (especially one who was just in silence/violence) doesn't shift out of defensive posture until they hear you acknowledge what's true in their view.

**B â€” Build**

Add what they missed. Not "but here's another angle" â€” "AND here's another piece."

> "When you agree but feel the information is incomplete, build."

Sample language: "I agree we missed the deadline. I'd ADD that I'm also noticing the requirements changed three times â€” both are at play." "You're right about the workload. I'd build on that by saying the on-call rotation is making it worse."

Why second: builds preserve their valid point; they don't replace it.

**C â€” Compare**

Where you do disagree, NAME the difference clearly â€” without dismissing their view.

> "When you differ significantly, don't suggest others are wrong. Instead, Compare your two views. Acknowledge that what they said and what you're about to say differ â€” and explore why."

Sample language: "I see this differently. You said X; I'm seeing more like Y. Let me share what's leading me there." "We're seeing this differently â€” can I explain my picture?"

The key: "I see this differently" preserves their view as legitimate while introducing yours. NOT "you're wrong" or "no, actually..."

### Why the order matters

If you Compare first ("I disagree") and only later Agree, the listener registers the disagreement and tunes out the agreement. Even if structurally you said all three, only Compare lands. Agree-Build-Compare front-loads the validation.

### When to use which combination

- If you fully agree â†’ just Agree (sometimes Build).
- If you mostly agree â†’ Agree + Build.
- If you partially disagree â†’ Agree + Build + Compare.
- If you fully disagree â†’ still find SOMETHING to Agree with (their concern is valid even if their conclusion isn't), then Compare.

There's almost always an Agree to make â€” even with views you find wrong, the underlying concern usually has merit.

## How to apply

After AMPP-ing someone:

1. **Note what they actually said.** Don't move to your response yet.

2. **Find the Agree.** Ask yourself: "What in what they said is TRUE?" There's almost always something. Often: their underlying concern, their factual observation, their evaluation of some part.

3. **State the Agree, genuinely.** "You're right that..." Then PAUSE. Let it land. Don't rush to but/however.

4. **Build, if there's something they missed that supports their view.** "I'd add..." or "Building on that..."

5. **Compare, if you genuinely see something differently.** "I see this differently â€” let me share where I'm coming from..." Use STATE for the Compare content if it's significant.

6. **Invite them back.** "Does that fit, or am I missing something?"

## Common failure modes

- **The fake Agree.** "I hear you, BUT..." with no real acknowledgment of the valid point. Listener registers "but"; agreement was theater.
- **Tepid Agree as set-up for big disagree.** "You're right that the sky is blue. NOW, about why everything else you said is wrong..." The Agree has to be substantive enough to actually validate.
- **Building when you should Compare.** Pretending to "add" when you're really replacing. "Yes, AND ALSO I think you're totally wrong about Y." That's not building.
- **Comparing as a verdict.** "I see this differently" followed by "and the truth is X." Compare offers your view; it doesn't pronounce theirs wrong.
- **Skipping ABC because "I just disagree."** Even in disagreement, the Agree exists somewhere â€” usually with the underlying concern or with one of their facts. Force yourself to find it.
- **ABC without AMPP first.** Responding before you've actually heard them. You'll Agree with what you THINK they said, Build on what you THINK they missed, Compare with a strawman. AMPP first; ABC after.

## Response-integrity check

ABC can be deployed cynically to soften disagreement without genuinely engaging. Three guardrails:

- **Real Agree, not strategic Agree.** If "agreeing" is just a verbal pattern you use before "but," you're not doing ABC. The Agree has to be a genuine acknowledgment of something true.
- **Compare isn't "let's agree to disagree."** Comparing means showing where and why you see differently â€” with the implicit invitation to keep examining. "Agree to disagree" is a way to stop the conversation; Compare keeps it open.
- **Don't ABC instead of doing real work.** When you're avoiding a hard truth ("they're wrong about Z but I'll just Agree-with-Y"), ABC becomes evasion. The Compare has to actually compare.
