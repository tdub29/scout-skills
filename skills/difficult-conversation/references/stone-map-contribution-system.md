# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” Map the Contribution System

## The framework

> "Abandon Blame: Map the Contribution System" (Ch 4)

Blame asks: who was bad? Contribution asks: how did each of us help produce this? They feel similar; they're opposite. Blame closes the door (judgment + punishment); contribution opens it (understanding + change).

### The core distinction

> "We judge people backward (what they are), while we judge ourselves forward (what we are trying to accomplish)... judging backward is blaming; judging ourselves forward is mapping contribution." (Ch 4, lines 2319â€“2353)

Blame is retrospective: "they were careless / mean / lazy" â€” a verdict on character.
Contribution is causal: "they avoided raising the issue, I was unapproachable, so we both contributed to the system that produced this outcome."

### The four contribution types

Stone/Patton/Heen enumerate four ways people contribute to a problem (Ch 4, lines 2675â€“2827):

**1. Avoiding** (lines 2675â€“2700)

Not raising an issue you needed to raise. Not bringing up the concern. Not asking the question.

> "She avoids expressing her concerns directly; he avoids engaging on the issue. These avoidances contribute to the problem."

**2. Being unapproachable** (lines 2710â€“2735)

Through tone, reactivity, or defensiveness, you create a climate where others won't raise issues. The "tells me my failures get punished" pattern.

> "When someone is unapproachable (through tone, reactivity, or defensiveness), others avoid raising issues, which contributes to the problem."

**3. Intersecting contributions** (lines 2750â€“2780)

Two parties each have strengths/styles that, alone, are fine â€” but at the intersection produce collision. Detail-oriented person meets fast-decision person. Cautious meets risk-tolerant.

> "Her contribution (being detailed and perfectionist) intersects with his contribution (wanting quick decisions), creating a collision."

**4. Role assumptions** (lines 2800â€“2827)

The structural roles you each occupy carry implicit assumptions that contribute. "I'm the boss, so I assumed I shouldn't have to repeat myself." "I'm the employee, so I assumed I shouldn't push back."

> "Assumptions embedded in roles (I'm the boss, you're the employee; I'm the parent, you're the child) can themselves be contributions."

### The procedure

1. **Drop the question "whose fault?"** Replace with "how did each of us contribute?"
2. **Map both contributions.** Yours and theirs. With specifics, not generic claims of mutuality.
3. **Identify which type each contribution is.** (Often more than one.)
4. **Communicate your contributions first.** Naming your own contribution invites them to name theirs; the opposite invites defensiveness.
5. **Surface contributions WITHOUT judgment.** "I avoided bringing this up" is contribution. "I'm such a coward" is blame turned inward.

## How to apply

When stuck on "they did it":

1. **Recognize the blame frame.** You're trying to settle who's at fault. Note this.
2. **For each party, ask: what did they do (or fail to do) that contributed?** Not judgment â€” what specifically.
3. **Check each contribution type:**
   - Did either of us AVOID something we should have raised?
   - Was one of us UNAPPROACHABLE so the other couldn't raise it?
   - Are our styles INTERSECTING badly?
   - Are we operating from ROLE ASSUMPTIONS that don't fit?
4. **List your own contributions first.** Honestly. Several.
5. **In the conversation: open with your contribution.** "I want to start by naming what I did to contribute to this. I avoided X. I was unapproachable about Y."
6. **Invite theirs.** "What's your sense of how your side of this contributed?" Without accusation.

## Common failure modes

- **Mapping contribution as "polite blame."** "I contributed 5% and you contributed 95%" â€” that's blame in contribution clothing. Honest contribution-mapping is usually more 40/60 than people initially think.
- **Refusing to find any contribution of your own.** "I genuinely did everything right; they're 100% at fault." Almost never true. Look harder â€” usually you avoided something, or were unapproachable, or stayed in a role that contributed.
- **Mapping their contribution before your own.** Triggers defensiveness instantly.
- **Confusing victim-of-harm with no-contribution.** Severe harm (abuse, assault, malice) is NOT the place for contribution-mapping. The framework is for relational and operational problems, not for situations where blame is actually appropriate.
- **Treating contributions as equal by definition.** Sometimes one party contributes much more. The framework doesn't equalize; it accurately distributes.

## Integrity check

The contribution framework can be weaponized to extract self-blame from victims. Three guardrails:

- **Some harms have NO contribution from the harmed party.** Don't apply this framework to abuse, assault, or harassment. Asking a victim "how did you contribute?" is itself harmful.
- **Contribution â‰  equal share.** Two parties can both contribute and one can still bear more responsibility. The framework reveals the system; it doesn't paper over asymmetry.
- **Naming your own contribution honestly is hard; refusing to is dishonest.** "I have no contribution" is almost always a lie â€” sometimes a self-protective one, sometimes a self-righteous one. Find your part.
