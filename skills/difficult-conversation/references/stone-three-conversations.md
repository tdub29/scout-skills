# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” The Three Conversations

## The framework

> "Each Difficult Conversation Is Really Three Conversations" (Ch 1)

Every difficult conversation contains three layers running simultaneously. People usually argue only the first one â€” and lose because the actual stakes are in the other two.

### The three conversations

**1. The "What Happened" Conversation** (line 934)

What did each person actually do/say? What did it mean? Who's responsible?

Typical traps:
- Treating your story as THE truth (when it's an interpretation).
- Treating their intent as known (when it's an inference from their impact on you).
- Locating blame on one side (when contribution is usually shared).

Operational skills: [[stone-explore-each-others-stories]], [[stone-disentangle-intent-from-impact]], [[stone-map-contribution-system]].

**2. The Feelings Conversation** (line 950)

What feelings are at play? Are they valid? Do they belong in this conversation?

Most people try to make this disappear: bury the feeling, pretend it's not there, or weaponize it as a complaint. Both fail.

Operational skill: [[stone-feelings-conversation]].

**3. The Identity Conversation** (line 965)

What does this say about ME? Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love?

This is usually invisible to the speaker, but it's often the heaviest layer â€” the one that makes hard conversations FEEL hard. When identity is threatened, people get defensive, change the subject, or escalate.

Operational skill: [[stone-identity-conversation]].

### The AND stance

> "Embrace both stories simultaneously... the capacity to hold both their perspective and ours, to embrace the complexity and the tension." (Ch 2, lines 1715â€“1727)

The unifying principle. The shift from "I'm right, you're wrong" to "I see it this way AND you see it that way AND we're going to learn what's behind that."

The AND stance is what makes a "battle" into a "learning conversation" â€” Stone/Patton/Heen's term for difficult conversations done well.

## How to apply

Before a difficult conversation:

1. **List what would normally be the "What Happened" content.** Facts, who said what, who did what.
2. **Identify the feelings.** Yours AND what you suspect theirs are. Don't bury or strategize them â€” name them.
3. **Identify the identity stakes.** "If this goes badly, what does it say about me?" Competence? Decency? Worthiness? Same for them.
4. **Plan the conversation across all three layers.** A hard feedback conversation isn't just about the facts; it's about feelings (theirs and yours) and identity (theirs and yours, both).
5. **Adopt the AND stance.** Before opening, internally rehearse: "I see X, they see Y, and there's something to learn from holding both."

In the conversation:

1. **If you're stuck in argument, ask which conversation you're in.** Usually you're stuck because you're trying to settle "what happened" while the actual issue is feelings or identity.
2. **Switch deliberately.** "I notice this is feeling charged for me â€” I think there's a feelings piece here, can we talk about that?"

## Common failure modes

- **Treating it as a 3-step procedure.** It's not three sequential conversations; it's three layers running at once. The work is naming which is active.
- **Skipping identity.** The identity conversation is the most-skipped because it requires admitting what's at stake for you. Without it, the conversation runs on a hidden engine.
- **Burying feelings to "stay professional."** The feelings are still there â€” buried, they leak as tone, withdrawal, or sudden escalation.
- **Demanding the other person agree with your "What Happened" before you'll address feelings or identity.** That's the trap. They won't â€” and the real work was never there anyway.

## Integrity check

The Three Conversations frame can be used to bypass legitimate "what happened" issues by relocating them to "feelings" or "identity." Three guardrails:

- **Facts matter.** Sometimes the dispute really is about what happened, and feelings/identity work is evasion. Don't use the framework to dissolve accountability.
- **Don't psychoanalyze the other person.** Identifying YOUR identity stake is work; declaring theirs ("this is REALLY about your competence") is invasion.
- **Three layers, one purpose.** The goal is a learning conversation, not a more sophisticated form of argument.
