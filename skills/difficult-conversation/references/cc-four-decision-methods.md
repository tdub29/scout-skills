# Crucial Conversations â€” Four Methods of Decision-Making

## The framework

> "There are four common ways of making decisions: command, consult, vote, and consensus." (Ch 10, lines 6871â€“6872)

Most dialogue failures aren't about CONTENT â€” they're about not agreeing in advance HOW the decision will be made. The same level of disagreement that breaks a Consensus attempt would work fine via Consult. Picking the method explicitly is half the work.

### The four methods

**1. Command**

> "Either we make autonomous decisions within our area of responsibility, or outside forces place demands on us (demands that leave us no wiggle room), or we turn decisions over to others and then follow their lead." (Ch 10, lines 6880â€“6882)

One person decides. Used when:
- The deciding person has clear authority and accountability.
- The decision is small or routine.
- Speed matters more than buy-in.
- Outside forces have already decided (regulatory, law, market).

Used POORLY when:
- The decision needs others' expertise to be smart.
- The decision needs others' buy-in to be executed.
- Authority is unclear (now you have a fight about who gets to decide).

**2. Consult**

> "Consulting is a process whereby decision makers invite others to influence them before they make their choice. You can consult with experts, a representative population, or even everyone who wants to offer an opinion." (Ch 10, lines 6910â€“6912)

One person decides, but solicits input first. Used when:
- The decider needs information others have.
- The decider wants others to feel heard, even if they won't get to vote.
- The decision affects multiple people but doesn't require their agreement.

Failure mode: pretending it's Consult when it's really Command. Asking for input you've already decided to ignore breeds cynicism faster than just commanding.

**3. Vote**

> "Voting is best suited to situations where efficiency is the highest value â€” and you're selecting from a number of good options." (Ch 10, lines 6919â€“6920)

Group decides by majority. Used when:
- There are several good options and any is acceptable.
- Efficiency matters; you can't endlessly discuss.
- The group has agreed in advance to abide by the vote.

Failure modes: voting on something that won't actually work with a 51% buy-in (anything requiring shared execution), or voting before consultation has surfaced the real considerations.

**4. Consensus**

> "This method can be both a great blessing and a frustrating curse. Consensus means you talk until everyone honestly agrees to one decision." (Ch 10, lines 6928â€“6929)

Everyone has to agree. Used when:
- Stakes are very high.
- The decision affects everyone equally.
- Execution requires every party's commitment.
- There's enough time to genuinely work through differences.

Failure modes: using Consensus for routine decisions (interminable). Faking Consensus when one party caved to social pressure. Pretending agreement to end the meeting.

### The four-question test for choosing

Patterson/Grenny offer a quick decision-method-selector (Ch 10):

1. **Who CARES?** Don't include people who don't care.
2. **Who KNOWS?** Include people whose expertise is needed.
3. **Who must AGREE?** People whose buy-in is required for execution.
4. **How MANY people is it worth involving?** More people = more cost.

The answers shape which method fits:
- Few care, only one knows, only one must agree â†’ Command.
- Many care, several know, only one must agree â†’ Consult.
- Many care, many know, many must agree, several good options â†’ Vote.
- Many care, all must agree, stakes are very high â†’ Consensus.

## How to apply

When a decision is approaching:

1. **Pause the content for 30 seconds.** Note that the decision is approaching.

2. **Run the four-question test.**
   - Who CARES about this?
   - Who KNOWS what's needed?
   - Who must AGREE for execution to work?
   - How many is it worth involving?

3. **Pick a method and STATE it.** "Given how this affects the team's daily work, I want to consult with everyone, then make the call." or "Since we all have to live with the outcome, let's go for consensus."

4. **Set the bounds of the method.**
   - Command: clarify who is deciding.
   - Consult: clarify whose input is sought, by when, and that the decider still chooses.
   - Vote: clarify the options being voted on and whether majority or supermajority.
   - Consensus: clarify what counts as agreement (everyone speaks; no-one objects) and the time-box.

5. **Run the method honestly.** If it's Consult, actually be influenced. If it's Command, just decide. If it's Consensus, don't push to fake agreement.

## Common failure modes

- **Defaulting to Consensus when Consult fits.** Most decisions don't need everyone to agree â€” they need everyone to be heard. Treating every decision as Consensus is a recipe for stalling.
- **Faking Consult.** Asking for input you've already decided to ignore. People learn this quickly; they stop offering real input.
- **Not naming the method.** The group thought it was Consensus; the leader thought it was Consult; everyone is confused and resentful.
- **Voting without prior Consult.** Picking the most popular option before knowing what the experts in the room think.
- **Command when Consult or Consensus was needed.** "I've decided we're doing X" on something that affects people who weren't consulted produces resistance even if the decision is good.
- **Treating method choice itself as Command.** "I've decided this is a Command decision" â€” sometimes the choice OF METHOD also needs Consult.

## Authority-integrity check

This framework names power dynamics explicitly, which is both its strength and its risk. Three guardrails:

- **Command isn't authoritarian; it's appropriate-when-it-fits.** Some decisions genuinely belong to one person (the surgeon in surgery; the parent on bedtime). Pretending Consult when you have Command authority creates worse outcomes.
- **Consult requires honesty about influence-able-ness.** "I'd like your input" said when you won't actually be moved is a form of dishonesty. Better: "I've decided X. I'd like to hear concerns but my mind is set."
- **Consensus protects minorities, not majorities.** The right to require Consensus rests with those most affected. Don't use Consensus to give a minority veto on issues that don't affect them.
- **Avoid the meta-loop.** Some teams spend more time deciding how to decide than deciding. If the method-choice is taking 15 minutes for a 5-minute decision, just pick one.
