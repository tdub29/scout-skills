# Carnegie â€” Be a Leader: How to Change People

## The framework

> "Part Four: Be a Leader: How to Change People." (line 7384)
> "A leader's job often includes changing your people's attitudes and behaviour." (lines 7385-7386)

Nine principles for delivering corrective influence â€” change-of-behavior â€” without producing the resentment that corrective feedback usually produces.

### The nine principles (verbatim, lines 7387-7405)

**1. Begin with praise and honest appreciation.**
Open with what's working. Genuine, specific. Lowers defenses before the corrective material lands.

**2. Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly.**
"And" beats "but." "You did well on X AND there's an opportunity in Y" lands; "You did well on X BUT Y was poor" cancels the praise.

**3. Talk about your own mistakes before criticising the other person.**
Lead with your own version of the error. "I've struggled with this same thing â€” let me share what I learned." Levels the relational ground.

**4. Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.**
"Could we try Y?" beats "Do Y." Order produces compliance; question produces ownership.

**5. Let the other person save face.**
Avoid public criticism. Frame so they can exit with dignity. Embarrassed people don't change; humiliated ones rebel.

**6. Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."**
Improvement is fragile. Praising every step (not waiting for the destination) keeps motion alive.

**7. Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.**
"You're someone who handles details well â€” here's an opportunity." The labeled identity becomes the target they grow into.

**8. Use encouragement. Make the fault seem easy to correct.**
"This is a small adjustment, not a fundamental flaw." Lowers the perceived gap; increases willingness to attempt.

**9. Make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest.**
Frame the change as in their interest (development, mastery, relationship), not just yours.

### The integration

Each principle alone is moderate; the combination is dramatic. Carnegie's argument: the manager who applies all nine produces willing behavior change. The manager who applies one or two produces grudging compliance.

### What this avoids

- **The "feedback sandwich" as fake praise.** Carnegie's P1 requires GENUINE praise, not performative.
- **The "constructive criticism" that's just criticism.** P2 is about subordinating direct criticism to indirect indication. Most feedback fails because it skips this.
- **The "I need you to..." command.** P4 replaces commands with questions that produce ownership.
- **Public correction.** P5 protects dignity; correction in private has dramatically better outcomes.

## How to apply

Before delivering corrective feedback:

1. **Find genuine praise first (P1).** Specific. True.
2. **Identify the corrective indirectly (P2).** Use "and," not "but." Bridge the praise to the development opportunity.
3. **Surface a parallel mistake of your own (P3).** "Here's how I've done the same thing..."
4. **Frame the correction as a question (P4).** "What if you tried Y?" or "What would change if we did Z?"
5. **Plan to preserve dignity (P5).** Private setting. No witnesses. No emails that could circulate.
6. **Calibrate the difficulty (P8).** "This is a small adjustment" â€” assuming it actually is.
7. **Frame the desired identity (P7).** "You're someone who..." â€” speak the reputation they'll live up to.
8. **Show alignment with their interest (P9).** Why is this change in THEIR development?

During and after:

9. **Watch for any sign of attempt or improvement (P6).** Praise it immediately. Don't wait for full success.

## Common failure modes

- **The "feedback sandwich" that's transparently performative.** Two compliments around criticism, both clearly setup. P1 has to be GENUINE specific praise, not stock phrases.
- **"And" used to mask "but."** "You're great AND you keep failing at this" â€” the listener still hears the but.
- **P3 omitted or generic.** Saying "I make mistakes too" without owning a specific parallel. Doesn't land.
- **Questions that are orders in disguise.** "Could you try... since I'd really like you to..." â€” P4 needs genuine openness.
- **Public correction.** Even minor. Often produces permanent loss of trust, regardless of how the rest was handled.
- **P6 (praise every improvement) abandoned mid-arc.** Praising the first attempt then going quiet. The person reads abandonment.
- **P7 deployed inauthentically.** "You're an excellent manager" said to someone who clearly isn't. The reputation-to-live-up-to has to be plausible.
- **P9 forced.** "This will be great for your development!" when it manifestly isn't. The framing collapses.

## Ethical guardrail

This is the most-misused Carnegie section because it's deployed by people in power over people not in power. Three guardrails:

- **Genuine improvement-orientation.** The framework requires the leader to actually want the person's development, not just compliance. If the goal is compliance, the principles will be performed and detected.
- **Honest praise (P1) and honest identity-framing (P7).** Both require truth. Pretending someone is performing well when they aren't (or pretending they're "the kind of person who handles X" when they aren't) corrupts both principles.
- **Respect for the receiver's perception.** P5 (saving face) is critical, and easy to violate even with good intent. Imagine the conversation from their side BEFORE having it.
