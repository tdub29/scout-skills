# Duhigg: The Matching Principle

## The Framework

> Effective communication requires recognizing what kind of conversation is occurring, and then matching each other. (Chapter 1)

Duhigg argues that many failed conversations are not failures of intelligence or goodwill. They are mismatches. One person is in a practical conversation, another is in an emotional one, or a hidden identity conversation is shaping everything being said.

The three dominant conversation types:

**1. What's this really about?**

The practical, decision-making layer. People are trying to define the topic, make a choice, compare options, negotiate rules, or solve a problem.

**2. How do we feel?**

The emotional layer. People want empathy, reassurance, recognition, shared joy, comfort, or permission to feel something out loud.

**3. Who are we?**

The social or identity layer. People are discussing how they see themselves, how others see them, what group they belong to, or whether they are respected, excluded, trusted, or misunderstood.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Clarify your own desired conversation before speaking.**

Pause and ask: "Why am I opening my mouth?" A one-sentence goal often reveals the intended conversation type: "choose a budget" is practical; "air our complaints and hear each other out" is emotional; "talk about how our team treats contractors" is social.

**2. Look for clues to the other person's desired conversation.**

Practical clues: options, plans, deadlines, costs, "what should we do?" Emotional clues: mood, frustration, relief, grief, excitement, "I need to vent." Social clues: reputation, fairness, respect, group labels, "people like me," "as a parent," "as a manager."

**3. Match before redirecting.**

If someone is emotional, acknowledge emotion before offering advice. If they are practical, help define options before exploring identity. If they are social, name the identity or belonging question before turning to logistics.

**4. Ask directly when the signal is ambiguous.**

Duhigg's classroom question is the cleanest diagnostic: "Do you want to be helped, hugged, or heard?" Adult versions: "Are you looking for advice, a sounding board, or just some company?" / "Should we solve this now, or do you want me to understand what it felt like first?"

## How To Apply

Produce a conversation-type alignment plan:

1. **Name your goal.** Write one sentence: "I want this conversation to..."
2. **Classify your layer.** Practical, emotional, social, or mixed.
3. **Read their clues.** What have they said or done that reveals the layer they want?
4. **Spot the mismatch.** Where are you likely to answer in the wrong language?
5. **Draft the matching opener.** Start on their layer.
6. **Plan the bridge.** If you need to move layers, ask permission: "Can I offer a practical thought?" or "Can I say what this brings up for me?"

## Common Failure Modes

- **Solution reflex.** Answering emotional distress with practical fixes: "What if you just invite him to lunch?" when the person wants empathy.
- **Therapy reflex.** Treating a practical decision as a feelings session when the other person needs options and tradeoffs.
- **Identity blindness.** Treating "this policy feels disrespectful to people like me" as a procedural complaint.
- **Mimicry instead of matching.** Matching is not copying tone or surrendering your view. It means genuinely understanding which layer the other person is speaking from.
- **Premature redirect.** "I hear you're upset, but..." is often a layer switch disguised as empathy.
