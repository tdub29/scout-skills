# Crucial Conversations â€” WWWF (Who Does What By When, Follow Up)

## The framework

> "As you close your conversations with commitments, make sure you consider the following four elements (sometimes shortened to the acronym WWWF): Who? Does what? By when? How will you follow up?" (Ch 10, lines 7011â€“7017)

The post-decision conversion step. Most dialogue failures aren't in the dialogue â€” they're in the gap between "we decided" and "nothing happened." WWWF is the four-question close that converts intention into a tracked commitment.

### The four elements

**1. Who?**

> "To quote an English proverb, 'Everybody's business is nobody's business.' If you don't make an actual assignment to an actual person, there's a good chance that nothing will ever come of all the work you've gone through to make a decision." (Ch 10, lines 7019â€“7022)

A named person. Not "the team," not "we," not "someone." A person.

If a task naturally has multiple owners, assign a primary owner who is accountable for the whole and decomposes it.

**2. Does what?**

> "Be sure to spell out the exact deliverables you have in mind. The fuzzier the expectations, the higher the likelihood of disappointment." (Ch 10, lines 7033â€“7034)

A specific, observable deliverable. "Look into X" is not a deliverable. "Send a one-page summary of X options to the team" is.

Test: would two people independently reading the assignment agree on whether it's been completed?

**3. By when?**

> "It's shocking how often people leave this element out of an assignment. Instead of giving a deadline, people simply point to the setting sun of 'someday.' ... Assignments without deadlines are far better at producing guilt than stimulating action." (Ch 10, lines 7083â€“7088)

A specific date or time. "By Friday EOD." "Before our next meeting on the 15th." Not "soon," not "ASAP," not "this week" (which week-end?).

If the assignment is bigger than one deadline, set the FIRST deadline (e.g., "draft by Friday; final by next Wednesday").

**4. How will you follow up?**

> "Always agree on how often and by what method you'll follow up on the assignment. It could be a simple email confirming the completion of a project. It might be a full report in a team or family meeting." (Ch 10, lines 7091â€“7094)

Two things: cadence (when do we check) and method (how do we know it happened â€” email, meeting, tracker entry).

Without follow-up, the deadline is a wish.

### Document it

> "Document your work." (Ch 10, line 7149)

Verbal-only WWWF tends to evaporate. Capture it in a shared place â€” meeting notes, ticket tracker, shared doc. Verbatim if possible.

## How to apply

At the end of any meeting or decision-making conversation:

1. **Pause before closing.** Don't let people leave until WWWF is done.

2. **List every commitment that came out of the conversation.** Often more than you think.

3. **For each:**
   - **Who?** Name a person. Not "we."
   - **What?** Specific deliverable. If unclear, refine until clear.
   - **When?** Specific date. If they don't know yet, ask "when WILL you know when?"
   - **Follow up?** When and how. "Email me when done." "Status at next standup." "Check at month-end review."

4. **Document.** Write it down. Share to all participants.

5. **For meetings with high turnover (ongoing teams), capture WWWFs in a single rolling document** so nothing falls through cracks.

## Common failure modes

- **Soft Who.** "The team will handle it." Nobody owns it.
- **Vague What.** "Look into pricing." Look how? With what depth? Producing what output? Use "by [time], send [artifact] to [recipient]."
- **Implied When.** "Soon." "ASAP." "When you get to it." None of these are dates.
- **No follow-up.** Assignment + deadline + no follow-up = wish. Always specify the check-in.
- **WWWF for big things only.** Small commitments also evaporate. If it matters, WWWF it.
- **Documenting in a place no one will look.** Notes that live in someone's email; in a doc no one re-opens; in someone's head. Documentation only works if it's in the team's working surface.
- **Setting WWWF and then not following up.** The Follow-up step requires you to actually DO the follow-up. If you set "I'll check on Friday" and don't, the next WWWF is worth less.
- **Treating WWWF as box-checking.** Doing the form without engaging â€” "yeah, John'll do that thing by sometime, sure" â€” makes the framework empty. The honest, specific answer is what makes it work.

## Commitment-integrity check

WWWF can become a vehicle for over-assigning, over-tracking, or coerced commitment. Three guardrails:

- **Don't WWWF someone without their genuine assent.** Public assignment that the recipient never agreed to is a coerced commitment that produces resentment and missed deadlines.
- **Realistic deadlines beat ambitious ones.** Setting a deadline you both know is fake undermines the system. Better: a realistic date, kept.
- **Follow-up is for support, not surveillance.** "How's it going on X?" is a check-in. "Why isn't X done yet?" is policing. The Follow-up step is meant to enable execution, not to catch failure.
- **Sometimes the right WWWF is "no commitment yet."** If a topic doesn't have an owner yet, the WWWF might be "Sarah, by Tuesday, figure out who should own this and propose them to the team." A meta-WWWF is better than a fake one.
