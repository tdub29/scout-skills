# Voss: Accusation Audit

## When to use

Trigger when the user is PREPARING for a live conversation that the counterpart is likely to come into hot. Concrete cues:

- A negotiation where the user knows the counterpart has grievances (real or perceived)
- A pitch to a stakeholder who is skeptical or has been burned before
- A performance review where the user has tough feedback to deliver
- An apology conversation where the recipient is already angry
- A first meeting with a counterpart who has heard bad things about the user's side ("you're a big bad corporation," "you're the new manager replacing the beloved one," etc.)
- "How do I disarm them before they attack?"
- A vendor / customer escalation where the customer is incoming with complaints
- A family conversation where the relative has stored-up resentment
- A board meeting where the user knows certain directors will object

Don't trigger for: live in-the-moment emotion naming (use `voss-labeling` â€” different timing, different scope), crafted written communication (this is a preparation ritual for *spoken* delivery), or pure brainstorming (the audit must connect to a specific upcoming conversation).

This skill is the *pre-conversation* preparation ritual within the broader `voss-tactical-empathy` family. The accusation audit is the work you do *before* you walk into the room; the labeling skill is what you use *inside* the room. Both are tactical-empathy moves applied at different timing.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Conventional wisdom: when you have to face a difficult counterpart, soften your approach, be polite, lead with the positives, build rapport, and only later address the hard stuff. Voss's reversal: **lead with the hard stuff. Voice the worst accusation the counterpart could hold against you. Name it out loud, in your own voice, in the opening of the conversation.**

Why this works (mechanism is the same as labeling, applied to pre-empted criticism):

1. The accusation, once named, *loses its weaponized energy*. The counterpart can't hold over you something you've already said yourself.
2. The counterpart's defensive readiness drops. They came in expecting to attack; the attack has already been disarmed by you.
3. The user *gains credibility*: someone willing to name the worst version of themselves is treated as more trustworthy than someone who pretends the bad version doesn't exist.
4. The conversation moves immediately from "establishing whether the user is the enemy" to "given that we both acknowledge the bad framing, what can we do together?"

Voss's case: Anna, a contractor, walked into a $1M negotiation knowing the counterpart (ABC Corp) viewed her firm as predatory. Her opening: *"You're going to think we're a big, bad prime contractor here to push you around."* ABC nodded â€” Anna had said the thing they were going to spend the meeting accusing her of. The energy of the accusation collapsed. They worked the deal together.

### The two-stage protocol

**Stage 1: Audit (preparation, hours-to-days before the conversation).**

Sit down and brainstorm every accusation the counterpart could level against the user's side. Don't filter. Don't soften. Be *uncharitable to yourself* â€” list the worst version of every charge.

Examples for a B2B vendor:
- "You're price-gouging us."
- "You don't actually care about our outcome."
- "You're going to disappear after the contract is signed."
- "The product doesn't actually work the way you said."
- "You only care about closing the quarter."
- "You over-promised in the pitch."

Examples for a manager about to fire someone:
- "You never gave me a real chance."
- "You're scapegoating me."
- "The targets were unfair."
- "You're firing me because I disagreed with you."
- "Nobody trained me properly."

Examples for a contractor pitching a homeowner:
- "Contractors always go over budget."
- "You'll disappear in the middle."
- "You're going to upsell us on stuff we don't need."
- "Your reviews are probably fake."

Aim for 5-10 candidate accusations. Don't worry about whether they're "fair." This is what the counterpart *might believe*, not what is *true*.

**Stage 2: Selection and rehearsal.**

From the list, pick the 2-3 that are most damaging â€” the ones the counterpart is most likely to hold AND most likely to weaponize. Compose a label for each, using the same template as `voss-labeling`:

- "You may believe..."
- "It probably seems like..."
- "You're going to think..."

Then rehearse the opening: deliver the 2-3 labels in succession, calmly, in the FM DJ voice. Pause after each. Voss is explicit (Ch 3): this needs to be **rehearsed.** Without rehearsal, the delivery comes out either sarcastic or wooden, and the move backfires.

### The post-label pivot

After you land the accusations, you cannot just sit there. The next move is a pivot from "acknowledging the bad framing" to "what can we work on together." Voss's standard pivot moves:

- A calibrated question: "How can we structure this so it doesn't feel like that?" (Route to `voss-calibrated-questions`.)
- An invitation to co-author the solution: "It sounds like you have a great handle on how this should work. How would you amend it?"
- A summary of mutual interests: "We both want X. Given the bad ways this could go, what would make you comfortable with how we proceed?"

The pivot is part of the audit. An accusation labeled without a pivot leaves the conversation in the negative space the accusation created.

### What to look for after the label

If you've done it right, you'll see:

- A nod or "yeah" from the counterpart â€” they're acknowledging that they did hold that view
- A softening of body language â€” the readiness to attack drops
- The counterpart adding their own clarification or qualification ("Well, not entirely, but yeah, we were worried about that")
- A faster move into substantive conversation

If you see *escalation* instead, you've probably either picked the wrong accusations to label or delivered them with tone problems. The recovery: drop into normal labeling (`voss-labeling`), step back from the audit, and let real-time work take over.

### Manipulation risk

The accusation audit can be misused in two specific ways:

1. **Inventing accusations the counterpart didn't hold.** If you label a worry the counterpart hadn't thought of, you've *planted* it. They now believe you suspect them of holding a grievance they didn't actually have, and the conversation goes to a worse place than it would have without the audit. Stick to accusations the counterpart almost certainly already holds.

2. **Using the audit to inoculate against true grievances.** "You're going to think I cheated you on the price â€” but here are the reasons it's fair." This uses the audit's disarming power to skate past a real wrongdoing. The counterpart's defenses drop, you escape accountability, and the user wins by manipulation. The honest version names the accusation AND substantively addresses whether it's true.

The diagnostic gate:
- **Truth.** Is the accusation you're labeling one the counterpart actually holds (or almost certainly would)?
- **Substance.** After you label the accusation, are you genuinely addressing whether it's correct? Or using the label to dodge?
- **Beneficiary.** When the counterpart's defenses drop, are you using the opening to find a workable deal â€” or to extract a concession they'd refuse if they could think clearly?

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Confirm a difficult conversation is coming.** Name the conversation, the counterpart, the stakes, and what you suspect they're walking in expecting from you.

2. **Generate the accusation list.** Brainstorm 5-10 candidate accusations the counterpart could level. Be uncharitable to yourself; this is *their* possible view, not the truth.

3. **Identify the 2-3 worst.** Most damaging Ã— most likely to be held. These are the ones to label out loud.

4. **Compose the labels.** Use the third-person template ("You may believe..." / "You're going to think..." / "It probably seems like..."). The labels should be specific enough that the counterpart recognizes their own view, not generic enough that they could apply to anyone.

5. **Rehearse the opening.** Speak it out loud, ideally to a partner. Get the tone (calm, slow, FM DJ voice). Get the pacing (label, pause, next label, pause, third label). If you can't rehearse with a partner, rehearse in front of a mirror.

6. **Design the pivot.** After the third label lands, what's the move? A calibrated question that hands the problem-solving to the counterpart? An invitation to co-author the solution? Decide and script it.

7. **Run the manipulation-risk gate.**
   - Are the accusations real ones the counterpart holds?
   - Are you substantively addressing whether they're correct, or dodging?
   - When their defenses drop, are you going to find a workable deal or extract advantage?

8. **Deliver the opening as rehearsed.** Calm, slow, paused. Don't add explanation. Don't apologize. Don't defend. Just label and pause.

9. **Read the response.** Nod? Softened body language? Verbal confirmation? Move to the pivot. Escalation or hostility? Drop into real-time labeling.

10. **Produce the Accusation Audit document** (the artifact):
    - Conversation context (counterpart, stakes, expected baseline)
    - Full accusation list (5-10 items)
    - Selected 2-3 with rationale
    - Rehearsed opening script (verbatim)
    - Pivot move
    - Manipulation-risk audit
    - Fallback: what to do if it doesn't land

## Common failure modes

- **Inventing accusations the counterpart didn't hold.** You think they're going to accuse you of X; they hadn't thought of X; you raise X; now they're thinking about X. Stick to accusations you have evidence for or that are almost certain given the situation.

- **Listing too many accusations.** Naming 7 accusations in your opening reads as guilt-tripping or self-flagellation. The counterpart starts to wonder what else you're hiding. 2-3 is the cap.

- **Tone problems.** Sarcasm ("Oh, you'll *definitely* think we're predatory"), false humility ("I just want to acknowledge that we might come across..."), or rapid-fire delivery all kill the move. The opening must sound like genuine, considered, calm acknowledgment.

- **Failing to rehearse.** First-time delivery of an accusation audit, without rehearsal, almost always comes out wooden or wrong. Voss is explicit (lines 2196-2207): role-play with a partner. The accusation audit is performance; performance requires practice.

- **No pivot.** Naming the accusations and then awkwardly waiting leaves the conversation in negative space. Have the pivot scripted: a calibrated question, an invitation to co-author, a summary of mutual interest.

- **Using the audit to dodge accountability.** "You may think I cheated you â€” but actually here are 12 reasons it's fair." This weaponizes the disarming power of the audit to escape responsibility for an actual wrongdoing. The honest version: label the accusation AND substantively address whether it's correct. If you did wrong, acknowledge it AFTER the label.

- **Stage-1 audit without Stage-2 execution.** Some users write the audit, find it cathartic, and then walk into the conversation winging it. The audit only works if you actually deliver the accusations in the opening. Rehearse and execute.

- **Audit pitched too high or too low.** Pitched too high (inflating minor concerns into outrage levels) sounds dishonest. Pitched too low (downplaying real grievances) leaves the accusations un-disarmed. Match the audit's intensity to the actual likely intensity of the counterpart's grievance.

- **Skipping the audit for short / casual conversations.** The audit is for *prepared* conversations. Don't run it for a 30-second hallway interaction. For high-stakes 30+-minute conversations, it's load-bearing.

- **Skipping the manipulation-risk gate.** The audit's power can be used to manipulate. The gate is: are the accusations real, are you substantively addressing them, and is the beneficiary genuinely shared?
