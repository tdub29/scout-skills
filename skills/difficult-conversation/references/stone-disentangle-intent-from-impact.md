# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” Disentangle Intent from Impact

## The framework

> "Don't Assume They Meant It: Disentangle Intent from Impact" (Ch 3)

Two truths about intent and impact:
1. **We judge OURSELVES by our intent** (I meant well, so I'm not at fault).
2. **We judge OTHERS by their impact on us** (it hurt, so they meant to hurt me).

Both moves are convenient and wrong. The framework forces the split.

### The Two Mistakes

> "Mistake #1: We notice the impact of their action on us, but we don't notice the impact of our action on them. Mistake #2: We assume we know their intent... we assume they mean us harm or are careless or selfish." (Ch 3, lines 2080â€“2140)

Mistake 1: asymmetry. You feel your hurt; you don't feel theirs.
Mistake 2: inference. You're inferring intent from impact, which gets the inference wrong roughly half the time.

### The three-question framework

> Ask these three questions, in order, before reacting (lines 2152â€“2156):

**1. Actions** â€” "What did the other person actually say or do?"
- Specific, observable behavior. Not "they were dismissive"; "they looked at their phone while I was speaking."

**2. Impact** â€” "What was the impact of this on me?"
- The legitimate, real, felt impact. Yours to name; not negotiable.

**3. Assumption** â€” "Based on this impact, what assumption am I making about what the other person intended?"
- The inference you're drawing about WHY they did what they did. Usually unconscious; usually wrong.

The split lets you express the IMPACT (which is real) while keeping the INTENT question OPEN (which is honest).

### The conversation moves that follow

**Expressing impact correctly:**
- "When you looked at your phone, I felt like what I was saying wasn't important. I don't know what you intended, but that was the impact."

**Receiving accusations of bad intent:**
- "I hear that the impact was hurtful. That wasn't what I intended â€” and I want to take seriously that the impact happened, even though my intent was different."

Crucial: validating impact â‰  admitting intent. You can fully acknowledge what they felt without confessing to motives you didn't have.

## How to apply

When you feel hurt and the words forming in your head are "they did that to me on purpose":

1. **Pause.** Don't act on the intent-assumption yet.

2. **Run the three questions on paper or silently:**
   - What did they actually DO? (Observable behavior.)
   - What was the IMPACT? (Your felt experience.)
   - What ASSUMPTION am I making about their intent? (The inference.)

3. **Name the assumption to yourself.** "I'm assuming they did it to hurt me." That naming alone often loosens the certainty.

4. **In the conversation: express impact, KEEP intent open.**
   - "When you X, the impact on me was Y. I'd really like to understand what was going on for you."
   - NOT: "You did X to hurt me."

5. **If receiving an intent accusation:**
   - Acknowledge impact: "I hear that landed badly."
   - Clarify intent: "What I was actually intending was..."
   - Hold both: "Even though my intent was different, the impact was real, and I'm sorry for that."

## Common failure modes

- **Smuggling intent back into impact statement.** "When you intentionally ignored me..." â€” that's the assumption sneaking in. Strip it.
- **Believing impact = intent.** "But it really HURT" â€” yes, AND that doesn't tell you why they did it.
- **Using the framework to escape consequences.** "I didn't INTEND to hurt them, so they shouldn't be upset." Intent doesn't cancel impact. Apologize for the impact even when the intent was clean.
- **Expressing impact as accusation.** "The impact was that you made me feel stupid" â€” that's a story about them, not a felt impact. "I felt embarrassed" is the impact.
- **Refusing to consider the impact when accused.** Defending intent so hard that the actual hurt never gets acknowledged. They're not asking you to confess to bad intent; they're asking you to see the impact.

## Integrity check

This framework's misuse pattern: using "intent vs. impact" to either escape accountability or weaponize impact. Three guardrails:

- **Impact is real even when intent was clean.** "I didn't mean to" doesn't undo the harm. Apologize for impact regardless of intent.
- **Don't make intent unknowable as a shield.** "Who can really know what I intended?" used to dodge reflection. Often intent is at least partly knowable â€” including the parts of it that weren't pure.
- **Stay open to mixed intent.** Most actions don't have ONE intent. You might have BOTH wanted to give honest feedback AND wanted to make someone feel small. Both can be true.
