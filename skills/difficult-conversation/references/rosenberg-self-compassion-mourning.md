# Rosenberg â€” NVC Mourning + Self-Forgiveness

## The framework

A two-step self-empathy procedure for when you've done something you regret. Rosenberg distinguishes "mourning" (feeling the unmet need underneath self-judgment) from "self-forgiveness" (empathizing with the part of you that made the choice).

> "NVC Mourning" (Ch 9, line 5322). "Self-Forgiveness" (Ch 9, line 5366).

### Step 1: NVC Mourning

Most self-blame uses moralistic judgments turned inward: "I'm so stupid." "I always mess this up." "Look, you just did it again!" These judgments are themselves obstacles â€” they shut down learning and produce shame, not change.

Mourning is the move from self-judgment to feeling the unmet need underneath.

> "If we find ourselves reacting reproachfully to something we did ('Look, you just messed up again!'), we can quickly stop and ask ourselves, 'What unmet need of mine is being expressed through this moralistic judgment?'" (Ch 9, lines 5329â€“5332)

The mourning process:

1. **Recognize the self-judgment.** "I'm such a fool." "What an idiot move."
2. **Stop the inner attack and ask:** "What unmet need of mine is being expressed in this self-criticism?"
3. **Connect to that need.** Often it's a need for competence, contribution, integrity, care for someone you let down.
4. **Feel the feelings that arise from the unmet need.** Sadness, regret, frustration, grief. (Not shame â€” shame stays in judgment; mourning moves to feeling.)

> "Mourning in NVC is the process of fully connecting with the unmet needs and the feelings that are generated when we have been less than perfect." (line 5349)

### Step 2: Self-Forgiveness

Mourning addresses the need that was violated. Self-forgiveness addresses the choice that violated it. Both are needed.

> "Turning our attention to the part of the self which chose to act in the way that led to the present situation, we ask ourselves, 'When I behaved in the way which I now regret, what need of mine was I trying to meet?'" (Ch 9, lines 5368â€“5370)

The self-forgiveness process:

1. **Look at the choice you made â€” without judgment.** Not "why did I do something so stupid" but "what was I needing in that moment?"
2. **Find the need.** Often: rest, safety, relief, connection, recognition, autonomy. Even bad choices serve real needs (ineffectively).
3. **Hold both selves.** The self that regrets, AND the self that took the action.

> "An important aspect of self-compassion is to be able to empathically hold both parts of ourselves â€” the self that regrets a past action and the self that took the action in the first place." (lines 5379â€“5381)

> "When we listen empathically to ourselves, we will be able to hear the underlying need. Self-forgiveness occurs the moment this empathic connection is made." (line 5374)

### Why this beats self-blame

Self-blame ("I'm so stupid") generates shame, paralysis, and repetition. Mourning + self-forgiveness generates feeling, learning, and capacity for different choices. Same regret, different metabolism.

## How to apply

When self-criticism is loud after a mistake:

1. **Catch the self-judgment in flight.** "What an idiot." "I never learn." "I'm a terrible parent."

2. **Mourning move:** Ask, "What unmet need of mine is this judgment pointing to?" Maybe a need for integrity, for being a good partner, for competence at work. Feel the sadness or regret of that need not being met. Stay there until it lands.

3. **Self-forgiveness move:** Ask of the SELF who made the choice, "What need was I trying to meet?" Maybe relief from stress, avoiding conflict, getting some rest, protecting yourself. The need is real even if the choice was poor.

4. **Hold both.** "I was needing X (the unmet need from the regret), and the choice I made was trying to meet Y (the need served by the action). Both are real."

5. **Now you can act differently â€” or repair.** From the place of both-truths-held, you can apologize, change behavior, or choose differently next time. From shame, you mostly just repeat.

## Common failure modes

- **Going straight to self-forgiveness without mourning.** "It's fine, I had reasons." Skips the genuine grief of what wasn't met. The other person (if there is one) feels excused-away.
- **Going straight to mourning without self-forgiveness.** Drowning in the regret without examining the choice. Repeat-prone.
- **Self-forgiveness as self-justification.** "I was just tired" doesn't honor the impact. The need IS real AND the impact IS real. Both stay present.
- **Skipping repair.** Self-compassion is for you. If you harmed someone, the repair work with them is separate and necessary. Don't substitute one for the other.
- **Performing self-compassion.** Saying the words ("I was meeting a need") without actually softening internally. The body knows.

## Compassion check (manipulation-risk)

Self-compassion has a dark twin: using NVC language to excuse oneself from accountability. Three guardrails:

- **Self-forgiveness does NOT erase impact.** "I was meeting a need" is not "therefore I owe nothing." It's "therefore I can look at this clearly and choose to repair."
- **Don't perform mourning to extract empathy from others.** "I feel so bad about it" as a way to flip the comfort-flow can re-victimize the person you harmed.
- **The two moves work together, not as alternatives.** Pick-one is a corruption: skip mourning, you fail to learn; skip self-forgiveness, you stay stuck.
- **For serious harm, this is not sufficient.** Self-compassion is one piece. Repair, restitution, accountability with the affected person are separate work.
