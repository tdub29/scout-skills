# Stone/Patton/Heen â€” Begin from the Third Story

## The framework

> "Getting Started: Begin from the Third Story" (Ch 8)

The opening move makes or breaks a difficult conversation. Most people open with their own story ("here's what happened, here's what you did, here's why I'm upset"). The other person hears accusation in sentence one and defends through the rest. The Third Story is the alternative.

### The three possible openings

**1. Your story** (DON'T)

"I want to talk to you about what happened on the project. Here's what I observed â€” you missed deadlines, you didn't communicate, you made unilateral decisions. I'm frustrated."

What the listener hears: accusation. They start drafting their defense before you finish.

**2. Their story** (DON'T)

"I think I know what you were trying to do â€” you were probably stressed and trying to make it work, but..."

What the listener hears: presumption. You've claimed to know their mind, usually wrongly. They're already correcting you.

**3. The Third Story** (DO)

> Frame as joint inquiry â€” what an impartial observer would say. (Ch 8)

"I'd like to talk about how the project went. I have my view of what happened, you probably have a different view, and I'm sure I'm missing some of what you saw. Can we walk through both?"

What the listener hears: invitation to a joint problem.

### The four sub-rules

1. **Don't open with your story** â€” puts them in defensive receiving mode.
2. **Don't open with their story** â€” claiming to know their view triggers correction.
3. **Open with the Third Story** â€” frame the disagreement itself as the topic, with both views equally legitimate.
4. **Invite them to share first** â€” listening before speaking signals genuine curiosity. They feel heard; their walls come down; they're more open to your view.

### What the Third Story sounds like

Templates:

- "I'd like to understand how X happened from your perspective and share mine, and figure out where we go from here."
- "I think we've seen this situation differently, and I want to understand the differences."
- "I'm sure I'm missing pieces of what you observed. Can we compare notes?"
- "I'd like to talk about [neutral framing of the topic] â€” we may have very different views and I'd like to hear yours first."

The shared feature: neither story is treated as ground truth. Both are part of the inquiry.

### What it requires

- **Genuine curiosity.** The Third Story collapses instantly if your "I want to understand" is theater.
- **Actually inviting them first.** Asking and then immediately giving your view defeats it.
- **Tolerating their version even if it stings.** They'll say things you disagree with. Listen before correcting.

## How to apply

Before opening:

1. **Write your "natural" opener.** Notice if it's your story ("here's what you did") or their story ("you were probably trying to...").
2. **Draft a Third Story opener.** Frame the situation itself, not either party's reading of it.
3. **Make the framing genuinely neutral.** "We had an issue with deliverables" not "you missed deadlines." "I want to understand what was at play" not "I want to understand why you did this."
4. **Plan to invite them first.** What's your opening question after the framing?

In the conversation:

1. **Open with the Third Story.** Brief, neutral, inviting.
2. **Pause.** Give them space.
3. **Invite their view first.** "Can you tell me how you saw this unfolding?"
4. **Listen fully before sharing yours.** This is the trust-builder.
5. **Then share yours**, using [[stone-expressing-with-clarity]].

## Common failure modes

- **Fake Third Story.** "I'd love to hear your perspective on why you screwed this up." The framing isn't neutral if the content is loaded.
- **Third Story followed by immediate own-story.** "Let me hear your view on this... actually, let me just explain what happened first..." Defeats the move.
- **Asking and not listening.** "Tell me your view" while internally rehearsing your rebuttal. They feel it.
- **Refusing to acknowledge anything they say.** They share their view; you respond with "OK but here's what really happened." Erases their share; reverts to your story.
- **Third Story as evasion.** Used to avoid raising a real concern. "Can we talk about how the project went?" when what you actually need to say is "I'm worried this can't continue if X doesn't change." The Third Story is the opener, not a permanent dodge.

## Integrity check

The Third Story can be deployed as faux-neutrality â€” sounding open while having decided the verdict. Three guardrails:

- **Real curiosity, not strategic.** If you've already decided the conclusion, the Third Story is theater. Either get actually curious or skip the move and just be direct.
- **Acknowledge what you actually need to say.** Sometimes you have a clear position. Open with Third Story to invite their view, but be honest that you also have a view you'll share.
- **Don't use it to dodge accountability.** If you've done something wrong, open with the impact and your part â€” not a neutral "let's understand both sides." Third Story is for genuine two-sided situations.
