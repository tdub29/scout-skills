# Hamming: Work on Important Problems

## When to use

Reach for this skill whenever someone is deciding where to point sustained effort, not just how to execute what is already decided. Concrete cues:

- They are picking a research topic, thesis, next project, or startup idea, and have narrowed the question to "what would actually matter" versus "what is tractable this quarter."
- They describe feeling busy, credentialed, and productive, but privately suspect none of it will matter in five years.
- They are auditing a backlog, roadmap, or grant portfolio and want to know if it is stuffed with safe, incremental, forgettable line items.
- They just got a new tool, dataset, hire, paper, or piece of infrastructure and are asking whether it changes what they should be working on.
- They are new to a field, team, or company and want the real map of what matters, not the official one.
- They are comparing two problems of similar prestige and cannot tell which one is worth a multi-year bet.
- A long-shelved problem just got a plausible opening, and they are torn between finishing what is on their plate and switching immediately.
- They keep producing solid, well-received work and still cannot name a single problem it moved forward.
- A mentor, manager, or peer just asked them "why are you working on that" and they realized they do not have a real answer.

Do not trigger for questions about building general skill or reputation before you are allowed to pick your own work. That is newport-so-good's career-capital lane, and it answers a different question ("how do I become good enough to deserve the choice") than this skill does ("given that you have the choice, what should you choose").

Do not trigger for scoring a list of already-chosen initiatives by impact and effort, the ICE/RICE family. Those frameworks rank candidates by expected value or value-over-effort; they never ask whether a line of attack exists at all, which is exactly the filter Hamming adds and the reason a high-value, attack-less problem is excluded here even though it would score well on a generic prioritization matrix.

Do not trigger for pure probability-calibration questions about an external event (how likely is X to happen), that belongs to tetlock-superforecasting. This skill answers a prior question, which problem is worth committing effort to, not how confident to be in any single forecast about it.

## The framework

Hamming lays out one mechanism with five moving parts: a thesis about why most people misallocate effort, a self-audit question that surfaces the misallocation, a standing list that holds the candidate problems, an attackability filter that decides which candidates are live, and an interrupt rule that fires when a candidate goes live. Apply all five together; running the audit without the list, or keeping a list without ever re-scoring attackability, breaks the mechanism.

### Core thesis

Hamming's claim, made bluntly to a table of Bell Labs chemists, is that most competent people spend most of their time on work they privately know is not important and unlikely to lead anywhere important. This is not a competence problem, it is a selection problem.

> "If you do not work on important problems how can you expect to do important work?"

Doing significant work requires deliberately auditing whether your effort is aimed at what actually matters, on a schedule, out loud, not just intending to someday. Hamming's second, sharper claim is that importance is not a single scale of stakes. A problem is important only when it combines (1) real value if solved and (2) a visible, current line of attack.

> "a problem is important partly because there is a possible attack on it, and not because of its inherent importance"

Value without an attack is not yet an important problem in Hamming's sense, it is just a wish, and the two must be evaluated together, not value alone.

### The self-audit question

Hamming forced this by walking up to a lunch table and asking the working scientists what the important problems in their field were, then what they personally were working on, then the question that emptied the table:

> "If what you are working on is not important and not likely to lead to important things, then why are you working on it?"

The one chemist who took it seriously spent a whole summer thinking it through, did not change his research, and still called it worth the effort. Within years he ran his group; about a decade later he made the National Academy of Engineering. Nobody else at that table was ever heard from again. The mechanism is not "think about it once."

Run the self-audit as a recurring practice, not a one-time epiphany:

- Ask it of yourself on a fixed cadence (weekly review, monthly planning, quarterly roadmap), not only when you happen to feel stuck.
- Compare your actual current task list against your own standing list of important problems (next sub-concept), item by item, not impressionistically.
- Accept an honest, unflattering answer ("this pays the bills," "this was assigned to me") as valid; the point is naming the reason, not always changing course immediately.

If you cannot answer the question at all, that inability is itself the finding, and it is the strongest signal that a work-selection audit is overdue.

### The standing list of 10 to 20 problems

Most people who do significant work carry a standing list, roughly 10 to 20 items, of the basic, important problems in their field that they personally do not yet know how to solve.

> "Most great people also have 10 to 20 problems they regard as basic and of great importance... When a clue does appear they generally drop other things and get to work immediately."

The list is kept in mind, or written down, specifically so that when a clue shows up it can be recognized as a clue instead of passing by as trivia. What belongs on it:

- Problems specific to your actual field or project domain, described in that field's own vocabulary, not generic world problems like "cure disease" or "solve poverty."
- Problems sized so the full list fits in working memory; if you cannot recall most of the list without looking it up, it is too long or too vague to function as a filter.
- Problems you personally do not currently know how to solve; a list of things you already know how to do is a task list, not a standing list.
- A mix of near-term and long-shot items; a list that is entirely safe bets is not aimed at importance, and a list that is entirely long shots gives you nothing to act on this year.

### Attackability ("ripeness")

Importance is not inherent value, it is value multiplied by the existence of a possible line of attack right now.

> "The three problems in Physics, antigravity, teleportation, and time travel are seldom worked on because we have so few clues as to how to start."

A problem earns a place on the working part of the standing list only when someone, even if not you yet, could point to a plausible first move. Practically, for every candidate problem, ask what the first experiment, prototype, or proof attempt would even look like:

- If you can name a concrete first move, even a weak or partial one, it belongs on the active, attackable part of the list.
- If the honest answer is "I have no idea, not even a bad one," it belongs on a separate, dormant list of merely-valuable problems, watched but not actively worked.
- Re-test this classification regularly; conditions change, and a dormant problem can become attackable the moment a new tool, dataset, or result appears.
- Resist scoring attackability by prestige or funding signals; a well-funded, high-visibility problem with no visible first move is still dormant in Hamming's sense.

### The clue-interrupt rule

When a genuine clue appears against one of the items on your standing list, a new technique, a paper, a tool, a hire, a dataset, a conversation, Hamming's observation is that the people who had already primed the problem drop what they are doing and get to work immediately.

> "When a clue does appear they generally drop other things and get to work immediately on the important problem. Therefore they tend to come in first, and the others who come in later are soon forgotten."

The rule is narrow, it is not "context-switch for anything interesting":

- The clue must land on a problem that is already, explicitly, on your written standing list, not a fresh idea you are excited about in the moment.
- The response is to interrupt current work in a concrete way (block real time this week to test the lead), not to add the clue to a someday list where priming decays.
- Priming, having genuinely thought about the problem for years, is what turns a stray fact into a recognized clue; without the standing list, the identical fact passes as noise to everyone else at the table.
- Expect to drop the interrupt after a bounded test, not to abandon current commitments indefinitely; the rule is "test immediately," not "pivot permanently on first contact."

### Courage as the enabling trait, not the mechanism

Hamming also describes the confidence needed to keep working an important problem through long, discouraging stretches with no success, and even names a private mantra he borrowed from Shannon ("I ain't scaird of nothing") for when he got stuck. Treat courage as what lets you act on this framework once you have done the work of building the list and judging attackability, not as a substitute for either. Willpower alone, applied to an unranked list of tractable-but-unimportant tasks, does not produce important results.

## How to apply

1. Write down, right now, the 10 to 20 problems you privately consider the biggest unsolved problems in your actual field or project domain, not generic world problems. Use the field's own vocabulary, not marketing language.
   - Example scope: for an ML engineer this might be "reliable long-horizon agent memory" or "cheap, faithful interpretability at scale," not "make AI safe."
   - If you cannot get past three or four items, that is itself worth noting; ask harder, or ask a peer what they would put on your list.

2. For each one, write one sentence naming a concrete line of attack that exists today, even a weak or partial one: a technique, a dataset, a collaborator, a recent result you could extend.

3. Tag each problem "attackable now" (a real first move exists), "valuable but dormant" (no visible attack yet, watch it, do not actively work it), or "already crowded" (attackable, but so well covered your marginal contribution is low).
   - Write the tag next to the problem, not just in your head; you will re-score it later and need to see what you originally thought.

4. Pull your actual current task list, backlog, or roadmap and mark, item by item, which of your important problems it serves, if any. Anything serving none of them is either necessary overhead or a candidate to cut.

5. Ask the self-audit question in writing: "If what I am working on this week is not one of my important problems, and not likely to lead to one, why am I working on it?" Name the honest answer instead of dressing it up as strategy.

6. Put a recurring date on the calendar, weekly or monthly, to re-read the list, re-score attackability, and add or retire items. Treat this the way Hamming treated his own Friday afternoons set aside for "great thoughts."

7. When something new crosses your desk, a paper, a tool, a hire, a dataset, a conversation, check it against the standing list by name before filing it away anywhere else.

8. If it is a genuine clue against an "attackable now" or "valuable but dormant" item, activate the interrupt rule: stop the current task, block real time this week to test the lead, and only fold it back into "later" if the test comes back empty.

9. Sanity-check the whole list against Hamming's test: for each item, can you explain to a smart outsider both why it matters and what the actual first move would be? If you can only do one of the two, the item is misfiled, either not important or not yet attackable, and belongs on the dormant list, not the working one.

Worked example of the mechanism, compressed from Hamming's own chemistry-table story: a scientist has a mental list that includes "why does this class of reactions plateau instead of completing." For years it sits tagged "valuable but dormant," no visible attack. A new instrument or a colleague's side result finally suggests a measurable variable nobody had isolated before. Because the problem was already named and already on the list, the new fact reads instantly as a clue rather than an interesting aside, and the interrupt rule fires the same week, not the next time the backlog happens to clear.

## Common failure modes

- Treating this as "dream big" inspiration and never producing the literal written list. The list is the artifact; a vague sense of wanting to matter more is not the same exercise and produces no decision.
- Ranking candidate problems by expected value alone. This is the ICE/RICE failure mode. Hamming's filter is value and a visible attack together; a huge, unattackable problem stays off the working list no matter how it scores on impact.
- Chasing fashionable "moonshot" language without an actual line of attack, mistaking prestige or buzz for attackability. If nobody, including you, can name a first move, it is a wish, not a working item.
- Letting the list grow past what you can hold in mind, or letting it go stale for years. A list of 40 problems you have not revisited since writing it is not a standing list, it is a museum piece.
- Interrupting current work for every merely interesting fact, not just genuine clues against the standing list. This burns the credibility of the interrupt rule and turns priming into plain distractibility.
- Confusing this with courage or grit as the active ingredient. Confidence and sustained effort are enabling traits, but the mechanism that produces important results here is problem selection plus the standing list, not willpower alone.
- Marking a problem "already crowded" and dropping it purely because competitors are present, instead of checking whether your specific angle, tooling, or data actually gives you a distinct line of attack the crowd lacks.

Sibling skills, same source:

- hamming-open-door: covers staying visible and accessible so you keep hearing the clues that feed the standing list in the first place, instead of optimizing pure throughput on the wrong problem.
- hamming-sell-your-work: covers what happens after you solve an important problem, since Hamming is explicit that good results do not sell themselves and must be presented deliberately.
- hamming-prepared-mind: covers priming yourself long before a clue arrives (Pasteur's "chance favors the prepared mind," which Hamming opens the chapter with), so you are equipped to recognize a clue as a clue when it crosses your standing list.

Use this skill first among the five. It decides what is worth working on; the others govern how to work on it once chosen, how to stay pointed at it, and how to make sure the result is not ignored once found.

Revisit the standing list whenever one of the sibling skills surfaces a change in circumstance, a solved ambiguity, a newly open door, or a well-received result, since any of those can shift a problem from dormant to attackable.
