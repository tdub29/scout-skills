# Inflammation evidence

> **Education, not medical advice.** This skill tells a coach what the evidence says about inflammation markers and lifestyle, and when to send someone to a clinician. It never diagnoses. A lab result is interpreted by the clinician who ordered it.

**Grades:** **Strong** = consistent RCT, meta-analysis or major guideline evidence. **Moderate** = real effect with smaller or mixed data. **Weak** = thin, conflicting or small effects. **Insufficient** = not enough reliable evidence. **Null/Avoid** distinguishes a null finding from a precautionary safety recommendation. Established definitions carry no treatment-efficacy grade. Tags: `PMID n`. **Own judgement** marks coaching inferences. **Screen:** retraction, concern, correction, funding and conflict checks are recorded in `references/sources.md`. If a rule depends only on industry-tied evidence, lower its grade one step unless independent evidence agrees or a stated direction-of-bias exception applies. Record funding and author ties separately; unknown funding is not clean.

## 0. Hard rails (check before any rule below)

1. **A coach never calls anyone "inflamed".** Acute inflammation is the normal response to infection and injury [PMID 18650913]; low-grade chronic inflammation is a risk concept read from markers that move with many things (section 2). The coach says "worth a doctor's look", nothing more.
2. **Defer elective cardiovascular hs-CRP baseline testing during acute illness or trauma.** CRP is made by liver cells under IL-6 and IL-1 control [PMID 38947332]; kinetics reflect the timing of the stimulus [PMID 36592205; authors declare drug/diagnostics-company ties]. The 2003 AHA/CDC protocol says to test a stable person, outside infection or trauma [PMID 12551878; mixed educational funding]. This is not a prohibition on clinically indicated acute-illness testing. Extra timing precautions after races or heavy drinking are own judgement (7.4, 8.6).
3. **An hs-CRP around or above 10 mg/L needs clinical assessment, not a diet score.** The 2003 statement uses greater than 10 mg/L, recommends looking for infection or inflammation and repeating after recovery, about 2 weeks later [PMID 12551878]. Treating exactly 10 the same way is conservative own judgement. The ordering clinician decides urgency and repeat timing.
4. **Bloating alone does not establish systemic inflammation.** AGA advises against probiotics to treat abdominal bloating and distention; consider celiac testing, with imaging or endoscopy reserved for alarm features, recent worsening or an abnormal exam [PMID 37452811]. New or worsening symptoms warrant clinical assessment (own judgement); the abstract does not list every alarm feature.
5. **Turmeric supplements can injure the liver.** DILIN reported 10 adjudicated cases, 8 women, 9 hepatocellular, typical latency 1 to 4 months, 5 hospitalizations, 1 death and a strong HLA-B*35:01 association; 3 of 7 tested products contained piperine [PMID 36252717]. This selected case series cannot estimate absolute risk for users, prove piperine caused the injuries, or quantify extra risk from absorption enhancers. It does not establish harm from culinary turmeric. **Own precautionary judgement:** stop the product and seek clinical assessment for suspected liver trouble; do not recommend enhanced-absorption turmeric for a nonspecific inflammation goal.
6. **High CRP with persistently long natural cycles is worth a doctor visit** (own judgement). In 414 women, CRP over 10 mg/L was associated with cycles of at least 35 days, aOR 3.7 [PMID 36244407; one author reports past consulting outside the work]. This is observational, not proof of cause. Use `female-nutrition-evidence` 2.1 and its referral list.
7. **Ask about hormonal contraception and hormone therapy before reading any hs-CRP** (3.4, 3.5). Estrogen and progestogen use is on the AHA/CDC list of things that raise it [PMID 12551878].

### 3.5 EE contraceptives and hs-CRP

Applies to combined hormonal contraceptives containing EE, for example levonorgestrel/EE pills, and the EE patch and ring.

**A. Do they raise hs-CRP? Yes (Strong).**
- RCT, 9 weeks: CRP rose by every route; pill 0.70 to 5.4 mg/L, patch 0.77 to 2.9, ring 0.98 to 3.7 [PMID 22811306].
- Crossover, n=24: CRP +292% on an EE/norgestimate pill, +220% on the patch [PMID 18238963]. Ring CRP ran 109% higher than a levonorgestrel/EE 150/30 pill [PMID 22982353].
- RCT, n=56, 9 weeks: EE plus dienogest raised hs-CRP 1.10 mg/L; estradiol valerate plus dienogest -0.06; dienogest alone +0.13 [PMID 32303765].
- 2,120 young adults: pill users 3.69 vs non-users 1.36 mg/L; 35.3% of users over 3; pill use was the largest single determinant in women (partial RÂ² 18.4%) [PMID 16238678]. Also 2.0 vs 0.9 mg/L [PMID 12850240].
- Athletes, n=205: 3 to under 10 mg/L in 26.4% of users vs 2.6% of non-users (OR 13.3); 1 or more in 62.3% vs 13.2% [PMID 27084393]. Olympic-level athletes: 2.02 vs 0.57 mg/L [PMID 32523546]. CRP stayed higher on both active-pill test days (n=8 vs 8) [PMID 37487629].
- Duration: in the Nurses' Health Studies ever vs never use did not differ, but CRP rose 9.9% per 5 years of use [PMID 38896053].

**B. Mechanism is unresolved: EE affects hepatic protein synthesis, while some studies also report other inflammatory-marker changes.** Moderate for observed biomarker discordance; Insufficient to quantify which mechanism predominates.

For a liver effect:
- Serum CRP is made by hepatocytes under IL-6 and IL-1Î² control; extrahepatic CRP does not add to serum levels [PMID 38947332].
- EE raises estrogen-sensitive liver proteins such as SHBG, CBG and TBG with patch and pill [PMID 16982228]. A proteomic RCT found changes in 121 proteins with EE versus 5 with estradiol valerate [PMID 36416543; two authors report contraceptive-company ties]; cross-sectional protein differences also occur [PMID 22984625].
- CRP rose without matching leukocyte or monocyte changes; the authors say the rise "does not correspond with the effects on other markers of inflammation" [PMID 22982353]. Cytokines and immune cells were unchanged in pill-using Olympians [PMID 32523546]; haptoglobin, another acute-phase protein, was unaffected [PMID 27084393].
- Patch and ring also raise CRP, so gastrointestinal first-pass exposure cannot be the whole explanation [PMID 22811306; PMID 18238963; PMID 22982353]. Comparisons across hormone products do not fully isolate route, molecule, dose or progestogen; the same-progestogen trial is more informative about estrogen formulation [PMID 32303765].

For some real inflammation:
- In a selected cross-sectional sample of 79 women, oral contraceptive users had higher sCD40L, and IL-6 and sTNF-RI tracked CRP among oral users. Vaginal users did not show the same other-marker pattern. Selection by CRP category limits generalization [PMID 25861161].
- NHANES, 496 users vs 1,583 non-users: other inflammation indices correlated with CRP more strongly in users; the authors read it as systemic inflammation [PMID 40198599].
- Pentraxin 3 rose with EE [PMID 22811306; PMID 32303765]; one trial's authors call the change chronic inflammation [PMID 22811306].

**Own judgement on B:** discordant marker changes support cautious interpretation, not a quantified conclusion that the effect is mostly hepatic or only slightly inflammatory. All serum CRP being made in the liver does not settle what stimulates it. **Risk meaning remains uncertain for hormone-related elevation:** the historical statement did not establish that an estrogen-associated CRP rise itself indicates greater cardiovascular risk [PMID 12551878]. This does not establish that contraception lacks cardiovascular or thrombotic risk; the prescriber assesses the whole risk profile.

## 5. Supplements

An exploratory registry check found completed studies with no readily linked results. Missing links are not proof of nonpublication, and broad query sets were not matched to trials in these meta-analyses. Provisional unpublished counts are withheld until a deduplicated trial-level ledger with matched completion-date denominators is available. Selective reporting is possible; magnitude and direction were not established. **Own methodological judgement.**

### 5.1 Omega-3 (Weak for a predictable marker benefit in a healthy individual)
- A mixed-population review contained 68 RCTs, n=4,601; its healthy subgroup had 16 CRP comparisons. Healthy-subgroup geometric-mean changes were CRP -16.5%, TNF -11.3%, and IL-6 -8.6%, the last not statistically significant [PMID 24505395]. These are marker changes, not established clinical benefits.
- An umbrella review across mixed health conditions found CRP effect size -0.40, I-squared 89.5%; it is not a healthy-adult-specific estimate [PMID 35914448].
- A VITAL biomarker subcohort, n=1,054, mean age 64.9, found no significant hs-CRP, IL-6, IL-10 or TNF benefit at years 2 or 4 after multiplicity adjustment. Dose: 1 g/day fish oil containing 840 mg EPA+DHA. This does not establish effects in young adults or at other doses; the trial had NIH support plus donated study products [PMID 36558465].
- **Own judgement:** do not recommend fish oil solely to lower CRP. Mixed findings and population/dose differences limit prediction for a healthy individual. This does not change separate triglyceride indications or food-based fish guidance.

### 5.2 Curcumin and turmeric (Weak for recommending it to healthy adults for nonspecific inflammation; precautionary Avoid for absorption-enhanced products)
- A 66-RCT review found CRP -0.58 mg/L, TNF -3.48 pg/mL and IL-6 -1.31 pg/mL; IL-1beta was nonsignificant [PMID 36804260]. A separate 103-RCT systematic review rated CRP evidence high but noted trial-method limitations [PMID 39478418]. The reviews may overlap and do not establish clinical benefit in healthy adults. A 13-RCT metabolic-syndrome review found CRP and TNF reductions but no significant hsCRP or IL-6 reduction; CRP and hsCRP each used only two studies [PMID 37522129].
- A pharmacokinetic experiment used 2 g curcumin with 20 mg piperine and reported a 2000% bioavailability increase [PMID 9619120]. Higher exposure is not proof of health benefit. Turmeric-associated liver injury is documented, but piperine-specific excess risk is unquantified [PMID 36252717]. **Own precautionary judgement:** do not recommend piperine or another absorption enhancer merely to improve an inflammation marker (rail 0.5).

### 5.3 Probiotics and synbiotics (Insufficient for a generic anti-inflammatory recommendation)
- A mixed probiotic/synbiotic review of 167 publications reported lower CRP and TNF in healthy subgroups [PMID 31060892].
- A healthy-adult synbiotic review contained 26 studies, n=1,319 overall; individual biomarker pools were smaller. Its primary pooled CRP, IL-6 and endotoxin findings were null [PMID 38341803]. Different products and trials prevent treating these reviews as direct interchangeable replications.
- Effects are strain-specific (`gut-health.md`, `supplements.md`); no probiotic for bloating (rail 0.4).

### 5.4 Vitamin D (Weak)
- 10 trials (n=924): hs-CRP -1.08 mg/L, -2.21 when baseline was 5 or more; heterogeneous; Begg 0.79, Egger 0.75 [PMID 24918698; part-funded by a vitamin maker's research fund].
- VITAL, 2,000 IU/day vitamin D: hs-CRP was 19% lower at year 2, not at year 4; other markers were unchanged. This large NIH-supported trial also received donated study products, so it is not wholly independent of product manufacturers. The specific vitamin-D CRP claim remains Weak for inconsistency plus mixed support [PMID 36558465].
- On hormonal contraception the vitamin D and CRP link is confounded (3.5.6). Dosing: `female-nutrition-evidence` 6.5.

### 5.5 Antioxidant megadoses around training (precautionary Avoid; own judgement)
- Vitamin C 1,000 mg plus E 235 mg for 11 weeks (n=54): VO2max rose the same (8% vs 8%) but mitochondrial adaptation markers were blunted [PMID 24492839].
- Vitamin C 1,000 mg plus E 400 IU for 4 weeks (39 men): insulin sensitivity improved only without the antioxidants [PMID 19433800].

**Own judgement on 5.5:** do not routinely add these high-dose C/E combinations to suppress exercise signals. These trials do not establish harm from ordinary food antioxidants or medically indicated deficiency treatment.
