# Newport: Career Capital Theory (craftsman mindset vs. passion mindset)

**Decision it drives:** *What should I actually build, and should I be reassured or alarmed that I don't feel "passionate"?* Produces a craftsman-mindset diagnosis + a "what capital am I building" audit + a go/stay/leave call on whether the current job can compound capital.

## When to use

Trigger when the user is doing any of these:

- Asking "should I follow my passion?", "is this my calling?", "what if I'm not passionate about this?"
- Choosing what to work on / what skill to invest in to advance a career.
- Feeling chronically dissatisfied at an entry-level or mid-level job and reading that as "wrong job."
- Tempted to quit a high-capital track to "start fresh" doing something they love (a yoga studio, a passive-income blog, a from-scratch pivot).
- Evaluating an offer/role on "will I love it" rather than "will it make me rare and valuable."

Don't trigger for the *autonomy* decision (use `the-control-traps-and-financial-viability`) or the *mission* decision (use `mission-little-bets-remarkable`). This reference is the foundation both of those sit on: capital first.

## The framework

### Core thesis
The traits that make a great job great â€” **creativity, impact, control** â€” are rare and valuable. Supply-and-demand says that to get rare and valuable things you must offer something rare and valuable in return. Newport calls that currency **career capital**: rare and valuable skills. So the order of operations is *get good first, then cash in the capital for the traits you want.* "Follow your passion" inverts this â€” it tells you to demand great traits before you have anything to trade. Passion is real but it's a **side effect** of mastery (Self-Determination Theory: motivation comes from competence + autonomy + relatedness, none of which is "match work to a pre-existing passion"), not a prerequisite you discover by introspection.

### Craftsman mindset vs. passion mindset
- **Passion mindset:** "What can the world offer *me*?" Makes you hyperaware of everything you dislike, traps you in unanswerable questions ("is this who I really am?"), and is most corrosive early-career when the work is *supposed* to be unglamorous.
- **Craftsman mindset:** "What can *I* offer the world?" Output-centric. "The tape doesn't lie" â€” focus on becoming so good they can't ignore you. This is the mindset that *acquires capital*, which is why it beats the passion mindset on pragmatic grounds, not philosophical ones.

### The argument-from-pre-existing-passion is backwards
The objection "craftsman mindset only works if you already love it" gets causality wrong. Performers like Tice and Martin weren't certain of their calling â€” Martin had anxiety attacks for a decade. They adopted the craftsman mindset because *it's what works*, and passion followed. You adopt the mindset first; passion is downstream.

### Three disqualifiers â€” when the craftsman mindset can't save a job
Career capital theory is agnostic to *field*, but not unconditional. Leave a job if it has any of:
1. **Few chances to develop rare & valuable skill** (you can't build capital here).
2. **It's useless or actively bad for the world** (you won't last long enough to build capital).
3. **It forces you to work with people you genuinely dislike** (same).
Disqualifier #1 kills skill growth; #2 and #3 kill your ability to *stay* and compound. Note none of these is "it doesn't match my passion."

### Capital markets: winner-take-all vs. auction
- **Winner-take-all:** one type of capital matters (TV writing = script quality; advice-blogging = compelling posts). Don't diversify; dominate the one thing.
- **Auction:** many capital types stack into a unique portfolio (cleantech VC). Pursue **open gates** â€” capital-building opportunities already open to you, because a moving freight train is easy to redirect but brutal to start from scratch.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Name the trait you actually want** (creativity / impact / control / money / flexibility). This is what capital buys; it is not free.
2. **Audit current capital.** What rare & valuable skill do you have *right now* that someone would pay a premium for? Be honest â€” "enthusiasm" and "courage" are not capital.
3. **Run the disqualifier test** on the current role. Any of the three present? If #1 (no skill growth), or #2/#3 are severe, the craftsman mindset cannot rescue it â€” flag a leave.
4. **Classify the capital market** you're competing in: winner-take-all or auction. State the consequence (dominate one skill vs. stack open gates).
5. **Pick the next capital to build** â€” the specific skill, named concretely. In an auction market, prefer an open gate over a from-scratch pivot.
6. **Reframe the passion question.** If the user is anxious they "don't feel passionate," restate: passion is a lagging indicator; the question to answer instead is "what's my plan to get so good they can't ignore me?"
7. **Sanity-check:** would this move *increase* the user's store of rare & valuable skill, or just relocate them to a place they like the vibe of? If the latter, it's the passion mindset wearing a disguise.

## Common failure modes

- **Mistaking a vibe for capital.** Liking a field, having courage, or having enthusiasm is not rare or valuable. Feuer traded years of marketing capital for a one-month yoga cert and ended up on food stamps. Don't reset to zero.
- **Treating dissatisfaction as a "wrong job" signal.** Early-career grind is supposed to feel unglamorous; the passion mindset misreads this as evidence you haven't found your calling.
- **Diversifying in a winner-take-all market.** Optimizing bounce rate / SEO / posting cadence when the only capital that matters is whether the writing compels. Find the one thing and dominate it.
- **Starting the freight train from scratch.** Ignoring open gates to begin a brand-new field with no leg up. Slower capital acquisition, higher failure rate.
- **Waiting to feel passionate before committing.** Passion follows mastery; if you wait for certainty you'll job-hop forever.

## Apply to Trevor's search

- **You are 23, Sr. Data Scientist, deliberately pivoting up.** Your `strategy/leveling-framework.md` already runs on this engine: career capital = the time-gated moats; AI commoditizes the *artifacts* (a dashboard, a model, a query) but not the *skill* to know what to build or the *reputation* that you reliably ship it. Frame every "what should I build?" question as "which rare-&-valuable skill compounds my moat?" not "which project sounds fun."
- **Market type:** Senior DS roles are mostly **auction** markets (stats + domain + comms + systems + judgment stack into a unique portfolio), with winner-take-all *sub-skills* (e.g., one signature capability you're known for). Decide deliberately: are you stacking open gates, or trying to be the single best at one thing? Don't accidentally run a winner-take-all play in an auction market or vice versa.
- **Disqualifier test on the current role before applying out:** Is skill growth still happening (#1)? If your capital acquisition has plateaued â€” the most common reason to leave even a comfortable role â€” that's the legitimate trigger to move, not "I'd be more passionate elsewhere."
- **Open gates over from-scratch:** when comparing a higher-comp role, weight the gate it opens (new domain, new scale of data, ownership) over the salary delta. The compounding capital is worth more at 23 than the marginal cash.
- **Anti-Feuer guardrail:** any move that *discards* accumulated DS capital to start over in an adjacent-but-unrelated field is a red flag â€” interrogate it hard.
