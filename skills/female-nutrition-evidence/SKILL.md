# Female nutrition evidence

> **Education, not medical advice.** This skill tells a coach what the evidence says and when to send someone to a clinician. It never diagnoses. Pregnancy dosing goes to the obstetric clinician; this skill stops at preconception.

**Grades:** **Strong** = consistent RCT, meta-analysis or major guideline evidence. **Moderate** = real effect, smaller or mixed data. **Weak** = thin, conflicting or tiny effect. **Insufficient** = not enough good data. **Null/Avoid** = no benefit or a safety concern. Tags: `PMID n` or `DOI`; `[ODS x]` and `[FDA fish]` are US government fact sheets (no PMID), listed with URLs in `references/sources.md`. **Own judgement** marks a rule that is mine, built on the cited facts.

## 0. Hard rails (check before any rule below)

1. **Never name a condition.** No single test diagnoses REDs, and a physician makes the call [PMID 37752002]. Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is a diagnosis of exclusion: pregnancy, thyroid disease, high prolactin, primary ovarian insufficiency, PCOS and medications must be ruled out first [PMID 41474492; PMID 28368518]. The coach says "this pattern is worth a doctor's look", nothing more.
2. **Disordered eating overrides every goal here.** Drive for thinness and dietary restraint track low RMR and low T3; compulsive exercise, time-restricted eating and planned weight cuts all count as routes into energy deficiency [PMID 41474492]. An overfocus on being lighter or leaner raises REDs and disordered eating risk, and body composition data is confidential medical information [PMID 37752006]. Any red flag: stop the plan and refer.
3. **Care is multidisciplinary** (medical, dietary, mental health) [PMID 28368518; PMID 24463911]. The coach is the dietary leg only.

## 1. Energy availability (EA)

**Formula** [PMID 33832385; PMID 17909417]:

```
EA = (energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure) / fat-free mass   [kcal/kg FFM/day]
Example: (2,000 kcal minus 500 kcal exercise) / 45 kg FFM = 33 kcal/kg FFM/day
```

Exercise energy expenditure is the cost of the training session only, not the whole day.

| # | Rule | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The classic anchors: LH pulsatility was unaffected at 30 and disrupted below 30 kcal/kg lean mass/day in 4 to 5 day lab trials; T3 fell between 19 and 25. These were short, tightly controlled studies in sedentary or untrained women. | Moderate | PMID 12519869; PMID 8160876; PMID 17909417 |
| 1.2 | **Do not treat 30 as a pass/fail line.** In a secondary analysis of a 3-cycle RCT (n=35) the odds of a menstrual disturbance fell 9% per unit of EA (OR 0.91) with no threshold. The 2025 Triad Coalition moves away from an EA threshold (full text: "is not recommended"): disturbances occur above 30, and EA is hard to measure outside a lab. | Strong (consensus) | PMID 29023359; PMID 41474492 |
| 1.3 | The size of the deficit matters. Deficits of 22% to 42% of needs (about 470 to 810 kcal/day) produced disturbances in a 4-cycle RCT; the percent deficit predicted how often they happened, not how severe. The Triad Coalition: 3 to 4 kg loss on a 500 to 800 kcal/day deficit over 2 to 3 months is enough to disturb cycles in normal-weight ovulatory women. | Strong | PMID 25352438; PMID 41474493 |
| 1.4 | Field EA is error-prone (self-reported intake and exercise cost). Use it as a trend, never a verdict; combine with serial markers taken at the same time of day: weight, BMI, eating attitudes, and if available RMR ratio, T3, IGF-1, leptin. | Strong | PMID 33832385; PMID 41474492 |
| 1.5 | Single-visit red flags for a referral: body weight under 85% of expected, BMI under 17.5, EA "much less than 45", or a low RMR ratio or T3. The RMR ratio cutoff of 0.90 is not universal; it depends on the prediction equation. | Moderate (Level B) | PMID 41474492 |
| 1.6 | Within-day timing counts. At similar 24 h EA, athletes with menstrual dysfunction spent more hours in deficit, and those hours tracked lower RMR ratio and estradiol and higher cortisol. Own judgement: spread intake around training; do not back-load the day. | Weak (cross-sectional, n=25) | PMID 29205517 |
| 1.7 | Not all low EA is harmful: the IOC splits it into adaptable (brief, benign) and problematic (prolonged or severe). Low carbohydrate availability is a separate risk. Use the IOC CAT2 tool via a clinician. | Strong (consensus) | PMID 37752011; PMID 37752002 |
| 1.8 | Recovery is food first. In the REFUEL RCT about +330 kcal/day for 12 months improved menstrual regularity alongside weight +4.9% and fat mass +18%. The Triad range to start recovery: +300 to 600 kcal/day. First menses took on average 2.6 months (range 1 to 7) in one intervention, longer when amenorrhea had lasted over a year. | Strong | PMID 41474493 |
| 1.9 | Treatment of energy deficiency is more intake and/or less training; no drug fixes the bone loss. | Strong | PMID 17909417; PMID 36303862 |

## 2. The menstrual cycle as a health signal

Clinicians are told to treat the cycle as a vital sign [PMID 26595586]. The coach reads it, logs it and refers. The numbers below are definitions from the cited bodies; **when to refer is own judgement built on them**.

| # | Fact | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Normal frequency, ages 18 to 45: every 24 to 38 days. Over 38 days is "infrequent" (FIGO dropped the word oligomenorrhea); under 24 is "frequent". | Strong (consensus definition) | DOI 10.1093/humrep/deac180 (PMID 35984284) |
| 2.2 | Normal regularity: shortest to longest cycle within 9 days (ages 18 to 25 and 42 to 45) or 7 days (ages 26 to 41). Infrequent or irregular bleeding should be treated as a possible ovulatory disorder. | Strong | PMID 35984284 |
| 2.3 | In the first year after menarche cycles typically run 21 to 45 days and settle to adult ranges within about 6 years. Median menarche is 12 to 13. | Strong | PMID 35984284; PMID 26595586 |
| 2.4 | Secondary amenorrhea: the Triad Coalition uses no menses for over 90 days (after 1 to 2 years post-menarche); FIGO's 2022 paper cites 6 months. Primary amenorrhea: Triad says no menses by 15; FIGO says 14 without breast development, 16 with it. **Use the earlier trigger.** | Strong (definitions differ) | PMID 41474493; PMID 35984284 |
| 2.5 | A regular-length cycle does not prove ovulation; ovulatory disorders can sit inside normal-length cycles. | Moderate | PMID 35984284 |
| 2.6 | One returned period is not recovery. Estrogen exposure did not rise until three consecutive cycles under 36 days. | Strong (Level A) | PMID 41474493 |
| 2.7 | Combined oral contraceptives produce withdrawal bleeds that can mask whether natural cycles have returned, do not improve bone density, and should not be used to restart cycles in FHA. A pill bleed is not a health signal. | Strong | PMID 41474492; PMID 28368518; PMID 36303862 |

**Suggest a doctor visit (own judgement on 2.1 to 2.6):** no period for 90 days; none by 15; cycles that keep falling outside 24 to 38 days or outside the 7 to 9 day spread; heavy bleeding, or fatigue with heavy bleeding (see 6.2); any red flag in 1.5; trying to conceive with irregular cycles. Phrase it as "worth checking", never as a named condition. An endocrinology referral is what the Triad Coalition advises for suspected hypothalamic causes [PMID 41474492].

## 3. Body fat and fertility: known vs not known

**Known (all observational, so association, not proof of cause):**
- Ovulatory infertility risk is U-shaped by BMI, rising below 20 and above 24 (Nurses' Health Study II, 830 cases). About 12% of US ovulatory infertility was attributed to underweight and 25% to overweight, so overweight and inactivity account for more than underweight. **Moderate** [PMID 11880759]
- Underweight women had higher odds of subfertility (OR 1.88) in a Rotterdam cohort of 3,604 women; overweight 1.35 and obesity 1.67. **Moderate** [PMID 39298166]
- In 2.3 million Chinese couples trying for a first pregnancy, the optimal female BMI for time to pregnancy was 20.6 to 23.1. **Moderate** [PMID 33066977]
- The underweight signal is not uniform: in a Danish cohort of 1,651 women, underweight women who had never given birth trended lower (FR 0.82, 0.63 to 1.06, not significant) while underweight women who had given birth conceived faster (FR 1.61, 1.08 to 2.39). **Weak** [PMID 19828554]
- In the lab, energy availability, not body fat, switched LH pulsatility off [PMID 12519869]. Recovery tracks restored intake plus weight and fat regain [PMID 41474493]. The 1974 "minimum fatness" idea [PMID 4469672] is history, not a rule. **Moderate**

**Not known (say so plainly):**
- No source read here gives a validated body fat percentage below which fertility stops. The IOC REDs subgroup found no unique body composition cutoff even for performance [PMID 37752006]. **Insufficient**
- Whether fat gain itself or restored energy availability restores fertility in lean active women; they move together in the trials. **Insufficient** (own synthesis of PMID 41474493)
- BMI does not separate muscle from fat, so a lean muscular woman and an underfed one can share a BMI. **Own judgement**

**Coach rule (own judgement):** for a lean woman who wants to conceive, the nutrition move is adequate energy and regular cycles, not a body fat target. Refer for fertility questions.

## 4. Recomposition for lean women ("skinny fat")

"Skinny fat" in the literature is **normal weight obesity (NWO)**: normal BMI, high body fat [PMID 24438734; PMID 34007010]. The fix is more muscle, not less food.

| # | Rule | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | NWO is real but loosely defined: body fat cutoffs vary widely, prevalence runs 4.5% to 22%. Do not quote a percentage as a diagnosis. | Moderate | PMID 34007010 |
| 4.2 | NWO carries cardiometabolic risk vs normal-weight lean (metabolic syndrome OR 2.24) and is more common in women and older adults. Cross-sectional only. | Moderate | PMID 35412026; PMID 24438734 |
| 4.3 | **Resistance training is the lever.** Across 58 RCTs (54 pooled) it cut body fat by 1.46 percentage points, fat mass by 0.55 kg and visceral fat, with no difference by sex. | Strong | PMID 34536199 |
| 4.4 | Women gain muscle at the same relative rate as men on the same program (no sex difference in hypertrophy; women gained more relative upper-body strength). | Moderate | PMID 32218059 |
| 4.5 | Protein amplifies it. In aspiring female physique athletes on 8 weeks of training, 2.5 vs 0.9 g/kg/day gave fat-free mass +2.1 vs +0.6 kg, and fat mass fell only on high protein. Small RCT (n=17). | Weak | PMID 29405780 |
| 4.6 | **Do not cut a lean woman to fix this** (own judgement on these facts). Leaner people keep more lean mass when loss is slow, and lean mass accrual is driven by a sustained surplus. A 470 to 810 kcal/day deficit already disturbs cycles (1.3). | Moderate | PMID 28630601; PMID 25352438 |
| 4.7 | No RCT has tested diet or training interventions in NWO specifically; 4.3 to 4.6 are borrowed from general and athletic samples. | Insufficient | PMID 34007010 |

**Default plan (own judgement on 4.3 to 4.6):** maintenance calories (small surplus if muscle gain is the main goal), progressive full-body resistance training, protein 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg/day, judge by strength and tape measurements over 8 to 12 weeks rather than scale weight.

## 5. Protein for women

| # | Rule | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Daily target 1.4 to 2.2 g/kg/day for active women at every menstrual stage and on contraceptives, split into doses every 3 to 4 h. Independent evidence (5.2, 5.3) backs about 1.4 to 1.9; the top of the range rests on the two ISSN stands alone. | Moderate (ISSN is part-funded by supplement companies and the 2017 authors declare supplement ties; see the screen in sources.md) | PMID 37221858; PMID 28642676 |
| 5.2 | Female tracer studies put needs well above the 0.8 g/kg RDA: intermittent exercise EAR 1.41, RDA 1.71 (n=6); strength-trained EAR 1.49; endurance EAR about 1.6, recommended about 1.9, no sex difference. | Moderate (small n, consistent) | PMID 28692631; PMID 30395050; PMID 37710376 |
| 5.3 | Gains in fat-free mass from added protein plateau around 1.6 g/kg/day (49 RCTs, mostly men). The senior author declares Dairy Council funding; that bias would push toward more protein, and the finding is a ceiling. | Strong | PMID 28698222 |
| 5.4 | Around training: 0.32 to 0.38 g/kg near the session; aim for the upper end in the luteal phase. ISSN says its whole timing section rests "upon the limited research". | Weak | PMID 37221858 |
| 5.5 | Lean, resistance-trained and in a deficit: 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg of **fat-free mass** to keep muscle. | Weak (ISSN stand; authors declare supplement-company ties) | PMID 28630601 |
| 5.6 | Peri and post-menopausal: a bolus with about 10 g essential amino acids near training to offset anabolic resistance. | Weak | PMID 37221858 |
| 5.7 | Energy comes first; protein targets do not protect anyone in low energy availability (own judgement). Of supplements, caffeine, iron and creatine (3 to 5 g/day) have the most female evidence. | Moderate | PMID 37221858 |

Female-specific protein data is thin; ISSN and its 2017 body-composition stand both say so [PMID 37221858; PMID 28630601].

## 6. Preconception micronutrients

For anyone who could become pregnant. Once pregnant, doses belong to the obstetric clinician.

### 6.1 Folic acid (Strong)
- 400 to 800 mcg/day folic acid as a supplement for anyone planning or able to become pregnant (USPSTF grade A, high certainty) [PMID 37526713].
- Trial effect: neural tube defects RR 0.31 at 400 mcg or more [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD007950.pub3]; the key Hungarian RCT OR 0.13 [PMID 28097361]; 72% protection in women with a prior affected pregnancy [PMID 1677062].
- Use folic acid, not 5-MTHF, even with an MTHFR 677C>T variant (CDC via ODS); the 400 mcg is in addition to food folate. RDA 400 mcg DFE, UL 1,000 mcg for folic acid from supplements and fortified food [ODS Folate].
- A prior affected pregnancy needs a clinician-set dose; the MRC abstract read here does not state the dose, so none is given here [PMID 1677062].

### 6.2 Iron and ferritin (Strong for testing; Moderate for treating low ferritin without anemia)
- RDA 18 mg/day (women 19 to 50), 27 mg in pregnancy, 1.8 times higher for vegetarians; UL 45 mg [ODS Iron].
- About 38% of non-pregnant reproductive-age women in high-income countries have iron deficiency without anemia and 13% have anemia; heavy menstrual bleeding is a main driver [PMID 40159291; PMID 36706856].
- Ferritin under 15 mcg/L (WHO) is expert opinion; physiology puts the iron-deficiency line near 25 [PMID 35404995]; a 2025 JAMA review uses under 30 or transferrin saturation under 20% [PMID 40159291].
- In fatigued non-anemic women with ferritin under 50, 80 mg/day iron for 12 weeks cut fatigue 18.9 percentage points more than placebo (47.7% vs 28.8%), with no effect on quality of life, mood or anxiety [PMID 22777991].
- **Test, do not guess:** testing is indicated with symptoms and worth considering with heavy bleeding or pregnancy; oral iron can be daily or alternate-day [PMID 40159291]. Hereditary hemochromatosis raises iron overload risk [ODS Iron], so never supplement blind (own judgement). **Own judgement:** coach suggests a ferritin test, the clinician sets the dose.

### 6.3 Iodine (Moderate)
- RDA 150 mcg, pregnancy 220, lactation 290; UL 1,100 [ODS Iodine].
- The American Thyroid Association 2017 guideline recommends 150 mcg/day as potassium iodide for those planning pregnancy, pregnant or lactating (as quoted by ODS; the abstract does not carry the number) [ODS Iodine; PMID 28056690]. Whether the 2026 ATA update keeps that dose was not checked [DOI 10.1177/10507256261445624].
- ODS notes that more research on iodine supplementation in pregnancy is needed, so the grade rests on guidelines more than trials [ODS Iodine]. Thyroid management is part of prepregnancy care [PMID 30575679].

### 6.4 Choline (Weak to Insufficient above the AI)
- AI 425 mg/day, pregnancy 450, lactation 550; UL 3,500 [ODS Choline].
- Only about 11% of pregnant women meet the AI; higher maternal choline was linked to fewer adverse pregnancy outcomes (OR 0.51, observational) [PMID 38607338].
- Trials conflict: 930 vs 480 mg/day in the third trimester sped infant processing (n=26) with a follow-up at age 7 [PMID 29217669; PMID 34962672]; 750 mg phosphatidylcholine from week 18 did nothing (n=140) [PMID 23134891]. A 2025 review rates the evidence insufficient [PMID 40077755].
- **Coach rule:** reach the AI from food (meat, fish, eggs, dairy, crucifers, beans); do not promise cognitive benefits.

### 6.5 Vitamin D (Strong for the DRI; Weak for pregnancy outcomes)
- RDA 600 IU (15 mcg), UL 4,000 IU [ODS VitaminD].
- Endocrine Society 2024: against doses above the DRI in healthy adults under 75 and against routine 25(OH)D testing; suggests empiric vitamin D in pregnancy [PMID 38828931].
- **Contradiction:** Cochrane 2024 rates pregnancy evidence low to very low certainty: very uncertain for pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes and preterm birth; may reduce severe postpartum hemorrhage (RR 0.68) and low birthweight [PMID 39077939]. Report both.

### 6.6 Omega-3 and fish (Moderate for supplementation in pregnancy; DRI only before it)
- ALA AI 1.1 g/day, pregnancy 1.4, lactation 1.3 [ODS Omega3].
- In pregnancy, omega-3 lowered preterm birth under 37 weeks (RR 0.89) and under 34 weeks (RR 0.58, high quality) but probably raised gestation past 42 weeks (RR 1.61) [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD003402.pub3]. The later ORIP RCT (n=5,486) found no effect on early preterm birth (aRR 1.13, 0.79 to 1.63) [PMID 31509674].
- For those who might become or are pregnant or breastfeeding: 8 to 12 oz/week of lower-mercury seafood, 2 to 3 servings from the FDA "Best Choices" list. Avoid king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, Gulf of Mexico tilefish and bigeye tuna [FDA fish].

## 7. Calcium and bone

| # | Rule | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | RDA 1,000 mg/day ages 19 to 50 (also in pregnancy), 1,200 mg for women 51 and over; UL 2,500 mg ages 19 to 50. | Strong | [ODS Calcium] |
| 7.2 | Lifestyle sets 20% to 40% of adult peak bone mass. Calcium and physical activity have grade A evidence, strongest in late childhood and around puberty; vitamin D and dairy have good evidence; DMPA injections harm accrual. | Strong | PMID 26856587 |
| 7.3 | Energy-deficient women and athletes need adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D; the Triad full text sets that at least at the RDA, from food with or without supplements. | Strong | PMID 36303862; PMID 41474492 (full text) |
| 7.4 | Calcium cannot outrun an energy deficit: bone loss continues while the deficit persists, low bone density shows up even with regular cycles, and the pill does not help. Restore energy, protein, calcium and vitamin D together. | Strong | PMID 41474492; PMID 36303862; PMID 17909417 |

## Anti-patterns

1. Calling someone "in REDs" or "amenorrheic" from a food log. That is a diagnosis (rail 0.1).
2. Treating EA 30 as a safe line, or EA 31 as fine (1.2).
3. Prescribing a deficit to a lean woman for "skinny fat" (4.6).
4. Reading pill bleeds as proof the cycle is healthy (2.7).
5. Calling one returned period "recovered" (2.6).
6. Quoting a body fat percentage for fertility (section 3, Not known).
7. Blind iron megadosing, or swapping folic acid for methylfolate in a preconception plan (6.1, 6.2).
8. Promising choline or vitamin D pregnancy benefits the trials do not show (6.4, 6.5).
9. Using calcium as the bone fix while energy stays low (7.4).

## Checklist

- [ ] Disordered eating and red flag screen done first (rail 0.2, rule 1.5)
- [ ] EA estimated as a trend, with the error stated (1.4)
- [ ] Cycle length and spread logged against 24 to 38 days and 7 to 9 days (2.1, 2.2)
- [ ] Referral triggers checked; wording names no condition (section 2 box)
- [ ] Lean client: maintenance or surplus plus resistance training, not a cut (4.6)
- [ ] Protein set at 1.4 to 2.2 g/kg/day, doses every 3 to 4 h (5.1)
- [ ] If pregnancy is possible: folic acid 400 to 800 mcg, iodine 150 mcg, fish advice, ferritin test suggested (6.1 to 6.6)
- [ ] Calcium 1,000 mg and vitamin D 600 IU met, energy first (7.1, 7.4)
- [ ] Thin evidence flagged aloud: female protein data, luteal timing, choline, vitamin D in pregnancy, NWO interventions
