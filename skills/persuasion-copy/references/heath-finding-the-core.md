# Heath: Find the Core

## When to use

Trigger when the user is trying to communicate something with multiple competing priorities and the question is "what's THE one thing?" Concrete cues:

- A pitch / deck / one-pager / strategy doc with 5 competing main points and no clear lead
- A team where everyone has a different answer to "what's our most important priority"
- A founder trying to compress their company into one sentence
- A movement, campaign, or initiative whose messaging is muddled
- A product where "we do many things well" is hurting positioning
- An internal-comms moment where leadership has 8 announcements and frontline workers only retain 1
- "If you had one sentence to say to a customer, what would it be?" â€” and the user can't pick
- A board update where the audience needs to walk away with ONE conclusion, not seven

Don't trigger for: prose tightening at the sentence level (use `prose-style` or `distill`), polishing an already-clear tagline (use `copywriting`), pricing decisions, or pure design-system reduction. This skill operates at the *message strategy* layer â€” what's the singular essence â€” not the surface-craft layer.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Heath's claim (echoing his collaboration with Carville's "It's the economy, stupid"): if you say three things, you don't say anything. Audiences don't retain everything you say; they retain the most-emphasized thing, and only if it's clear. The discipline of finding the core is the discipline of admitting that your audience can hold exactly ONE priority at a time â€” and the painful work of choosing which one.

The Heath brothers structure stick-making as a two-step process:
1. **Find the core** (this skill) â€” strip to essence
2. **Translate the core using SUCCESs** â€” make it concrete, unexpected, credible, etc. (the other six Heath skills)

Most messages fail at step 1. Smart people see complexity, value nuance, and resist cutting anything. So they ship messages where the core is buried in qualifications, alternatives, and supporting context. The audience receives noise.

### Commander's Intent â€” the military model

Heath cites U.S. Army Commander's Intent as the operational template. Before any operation, the commanding officer must complete two sentences:

1. **"If we do nothing else during tomorrow's mission, we must ___."**
2. **"The single most important thing that we must do tomorrow is ___."**

These force the commander to pick. The Commander's Intent then guides every soldier when the plan breaks (and plans always break). When a sergeant on the ground encounters a situation the plan didn't anticipate, they don't need to phone HQ â€” they apply the Commander's Intent and improvise.

This is the test of a real core: does it guide action when the plan fails? If the core is so abstract or so safe that it doesn't help a frontline person make a decision, it's not a core; it's a slogan.

### The Southwest example â€” core as decision filter

Southwest's core (per Herb Kelleher, cited by Heath): "We are THE low-fare airline." That one phrase decides:
- Whether to serve in-flight meals (no â€” adds cost)
- Whether to use multiple aircraft types (no â€” adds maintenance cost)
- Whether to compete on every route (no â€” only short-haul where low-fare wins)
- Whether to hire on attitude vs. credentials (yes â€” attitude scales without payroll)

The test: every operational question can be filtered through the core, and the core gives an answer. If the core can't filter decisions, it's a brand mood, not an operational core.

### Core as proverb â€” the compact form

A core that works at the strategic level still needs *compact form* to spread. Heath cites proverbs as the ideal: 2â€“7 words, memorable, sticky.

- "It's the economy, stupid." (Carville's wall sign in the 1992 Clinton campaign)
- "Names, names, and names." (Bill Maupin, small-town newspaper editor â€” meaning: print every name you can in the paper)
- "Show, don't tell." (writing rule)
- "Move fast and break things." (Facebook, early)
- "Don't be evil." (Google, early)

A proverb is a core that has been compressed into something a stranger can remember after one hearing. Strategic clarity that doesn't survive compression isn't sticky.

### What "core" is NOT

- Not a *list of priorities*. A list of 3-5 "core values" is not a core. A core is singular.
- Not a *mission statement* in the usual corporate sense â€” those are typically aspirational and abstract. A core is operational: it guides specific decisions.
- Not the *most accurate* description of the strategy. The most accurate description is usually too qualified and contextual. The core is the most *compressed* version that's still substantively true.
- Not a *slogan*. Slogans are marketing artifacts; cores are decision filters. A slogan that doesn't filter decisions is just a tagline.

### The hard part â€” what you give up

Finding the core requires saying NO to things that are genuinely important. Heath is blunt: smart people resist this because they can see that the cut items have value. The discipline isn't about cutting unimportant things; it's about cutting *important* things that are not THE most important.

When you find the core, you should feel slight pain about what got cut. If you don't, you probably haven't cut deeply enough â€” you've just renamed the existing list.

### Manipulation risk

Compression is dangerous. Specific traps:

- **False simplification.** Stripping context until the core is no longer true. "We're THE low-fare airline" works because Southwest actually delivered low fares; if they hadn't, the core would have been a lie. Cores must be true.
- **Hiding the cuts.** The audience deserves to know that other priorities exist and were deprioritized. A leader who pretends the core is the *only* consideration treats the audience as unable to handle complexity. Acknowledge what was cut and why.
- **Slogan masquerading as core.** A marketing-friendly phrase that doesn't actually filter decisions. Test: does the core stop you from doing things you otherwise would? If no, it's decoration.
- **Compressed for spread, not for truth.** A proverb form that survived compression by losing accuracy. Test: would the people who have to *execute* the core agree it represents the actual strategy?

The honest core is true, operational, painful to cut to, and survives compression without becoming a lie.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **State the situation the core needs to govern.** What are you communicating about? To whom? What decision do they need to make or what action do they need to take? If you can't name the target action, the core won't have anything to filter.

2. **Brainstorm the candidate cores.** Write 5-10 candidate cores. Don't filter yet. Include some that feel too narrow, some that feel too broad. Range matters.

3. **Apply the Commander's Intent forcing function.**
   - "If we do nothing else, we must ___."
   - "The single most important thing is ___."
   
   Each candidate core should be able to complete these sentences honestly. If a candidate doesn't survive these completions, drop it.

4. **Apply the decision-filter test.** For each surviving candidate, list 5 specific decisions the user team will face. Does the core give a clear answer to each one? A candidate that gives "depends" answers to most decisions isn't yet a core â€” it's still too vague.

5. **Apply the cut test.** For each surviving candidate, name 2-3 important priorities that this core *deprioritizes*. If you can't name what you're cutting, the candidate is probably a list disguised as a single thing.

6. **Pick the single core.** Choose the candidate that survives all three tests with the cleanest filter and the most honest acknowledgment of what's being cut. There should be slight pain. If there isn't, you haven't cut deep enough.

7. **Compress to proverb form.** 2-7 words. Memorable. Quotable. The proverb is what spreads; the longer-form core is what gets explained when someone asks.

8. **Run the manipulation-risk check.**
   - Is the core *true*? Would executors agree it represents the actual work?
   - Does it filter decisions, or is it a mood-board?
   - Are the cuts being acknowledged, or hidden?
   - Did compression cost any accuracy?

9. **Produce the Core Discovery artifact.**
   - Single-sentence core
   - Proverb form (2-7 words)
   - List of what was deliberately cut (and why)
   - 5 example decisions the core filters
   - Coherence check: does every other element of the broader message support this core, or do they fight it?

10. **Hand off to the SUCCESs principles.** The core is step 1. To make the core stick, translate it through `heath-concrete`, `heath-unexpected`, `heath-credible`, `heath-emotional`, `heath-stories`. This skill ends at the core; downstream skills make the core land.

## Common failure modes

- **Picking the easiest candidate.** The candidate that's least controversial usually isn't the right core â€” it's the lowest-common-denominator restatement of what everyone already agrees on. Real cores produce disagreement; that's how you know they're operational.

- **Listing instead of choosing.** "Our core is excellence, integrity, and customer focus." That's a list. The discipline is to pick ONE. If three feel essential, the message is "we don't have a strategy; we have aspirations."

- **Slogan without filter.** "We make the world a better place." Filters nothing. Any company can claim it; no decision is constrained by it. Replace with something that excludes specific actions.

- **Survival via abstraction.** Compressing the core by abstracting it â€” "we serve our customers" rather than "we serve THE low-fare customer." Abstraction makes the core uncontroversial and therefore useless.

- **Cutting cosmetically but not substantively.** Reorganizing the same list of priorities into a hierarchy and calling the top one "the core." If the items below the top are still treated as essential in practice, you haven't actually cut.

- **Confusing brand for core.** Brand language ("authentic," "innovative," "premium") is rarely operational. A brand position can derive from a core, but it's not the core itself. The core has to constrain action.

- **Skipping the proverb compression.** A 40-word core may be substantively right but won't spread. If the team can't repeat the core from memory in normal speech, it's not yet a stick-able core.

- **Treating the core as permanent.** Strategy shifts. The core may need to evolve. The discipline of singular-core *at any given time* is what matters; cores can be revised when the situation genuinely changes.

- **Finding the core but never doing the SUCCESs translation.** Finding the core is Step 1. The Heath book has six more steps. A core that hasn't been made concrete / unexpected / credible / emotional / story-bound is just a strategic statement, not yet a sticky idea. Route to the principle skills next.
