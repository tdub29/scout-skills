## The framework

### Core thesis

Aristotle's central style claim: good prose is *clear, appropriate, and ornamented*. Not all three at maximum â€” at the right balance for the genre and the occasion. Clarity is the floor; below it, nothing else matters because the audience can't follow. Propriety is the tuning to the moment; out of register, even brilliant style feels wrong. Ornament is what lifts prose from competent to memorable â€” and it's where the actual rhetorical craft lives.

Aristotle's deeper contribution, beyond the three virtues, is the *mechanism of memorable sentences*. He identifies the recipe: proportional metaphor + antithesis + actuality (showing things in motion, "before the eyes"). Lines that combine all three are what audiences quote back later. Lines that miss them are forgotten by the end of the week.

The frigidity catalog â€” what makes prose feel cold and contrived â€” is the diagnostic for over-trying. Most stilted writing fails through the same four mechanisms; naming them lets you fix the prose specifically rather than vaguely "loosening it up."

### The three style virtues

**Clarity.** Use ordinary, current words. The audience must understand without effort. Rare words, archaic vocabulary, technical jargon outside the audience's domain â€” all reduce clarity. Aristotle is blunt: prose's first job is to be understood. If you have to choose between a clearer phrasing and a more elegant one, choose clearer.

**Propriety.** Match the style to the subject, the speaker, and the audience. Heightened diction (poetic, elevated) fits some occasions (ceremonial speech, high stakes). Plain diction fits others (informal context, technical subject). Mismatch â€” heightened diction in a casual context, plain diction in a moment that calls for elevation â€” feels off even when each piece is individually fine. Propriety also covers emotional register: speech expressing anger should *sound* angry (or controlled if controlled is the move), not flatly neutral.

**Ornament.** The aesthetic and rhetorical lift â€” unusual words, metaphor, periodic structure, antithesis, sound patterns. Ornament is where the prose stops being merely correct and becomes *rhetorical*. Used well, it makes lines memorable. Used badly (excess, far-reach), it produces frigidity.

The three are in tension. Maximum clarity often means stripping ornament. Maximum ornament can violate propriety. The skill is in the balance â€” which Aristotle ties to the genre (see `aristotle-three-genres`): ceremonial speech tolerates more ornament than forensic; forensic tolerates more than deliberative casual conversation.

### Graphic metaphor â€” the proportional kind

Not all metaphors are equal. Aristotle classifies metaphor types and singles out the *proportional* metaphor as the strongest: a metaphor that holds the structure A:B::C:D. "Old age is the evening of life" â€” old age (A) is to life (B) as evening (C) is to day (D). Both pairs share the same relational structure.

What makes proportional metaphors especially powerful is when they include *actuality* â€” the suggestion of motion, life, action. Aristotle praises Homer for metaphors that "give lifeless things life and movement":

- "The arrow flew" (rather than "moved")
- "The mountain roared"
- "The sword bit into him"

Lifeless things, given action through the metaphor's verb, *become* alive in the audience's imagination. This is the mechanism behind every memorable metaphor in political and commercial rhetoric: "the iron curtain descended"; "we have a dream that one day...will be transformed."

Aristotle is also specific about where good metaphors come from: *kindred* sources, not far-fetched ones. A metaphor that draws from an analogous domain (war â†’ contest, garden â†’ cultivation of mind) lands. A metaphor that reaches too far (the Empire State Building â†’ a flamingo) feels strained even if technically proportional.

### Lively sayings â€” the recipe for the memorable line

Aristotle's recipe for sentences audiences quote back:

1. **Antithesis** â€” contrasted ideas placed side by side. "Peace in our time / war in our future." "Ask not what your country can do for you / ask what you can do for your country." The contrast clarifies through opposition and gives the sentence a hinge.

2. **Metaphor** â€” preferably proportional, preferably with actuality. The metaphor gives the line concrete imagery the audience can hold in mind.

3. **Actuality** â€” events shown as if happening *now*, in motion. Not "the proposal will reduce employment" but "this proposal will throw people out of work." The audience sees it.

When all three combine â€” antithesis + proportional metaphor + actuality â€” you get the lines that survive the speech and get quoted. Aristotle's specific observation: liveliness is the felt experience of the audience *just barely* catching up with the implication. The line is fast; the audience completes it; that completion feels like insight.

### Frigidity â€” the four faults of bad taste

Aristotle's diagnostic for prose that's straining for effect and falling flat. The four faults:

1. **Compound-word excess.** Inventing or stacking compound words that the language doesn't naturally support. Modern equivalent: "synergistic-multiplatform-customer-engagement-strategy." Each component is real; the compound is grotesque.

2. **Strange / rare words.** Reaching for vocabulary the audience won't recognize, when an ordinary word would serve. Pretension dressed as elegance.

3. **Unbalanced epithets.** Adjectives that overwhelm their nouns: "the magnificently effulgent dawn." Aristotle's phrasing: "like seasoning that swallows the meat." A weak noun can't carry an aggressive epithet.

4. **Far-fetched metaphors.** Metaphors whose source domains are too remote from their targets. The audience can't make the leap; the metaphor stalls instead of landing. Far-fetched metaphors often sound poetic to the writer but read as inscrutable to the reader.

The frigidity audit is the most useful editing pass for rhetorical prose. Most stilted writing fails on at least two of the four; naming which ones lets the editor surgically remove them rather than gesturing at "make it less try-hard."

### Manipulation risk

Style mostly has lower manipulation risk than content-targeting frameworks (emotions, motives). But Aristotle warns about specific style-level manipulations:

- **Faking sincerity via propriety.** Matching tone to what the audience *expects* sincere speech to sound like, without actually being sincere. The skill of seeming heartfelt without the substance.
- **Lively sayings as cover for falsehood.** Wit can make a false claim memorable. The line lands; the audience remembers it; nobody checks whether it's true. Aristotle himself notes that lively sayings work partly because they outrun the audience's ability to fact-check in real time. Use the recipe to make *true* claims memorable, not to slide false ones past examination.
- **Graphic metaphor as visceral slander.** "She's a snake" uses proportional metaphor + actuality to trigger disgust. The image is vivid; the underlying claim ("she is untrustworthy") is hidden inside it. Powerful â€” but if the underlying claim is false, the metaphor is propaganda.

The skill builds these risks into the audit: any rhetorically powerful line should also pass the truth-test.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Identify the artifact's purpose and genre.** A line in a deliberative pitch wants different style than a line in a eulogy. Route to `aristotle-three-genres` if uncertain.

2. **Score the three virtues at the line level.** For the specific lines you want to elevate:
   - *Clarity:* will an unfamiliar reader follow it on first pass? If no, fix clarity before adding ornament.
   - *Propriety:* does the diction match the genre, audience, and emotional register? Heightened where it should be heightened; plain where it should be plain.
   - *Ornament:* is there enough rhetorical lift? Below the right level, prose feels professional but flat.

3. **For lines that need to land, apply the lively-saying recipe.** Walk through:
   - *Antithesis:* is there a contrast in the line, or can one be introduced? Two ideas in tension produce hinge-and-pivot energy.
   - *Metaphor:* is there a proportional metaphor (A:B::C:D structure)? If not, can one be added from a *kindred* domain (not far-fetched)?
   - *Actuality:* is the verb showing motion / action, or static abstraction? "Increases by 30%" is static; "doubles" is half-static; "explodes" is active. Choose the level appropriate to the genre but lean active.

4. **Test the metaphor source domain.** Is the metaphor's source kindred to the target (related fields, analogous structures), or far-fetched? Test by stating the metaphor's mechanism in plain English: "I'm comparing X to Y because both involve Z." If you can't articulate Z, the metaphor is too remote.

5. **Run the frigidity audit on the whole piece.**
   - Compound-word excess: any compound nouns or phrases that should be split?
   - Strange / rare words: any vocabulary that an audience member would have to look up?
   - Unbalanced epithets: any adjective-noun pairs where the adjective overwhelms the noun?
   - Far-fetched metaphors: any metaphors that don't pass the source-domain test in step 4?

   Flag each instance and either fix or accept with rationale.

6. **Manipulation-risk check.**
   - Are any lively sayings carrying claims that wouldn't survive scrutiny?
   - Are any graphic metaphors smuggling judgments (about people, decisions, situations) past the audience's examination?
   - Is the propriety calibration honest, or is it performed sincerity?

7. **Produce the style audit.** Per high-value sentence: current state + virtue scores + recommended moves + manipulation-risk flag. For the overall piece: a frigidity-audit summary and the small number of lines that are doing the rhetorical heavy lifting (the audience will quote those; design them deliberately).

## Common failure modes

- **Ornament without clarity.** Adding metaphor, antithesis, and elevated diction to prose the audience can't follow. The result feels purple, not powerful. Always pass the clarity floor first.

- **Propriety mismatch.** Heightened diction in a memo, or plain diction in a graduation speech. Each piece sounds individually fine but feels off because the *register* doesn't match the occasion. Match the elevation to the genre.

- **Treating ornament as decoration.** Adding metaphors that don't carry argumentative weight, antitheses that don't pivot anything. Ornament should *do work* â€” clarify a relationship, intensify a contrast, make the abstract concrete. Decoration alone reads as filler.

- **Far-fetched metaphor mistaken for creativity.** "Our pivot is the chrysalis from which we'll emerge as a butterfly piloting a starship." Each metaphor element is creative; the combination is incoherent. Aristotle's rule: metaphors from *kindred* sources. The further the reach, the harder the landing.

- **Frigidity via compound-word abuse.** "Synergistic-customer-engagement-platform." The corporate-speak equivalent of Aristotle's Lycophron and Gorgias examples â€” every part is "professional," the combination is dead.

- **Antithesis as gimmick.** "Not just smart â€” wise." "Not selling products â€” building relationships." Antithesis works when the two halves are actually in meaningful contrast; gimmick antithesis pairs near-synonyms. The audience clocks the move and the sentence feels formulaic.

- **Actuality applied to inappropriate genres.** "Our quarterly numbers EXPLODE upward!" â€” in a financial report, actuality feels manipulative. Save high-actuality verbs for moments and genres that warrant them.

- **Memorable lines carrying false claims.** The wit lands; the audience remembers; nobody fact-checks. Aristotle's warning is direct: use the recipe to make *true* claims memorable, not to launder false ones. The line's rhetorical power is amplified by its falseness when the falseness eventually emerges.

- **Stripping all ornament for "clarity."** The over-correction. Prose without metaphor, antithesis, or actuality is *clear* but not *memorable* â€” competent but forgettable. The clarity-only fix is correct for technical documentation; wrong for rhetoric.

- **Confusing this skill with `prose-style`.** `prose-style` is about sentence-level *correctness and craft* (cadence, structure, word-choice precision). This skill is about *rhetorical power* (the recipe for lines that land in persuasion contexts). They compose â€” run prose-style first for craft, then this skill for rhetorical lift on key lines.
