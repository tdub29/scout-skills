### Three symbol channels (substance-free triggers)

1. **Titles** â€” "Dr.," "Professor," credentials. Hard to verify in the moment, easy to assert; they measurably change how people judge the same person's height, opinions, and instructions.
2. **Clothes/uniforms** â€” lab coats, suits, badges, security attire. People follow instructions from a uniform they'd ignore from street clothes.
3. **Trappings** â€” luxury car, office, watch; markers that *imply* status and get treated as proof of it.

### The trust-building move

The most persuasive authorities first establish **expertise** (genuine competence) and then **trustworthiness**. A reliable trustworthiness signal: mention a small weakness or downside *before* the main argument. The early, voluntary admission of a negative makes the subsequent positives far more believable.

## How to apply

To invoke it ethically:

1. **Establish genuine expertise early** â€” relevant credentials, track record, specifics â€” *before* the recommendation, not after.
2. **Build trustworthiness by conceding a real limitation first.** "This won't help if X" or "the weak point is Y, butâ€¦" earns credibility for everything that follows.
3. **Cite authorities the audience already respects** and that genuinely back your point.

To detect it on yourself:

1. **Ask two questions Cialdini recommends:** "Is this authority *truly* an expert *on this*?" and "How truthful can we expect this expert to be here?" (What's their incentive?)
2. **Strip the symbols.** Would the claim persuade you from the same person in street clothes with no title? If not, you're following the costume.
