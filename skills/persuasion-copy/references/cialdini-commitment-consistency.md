## The framework

> "Once we make a choice or take a stand, we will encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to behave consistently with that commitment." (Influence, Ch. 3 â€” German ed.: *Commitment und Konsequenz*)

After committing to something â€” especially out loud, in writing, or through effort â€” people work to align later behavior with that commitment, because consistency is socially prized and cognitively easy (it spares us from rethinking). The lever is the **initial commitment**: get a small one, and consistent larger behavior tends to follow on its own.

### What makes a commitment "stick"

A commitment drives future behavior most strongly when it is:
1. **Active** â€” written or stated, not merely thought.
2. **Public** â€” witnessed by others.
3. **Effortful** â€” costly to make (initiation rituals, application hurdles); effort breeds valuation.
4. **Voluntary** â€” freely chosen, with no large external reward or threat the person can credit instead. (Inner ownership is what makes it self-sustaining.)

### Classic mechanisms

- **Foot-in-the-door.** A tiny first agreement reshapes self-image, making a much larger later request consistent. Freedman & Fraser: homeowners who agreed to a small "drive carefully" window card later allowed a huge ugly billboard on their lawns at far higher rates.
- **Low-balling.** Secure a commitment, then change the terms for the worse. The decision often survives because the person has already grown their own reasons to support it.
- **Throwing good after bad.** Prior investment (money, time) pulls people to escalate consistently â€” a hook worth naming explicitly so it can be resisted.

## How to apply

To invoke it ethically:

1. **Start with a small, genuine commitment** related to the goal, then build.
2. **Make it active and public** â€” get it written down, said aloud, or shared with others the person respects.
3. **Let it be freely chosen.** Don't over-incentivize or pressure; external rewards undercut the internal ownership that makes consistency durable.
4. **Reflect the commitment back** as part of the person's identity ("you're someone whoâ€¦") only when it's true.

To detect it on yourself:

1. **Ask the "knowing what I now know" question.** "If I could go back, knowing the real terms, would I make this same choice again?" Cuts through low-balling and sunk-cost escalation.
2. **Notice stomach-vs-story.** When consistency pressure pushes you toward a choice your gut resists, the commitment â€” not the merits â€” may be steering.

## Common failure modes

- **Manufacturing trivial commitments** the person doesn't believe â€” produces compliance without the self-reinforcing engine, and resentment when noticed.
- **Low-balling that gets exposed** destroys trust permanently.
- **Confusing coerced agreement with commitment** â€” pressured or paid commitments don't generate internal consistency.
- **Ignoring escalation traps** in your own decisions because admitting a reversal feels inconsistent.

## Ethical guardrail

Legitimate when the commitments are genuine, freely made, and in the person's own interest to keep (e.g., a patient writing down their own health goals). Manipulative when small commitments are engineered as a trap to extract larger compliance the person wouldn't otherwise give, or when terms are switched after the hook (low-balling).
