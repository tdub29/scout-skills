## The framework

Two operationally distinct skills, drawn from Cialdini's chapters 5 and 6.

> "Commanders of Attention 1: The Attractors" (Ch 5) â€” what GRABS attention.
> "Commanders of Attention 2: The Magnetizers" (Ch 6) â€” what HOLDS attention.

Different jobs. Different triggers. Often used in sequence â€” attract first, then magnetize.

### Attractors: 4 categories that grab attention automatically

**1. Sexual stimuli**
Attractive faces, sexual imagery â€” captured before conscious processing.

**2. Threat / danger**
The orienting response to threat is faster than to anything else. Rare/involuntary/uncontrollable threats over-grab.

**3. Novelty and change**
> "Pavlov's dogs didn't salivate at the bell because of learning alone; they salivated because the bell represented change in their environment." (Ch 5)

ANY change is an attractor. New, different, unexpected, surprising.

**4. Self-referential cues**
"You." Your name. Information personally relevant to you. Self-referential stimuli automatically attract attention before the rest of the message.

### Magnetizers: 3 categories that hold attention

**1. The Zeigarnik Effect (unfinished tasks/information)**
> "Uncompleted tasks are remembered and attended to more readily than completed ones." (Ch 6, Beer Garden Waiter study)

Open loops hold attention. Cliffhangers, unanswered questions, partial reveals. The mind WANTS to close them; until closed, attention is captured.

**2. Mystery and the unknown**
Information presented as concealed, mysterious, "you won't believe what happens next." Holds attention longer than the same content presented plainly.

**3. Self-relevant continuation**
> "Personalized information holds attention more effectively than generic." Self-relevance both attracts (above) AND magnetizes. The "next-in-line effect" â€” anticipating personal relevance ahead keeps attention forward-directed.

### Attract â†’ Magnetize is the sequence

Most successful openers use BOTH:
- Headline: "What Happened When This [Self-Referential] Tried [Novel Thing]" â€” attractor.
- The story doesn't reveal the answer until the end â€” magnetizer.

Each alone is weaker. Attraction without magnetization = scrolled past. Magnetization without attraction = never noticed.

## How to apply

When designing an opener or hook:

1. **Pick an Attractor for the first second.**
   - Self-referential ("You", their name, "Are you a...")
   - Novelty/change ("What just happened with X")
   - Threat (carefully â€” overuse breeds desensitization)
   - Sexual (almost always overused and off-strategy except in specific contexts)

2. **Layer a Magnetizer for the next minute.**
   - Open a loop ("In the next 60 seconds I'll show you...")
   - Withhold the answer ("Here's what I learned... but first...")
   - Promise self-relevance ahead ("If you do X, this will save you Y")

3. **Close the loop eventually.** Magnetizers that NEVER close train cynicism (clickbait fatigue). The promise must pay off.

4. **Match attractor to magnetizer to content.** Bait-and-switch (sex attractor â†’ spreadsheet content) destroys trust faster than no attractor at all.

When evaluating ad creative or content:

1. **First-second attractor?** What's grabbing the eye?
2. **First-minute magnetizer?** What's keeping it past the initial pull?
3. **Resolution?** Does the loop close?

## Common failure modes

- **All attractor, no magnetizer.** Loud headline; thin content. Attention captured for 0.5 seconds, gone.
- **All magnetizer, no attractor.** Intriguing story buried after dull setup; nobody got to the intrigue.
- **Mismatch between hook and substance.** Sex-attractor on an article about taxes; threat-attractor on a feel-good product. The mismatch is detected within seconds.
- **Open loops that never close.** Trains the audience that your magnetizers are tricks; they stop responding.
- **Over-using threat.** Threat works powerfully once; fatigue sets in fast.

## Ethical guardrail

Attention commanders are content-agnostic â€” they grab and hold regardless of whether the underlying claim is true or false, useful or harmful. Three checks:

- **Promise = payoff?** If the attractor or magnetizer promises X, the content delivers X. Bait-and-switch is manipulation.
- **Proportional intensity.** Threat attractors that overstate the danger (manipulative health/security marketing) cross a line.
- **Substantive content under the hook.** Strong hooks attached to thin content waste audience time; common but corrosive.
