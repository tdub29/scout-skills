# Claude Hopkins: Headlines

## When to use

Trigger when the headline fails at *selection* â€” wrong people read it, right people pass it by, or cleverness hides relevance. Concrete cues:

- High impressions, low engagement â€” body never gets a hearing
- Headline is a pun, riddle, or "blind" teaser unrelated to the offer
- Product serves a narrow audience but headline could mean anything
- User spends time on body copy but rushes the headline
- Same ad with headline variants shows wildly different returns (or user suspects it would)
- "People don't read ads" â€” Hopkins's answer: they don't read what doesn't interest them at a glance

Don't trigger for: body rewrite (`hopkins-reason-why-copy`), bullet specificity (`hopkins-scientific-claim-check`), service-first offers (`hopkins-offer-service`), or structured market tests (`hopkins-test-campaign`). This skill owns the *first gate* â€” who stops and reads.

Additional trigger contexts:

- CTR high but conversion terrible (wrong audience selected)
- Headline A/B test planned without appeal typing
- User loves a pun the data would kill
- Magazine-style "clever" campaign brief
- Persona doc exists but headline ignores persona selfish want
- Same headline across all segments
- User cites "people don't read ads" â€” Hopkins's rebuttal is selection failure

## The framework

### Core thesis

The salesman demands attention in person; the advertisement can be ignored. But the salesman wastes time on hopeless prospects he cannot pick out. The advertisement is read only by interested people who, by their own volition, study what we have to say.

**The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest.** You wish to talk to someone in a crowd â€” first you say "Hey there, Bill Jones" to get the right person's attention. Create a headline which hails those people only.

What you have will interest certain people only, for certain reasons. You care only for those people.

### Blind headlines vs. selective headlines

Perhaps a blind headline or clever conceit will attract many times as many. But they may consist mostly of impossible subjects for what you have to offer. The people you are after may never realize the ad refers to something they may want.

Hopkins's "Egyptian Psychology" example: an article stating a certain woman is the most beautiful in the city would intensely interest her and her friends â€” but neither would read it if the headline were "Egyptian Psychology."

So in advertising: misleading concealment wastes everything below the headline.

### News-headline logic

Headlines on ads are like headlines on news items. Nobody reads a whole newspaper â€” each reader picks by headlines. They don't want misleading headlines. The writing of headlines is one of the greatest journalistic arts â€” they either conceal or reveal an interest.

People are hurried. The average person worth cultivating has too much to read. They skip three-fourths of paid reading matter. They won't read your business talk unless you make it worth their while **and let the headline show it.**

### Selfish-appeal typing

People want economy, beauty, labor saving, good things to eat and wear â€” products that interest them more than anything else in the magazine â€” but they will never know it unless the headline or picture tells them.

On soap: "Keep Clean" â€” too commonplace. "No animal fats" â€” many don't care. "It floats" â€” might interest. Beauty/complexion angle â€” many times more. Automobile "good universal joint" â€” flat; "Sportiest of Sport Bodies" â€” outpulls fifty to one.

**The appeals we like best rarely prove best** â€” we don't know enough people to average their desires. Learn by experiment. But back of all lie fixed principles: go after your percentage and strike the chord that responds.

### Keyed returns as headline laboratory

The identical ad run with various headlines differs tremendously in returns. A change multiplies returns five to ten times. Keyed returns on nearly two thousand headlines on a single product â€” story nearly identical, returns vary enormously. Each keyed return records the headline used. Learn what proportion of ads should attract each class of appeal.

When using twenty magazines, may use twenty separate ads â€” circulations overlap; different people respond to different approaches. Reach them all.

### Hours on headlines (Hopkins work ethic)

"The writer of this chapter spends far more time on headlines than on writing. He often spends hours on a single headline. Often scores of headlines are discarded before the right one is selected."

Agent implication: headline work is **not** a quick wrap on body copy. Generate scores, discard freely, select by selfish appeal fit and keyed hypothesis â€” not by writer taste.

### "People do not read advertisements" â€” Hopkins's rebuttal

Silly, Hopkins says â€” keyed returns show enormous readership (20% clipping coupons in some cases). They don't read **what doesn't interest them at a glance**. Failure is mis-selection, not universal ad blindness.

### Razors, rouge, corsets (exclusion examples)

Don't advertise corsets to men and children. Razors won't attract women. Rouge won't interest men. **Address the people you seek, and them only** â€” exclusion is a feature, not a limitation.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Define the sought reader.** Who is "Bill Jones" for this product? Not demographics alone â€” the selfish motive (beauty, economy, speed, status, fear, comfort). If corsets, men and children aren't the audience. If cigars, non-smokers aren't.

2. **Audit the current headline:**
   - *Selective:* names or strongly signals the sought reader and their motive
   - *Blind:* clever, abstract, or entertaining â€” could apply to many unrelated interests
   - *Wrong audience:* attracts readers who cannot buy or don't care
   - *Misleading:* promises an interest the body doesn't deliver

3. **Apply the glance test.** Would the right reader know in one second this ad is for them? Would the wrong reader pass? Hopkins: they decide at a glance â€” by headline or pictures.

4. **Generate appeal-typed alternatives (5â€“10 minimum).** For each, label the selfish chord: economy, beauty, speed, health, sport, convenience, etc. Avoid interchangeable platitudes ("Quality you deserve").

5. **Reject blind cleverness.** Score each alternative: Does it reveal or conceal interest? Discard high-traffic/low-fit options Hopkins warns about.

6. **Coordinate with picture (if present).** Headline and picture must tell the selfish story to the sought reader. A women's dress double spread gains no glance from a man.

7. **Rank for keyed test.** Prioritize variants that differ in appeal type, not synonym shuffling. Hopkins's lesson: story identical, headline drives multiplier.

8. **Produce the deliverable:**
   - Current headline diagnosis (selective/blind/wrong/misleading)
   - Appeal-typed alternative list
   - Test priority ranking with hypothesis (which chord should win for this line)
   - "Do not run" list (clever blind headlines)

9. **Sanity-check with the Bill Jones test.** Read only the headline to someone matching the prospect profile. Do they say "that's for me" or "what's that about?"

## Common failure modes

- **Cleverness for amusement.** People don't read ads for entertainment. A blind headline wins curiosity from people who will never buy.

- **Assuming the body will explain the headline.** If the headline doesn't select, the best body never gets read. Hopkins spends hours on headlines because the entire return depends on attracting the right readers.

- **Writer's taste vs. reader desire.** "The appeals we like best will rarely prove best." Personal preference is not a headline strategy â€” keyed experiment is.

- **One headline for every channel.** Overlapping circulations still contain different appeal sensitivities. Twenty magazines may need twenty headlines on the same story.

- **Feature headlines to the wrong audience.** Universal joints vs. sport bodies â€” technical features fail when the selfish chord is identity or appearance.

- **Platitude headlines.** "Keep Clean" on soap â€” too commonplace to select. Specificity in headlines often means a definite selfish hook (coordinate with `hopkins-scientific-claim-check` for figure-led headlines).

- **Picture contradicts headline.** Double-page dress ad to men â€” zero glance. Selection fails in visual channel too.

- **Confusing with `schwartz-*` headline skills.** Schwartz addresses market sophistication and desire channels. Hopkins addresses **selection** â€” hailing the right person. Run Hopkins when the wrong people read; run Schwartz when the right people read but desire stage demands a different promise type.

### The selection funnel (Hopkins logic)

```
All readers who see the ad
        â†“ headline + picture filter

        â†“ body + reason-why
```

Every leak at the headline stage wastes everything below. Hopkins: the best salesmanship has no chance unless we get a hearing.

### Appeal typing worksheet

When generating alternatives, force each headline into one primary chord (Hopkins soap/auto examples):

| Chord | When it wins | Weak when |
|---|---|---|
| Economy | Price-sensitive, commodity | Premium/luxury positioning |
| Beauty / complexion | Personal appearance products | B2B industrial |
| Labor saving | Appliances, tools | Emotional/status buys |
| Sport / identity | Autos, fashion | Feature-only buyers |
| Health / prevention | Foods, remedies | Requires proof in body |
| Novelty / curiosity | "It floats" â€” mild intrigue | Becomes blind if too abstract |
| Specific feat | 78-second shave | No substantiation |

Run multiple chords in parallel keyed tests â€” not preference debates.

### Keyed multiplier evidence

Hopkins records:

- Identical story, different headlines â†’ **5 to 10Ã—** return difference
- ~**2,000** headlines on one product, story nearly identical
- Each keyed return logs the headline â€” building a **headline laboratory** over time

Your audit should recommend **logging headline variant in every keyed response** â€” without that, learning stops.

### Picture + headline coupling

Headlines don't work alone:

- Double-page women's dress ad â†’ man skips (wrong audience)
- Shaving cream ad â†’ woman skips
- Picture and headline must **jointly** reveal selfish interest

Audit both channels when visuals exist. A selective headline with a generic stock photo still fails the glance test.

### Digital headline notes

- **Email subject lines:** Same Bill Jones rule â€” "Hey [role/pain]" beats internal campaign name.
- **Paid search:** Query intent is pre-selection; headline must match selfish want exactly or CTR is hollow.
- **Social:** Blind curiosity drives cheap clicks, expensive unqualified sessions â€” Hopkins's warning amplified.
- **SEO titles:** Selection vs clickbait â€” blind SEO titles attract bounces like blind print headlines.

## Extended failure modes (agent checklist)

- **Internal campaign name in subject.** "Project Sunrise launch" â€” blind to buyers; use selfish want.
- **Segment mismatch.** Headline for "CEOs" on a practitioner product â€” Bill Jones wrong person.
- **Curiosity gap abuse.** "You won't believe..." â€” attracts clickers who won't buy.
- **Feature headline to emotional buyer.** Universal joint vs sport body â€” chord error.
- **Localized headline, national waste.** One headline for all geos when selfish wants differ regionally.
- **Winning headline never logged.** Repeat discovery failure next campaign â€” build headline library with keyed results.
