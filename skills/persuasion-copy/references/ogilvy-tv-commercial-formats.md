# Ogilvy: TV Commercial Formats

## When to Use

Use this when choosing a television or video ad format, reviewing a storyboard, or diagnosing why a commercial is memorable but not persuasive.

Trigger when the user asks what kind of commercial to make, whether a TV concept can sell, how to structure a demo/problem-solution/testimonial spot, how to use emotion without losing the reason why, or how to make a video ad clearer and more product-focused.

Do not trigger for finished scriptwriting or ad variants; route execution to `copywriting` or `ad-creative`. Do not trigger for formal tests or pilots; route to `hopkins-test-campaign`. Do not trigger for claim proof or headline-only issues; route to `hopkins-scientific-claim-check` or `hopkins-headline-audit`.

## Framework

Ogilvy ranks TV formats by their ability to change brand preference, not by awards or recall.

Above-average formats:

1. Humor, but only if the writer can actually be funny.
2. Slice of life, where a doubter is converted in a realistic product situation.
3. Testimonials, especially loyal users defending the product in credible, amateur language.
4. Demonstrations that show product performance.
5. Problem-solution: familiar problem, product solves it.
6. Talking heads, especially for new products.
7. Characters that become relevant long-term symbols of the product.
8. Reason why, when a rational mechanism or process can persuade.
9. News, especially for new products or new uses.
10. Emotion, provided the product can deliver it and a rational excuse is included.

Below-average traps:

- Celebrity testimonials that make people remember the celebrity and forget the product.
- Cartoons for adults.
- Musical vignettes that entertain without selling.

## How to Apply

1. Define the product promise and desired brand-preference change.
2. Pick the format that best fits the product truth: demo, problem-solution, reason why, testimonial, news, emotion, or character.
3. Check brand identification: name in the first ten seconds, repeated naturally, package shown, and product in use.
4. Check attention: open with the fire, use visual surprise, and avoid banal scenes.
5. Check comprehension: one clear promise, supers that exactly match spoken words, on-camera voice when possible, and no confusing scene changes.
6. Check selling mechanics: show performance, end-result, package, rational excuse, sound effects where useful, and ordering information for direct response.
7. Check production discipline: remove actors, locations, music, animation, and celebrities that do not increase selling power.

## Output Format

Return a TV format audit:

- Recommended format: primary format and why it fits the promise.
- Avoided formats: tempting but weak choices.
- Storyboard fixes: brand ID, opening, product use, demonstration, supers, voice, package, clarity.
- Persuasion risks: celebrity memory, jingle words, visual banality, miscomprehension, overproduction.
- Cost discipline: what can be removed without hurting selling power.
- Next route: script execution to `copywriting` or `ad-creative`; formal testing to `hopkins-test-campaign`.

## Common Failure Modes

- Optimizing recall, awards, or entertainment instead of brand preference and sales.
- Opening dull and assuming the viewer will wait for the payoff.
- Letting the celebrity, music, or joke become the hero.
- Using demonstrations that are spectacular but unclear.
- Failing to name the brand early and often enough.
- Using supers that diverge from the spoken words.
- Spending production money that types or draws itself into the storyboard without selling more.
