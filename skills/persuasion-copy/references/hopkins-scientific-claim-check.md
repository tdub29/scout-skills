# Claude Hopkins: Being Specific

## When to use

Trigger when claims are *discountable* â€” the reader's brain treats them as sales talk, not facts. Concrete cues:

- Copy says "best," "pure," "lowest," "fastest," "quality," "amazing" without numbers
- Competitors all use the same platitudes and no one stands out
- User asks to make claims more credible or "scientific"
- Bullets list benefits without durations, counts, percentages, or test references
- A price or performance announcement lacks magnitude ("reduced" vs "reduced 25%")
- Compliance or brand team flagged unsubstantiated superlatives

Don't trigger for: overall ad length and palaver cuts (`hopkins-reason-why-copy`), who the headline hails (`hopkins-headline-audit`), trial/sample offer structure (`hopkins-offer-service`), or market tests before scaling (`hopkins-test-campaign`). This skill owns *claim granularity*.

Additional trigger contexts:

- Legal asks to "soften" claims â€” Hopkins softens by **specifying truth**, not by going vague
- Competitor ad uses "pure" / "best" and user wants differentiation without lying
- Bullet list of adjectives with no numbers
- Annual report marketing copy imported into consumer ads
- User says "sounds like marketing speak"
- FAQ answers full of generalities
- Product Hunt tagline is one vague superlative

## The framework

### Core thesis

Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. They leave no impression. To say "Best in the world," "Lowest prices in existence," etc. is at best claiming the expected â€” and superlatives of that sort are usually damaging. They suggest looseness, exaggeration, carelessness with truth. They lead readers to discount *all* statements you make.

People grant some license in selling talk â€” as in poetry â€” but general statements count for little. A man inclined to superlatives must expect every statement to be taken with caution.

A man who makes a **specific claim** is either telling the truth or a lie. People do not expect an advertiser to lie in the best mediums. Growing respect for advertising has come through growing regard for its truth. Definite statements are usually accepted. Actual figures are not generally discounted. Specific facts, when stated, have their full weight and effect.

In print at enormous cost, every word may cost $10 to insert. The weight of your claims matters. No generality has any weight whatever â€” like saying "How do you do?" with no intention of inquiring about health.

### The specificity multiplier

Hopkins's pattern: the weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific.

- Tungsten lamp "more light than carbon" â†’ doubt remains
- "Three and one-third times the light" â†’ readers realize tests and comparisons were made
- "Prices reduced" â†’ weak
- "Prices reduced 25 percent" â†’ full value of announcement
- "Lowest prices in America" â†’ copied until commonplace
- "Our net profit is 3 percent" â†’ definite; with volume, prices must be minimum

### First to specify wins

In beer, all advertised "Pure" until one brewer described plate-glass rooms, white-wood pulp filters, four-times-washed bottles, 4,000-foot water, 1,018 yeast experiments â€” mere essentials of ordinary brewing, but **first to tell** while others cried "pure beer." Greatest success in beer advertising.

Shaving soap: "Abundant lather," "Does not dry," "Acts quickly" â€” parity claims. New entrant: "Multiplies itself in lather 250 times," "Softens beard in one minute," "Maintains creamy fullness ten minutes," "Final result of testing 130 formulas" â€” sensational success in a difficult field.

Safety razors: "Quick shaves" vs **"78-second shave"** â€” definite, indicates tests, sensational advance.

Pattern: **specific beats vague even when the underlying advantage is the same.**

### Elastic vs. definite claims

"Used the world over" is elastic. "Used by the peoples of 52 nations" â€” many followed. One statement may take as much room as another; a definite statement is many times as effective. If a claim is worth making, make it in the most impressive way.

### Superlative license vs. weight

Hopkins grants that a salesman may say "Supreme in quality" without seeming a liar â€” poetry license â€” but **general statements count for little**. The man inclined to superlatives must expect **every statement taken with caution**. Specificity isn't only for compliance; it's for **weight per word** when copy is expensive.

### Automobile hidden-cost example

Hopkins's 9% profit on a $1,500 car â€” citing $735 in hidden costs without including visible ones â€” is a **transparency specificity** play. It reframed industry suspicion of excessive profits. Use when the objection is "you must be overcharging" rather than "you must be cheapest."

### Weight multiplication principle

"The weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific" â€” this is the operational formula. Audit each weak paragraph by asking: **what single number or named test would double the belief in this sentence?**

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Extract every claim.** Headlines, subheads, bullets, body, captions, footnotes. Include implied claims ("industry-leading," "trusted," "innovative").

2. **Classify each claim:**
   - *Platitude:* interchangeable with competitors; no test implied
   - *Vague comparative:* direction without magnitude ("more," "better," "faster")
   - *Specific:* numbers, durations, counts, named tests, dated results
   - *Unsubstantiated specific:* number present but no source â€” flag legal/ethical risk

3. **Apply the duck test.** Would this roll off without impression? If a competitor could paste the same line unchanged, it's a platitude.

4. **Apply the truth-or-lie test.** If you rewrote it with a specific figure, would the reader believe you'd be caught lying? That's the credibility zone Hopkins targets.

5. **Rewrite ladder â€” for each platitude/vague claim, escalate:**
   - Add magnitude (percent, multiple, seconds, minutes, count)
   - Add test reference (formulas compared, experiments, trials)
   - Add process detail that implies measurement (filters, washes, depths)
   - Add economic specificity (margin percent, cost breakdown) when pricing is the claim
   - Replace "pure/best/lowest" with the **first-to-tell** process story competitors share but don't say

6. **Check space efficiency.** Hopkins: one line can replace another with vastly different weight. Prefer one definite sentence over three vague ones.

7. **Produce the deliverable â€” claim audit table:**

   | Claim | Class | Credibility risk | Hopkins rewrite | Evidence needed |
   |-------|-------|------------------|-----------------|-----------------|

8. **Flag claims that cannot be supported.** Specificity obligates truth. Better a vague claim removed than a definite lie. Mark "do not publish without substantiation."

9. **Sanity-check with the discount test.** Read the ad as a skeptical buyer who has heard every competitor claim. Which lines still land as facts? If none, the audit isn't done.

## Common failure modes

- **Swapping one platitude for another.** "Premium quality" â†’ "unmatched excellence" is not Hopkins specificity. You need numbers, durations, or test trails.

- **Fake precision.** "78-second shave" works when tests exist. Invented decimals destroy ethos and legal standing.

- **Specific headline, vague body.** Headline specificity raises expectations the body must fulfill â€” coordinate with `hopkins-reason-why-copy`.

- **Burying the best figure.** Hopkins puts definitive claims where they carry weight â€” often early, always clearly. A proof point hidden in paragraph nine is half wasted.

- **Copying competitor specifics.** "52 nations" works once. The win is **first to tell** a true process or test story in your category, not cloning someone else's integer.

- **Superlative addiction.** "Best," "purest," "leading" â€” even with license, they train discounting. Replace with measurable superiority.

- **Ignoring the profit/specificity move.** "Our net profit is 3 percent" reframed price credibility when "lowest prices" failed. Economic specifics are Hopkins-valid when pricing is the objection.

- **Confusing with `schwartz-mechanization`.** Schwartz names and features a proprietary mechanism for belief in sophisticated markets. Hopkins adds **figures and tests** â€” complementary; run specificity first for raw credibility, mechanism naming when the market needs a handle.

### Platitude damage mechanism

Hopkins is explicit about the cascade:

1. General superlative appears ("best in the world")
2. Reader registers looseness / exaggeration / carelessness
3. Reader discounts **all** subsequent statements â€” even true ones
4. Ad becomes expensive noise at $10 per word

Specific claims interrupt the cascade because the reader assigns accountability â€” truth or lie, not poetry.

### Rewrite ladder (expanded)

For each weak claim, climb until you hit substantiation ceiling:

**Level 0 â€” Platitude:** "Pure beer" / "Great lather"  
**Level 1 â€” Vague comparative:** "More light" / "Faster shave"  
**Level 2 â€” Magnitude:** "25% more" / "Quick shave"  
**Level 3 â€” Definite measure:** "3â…“Ã— light" / "78-second shave"  
**Level 4 â€” Test trail:** "After 130 formulas..." / "1,018 yeast experiments"  
**Level 5 â€” Process story:** Plate-glass room, pulp filter, washed four times  

Stop at the highest level you can **prove**. Level 5 without evidence is worse than Level 0.

### Legal and ethics gate

Hopkins assumes reputable mediums â€” lying destroys the model. Before publishing Level 3+:

- Can you produce test records, accounting, or process documentation?
- Would a competitor sue for false comparative?
- Does the specific claim imply a guarantee the product can't meet?

Mark unsupported specifics **DO NOT PUBLISH** in the audit table.
