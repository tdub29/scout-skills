# Heinrichs: Commonplace Frame

## The Framework

> A commonplace is a shared attitude, belief, or value: one of the building blocks of persuasion.

Heinrichs uses commonplaces to show that arguments do not start from neutral facts. They start from what an audience already treats as common sense. Good framing makes your choice feel like the natural expression of those shared beliefs.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Start from the audience's common sense, not yours.**

What do they already believe is admirable, obvious, fair, practical, patriotic, modern, safe, or responsible?

**2. Define the terms before the opponent does.**

Frames decide what counts as the issue: "investment" vs "cost," "instruction" vs "manipulation," "accountability" vs "blame."

**3. Reframe by moving to a more favorable commonplace.**

If the current frame hurts you, shift to a shared value where your choice has advantage.

**4. Avoid frames that activate the wrong value.**

Some true claims make the wrong commonplace salient. Do not argue "efficiency" to an audience currently protecting dignity unless you first bridge to dignity.

## How To Apply

Build a commonplace frame map:

1. **Name the audience.**
2. **List their commonplaces.**
3. **List contested words or frames.**
4. **Identify the opponent/default frame.**
5. **Choose your favorable frame.**
6. **Write the reframe line.**
7. **Check for ethical distortion.**

## Common Failure Modes

- **Arguing from private values.** Your premise is not a commonplace unless the audience shares it.
- **Definition drift.** Letting key words change meaning mid-argument.
- **Frame negation.** Repeating the opponent's frame to deny it often strengthens it.
- **Clever reframe with no proof.** A frame opens the door; it does not replace evidence.
- **Manipulative reframing.** Renaming harm as "discipline" or extraction as "partnership" crosses the ethical line.
