# Heinrichs: Fallacy Foul Call

## The Framework

> The seven logical sins boil down to three: bad proof, bad conclusion, and disconnect between proof and conclusion. The biggest rhetorical foul is turning an argument into a fight.

Heinrichs is pragmatic about fallacies. In real argument, simply shouting "fallacy!" rarely persuades. The useful move is to classify the problem, decide whether it matters to the decision, and respond in a way that keeps the conversation deliberative.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Classify the logical sin.**

Bad proof: false comparison, bad example, ignorance as proof, tautology. Bad conclusion: false choice, red herring. Disconnect: slippery slope, post hoc/Chanticleer, proof that does not support the choice.

**2. Ask whether the fallacy blocks the decision.**

Some technically invalid appeals still move emotion. Call out only what distracts, misleads, or turns the argument into a fight.

**3. Reply rhetorically, not pedantically.**

Use a question, concession, reductio, or reframe. Preserve persuasion.

**4. Call a foul when the argument becomes domination.**

The major foul is not imperfect logic; it is shifting from winning over to winning against.

## How To Apply

Create a fallacy-foul response plan:

1. **Quote the claim.**
2. **Separate reason and conclusion using "because."**
3. **Classify the issue.**
4. **Decide response mode:** ignore, question, concede, reframe, or call foul.
5. **Draft the reply.**
6. **Shift back to future choice if needed.**

## Common Failure Modes

- **Pedant trap.** Winning the logic point while losing the audience.
- **Fallacy hunting as dominance.** Calling every imperfection becomes its own rhetorical foul.
- **Ignoring the emotional work.** A bad logical argument may still reveal a real fear or value.
- **False-choice surrender.** Accepting the offered options as the only options.
- **Post hoc overclaim.** Treating sequence as causation because it helps your side.
