# Heinrichs: Persuasion Detectors

## The Framework

Heinrichs's defense section teaches a habit: do not merely ask whether a claim is true. Ask how the persuader is trying to move you, what role they are performing, what they want, and whether the argument preserves your ability to choose.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Inspect ethos for virtue, disinterest, and practical wisdom.**

Does the persuader appear to share your values? Do they seem to benefit from your choice? Do they demonstrate judgment about the actual situation?

**2. Look for frame control.**

What definitions, commonplaces, or choices are being smuggled in? What is made to seem normal, urgent, shameful, patriotic, sophisticated, or inevitable?

**3. Separate emotion from compulsion.**

Good persuasion leaves you able to choose. Fear, shame, status threat, or bully tactics can bypass deliberation.

**4. Answer bullies by preserving choice and audience.**

Do not get dragged into point-scoring. Smile, ask questions, expose the tactic, or return to the decision.

## How To Apply

Build a persuasion-detector scorecard:

1. **Identify the desired action or belief.**
2. **Name the persuader's stake.**
3. **Audit ethos:** virtue, disinterest, practical wisdom.
4. **Audit frame/commonplaces.**
5. **Audit emotion:** what feeling is being raised, and does it fit?
6. **Check choice integrity:** are alternatives visible?
7. **Choose a response:** accept, ask, resist, reframe, or disengage.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Fact-check tunnel vision.** A message can use true facts inside a manipulative frame.
- **Ethos halo.** Liking the speaker makes their interest disappear from view.
- **Cynicism overcorrection.** Not every emotional appeal is manipulation.
- **Bully engagement.** Trying to out-argue someone whose real tactic is domination.
- **Missing hidden choices.** The strongest manipulation often lies in presenting two options when more exist.
