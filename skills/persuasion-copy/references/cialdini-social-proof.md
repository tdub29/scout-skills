## The framework

> "We view a behavior as more correct in a given situation to the degree that we see others performing it." (Influence, Ch. 4 â€” German ed.: *soziale BewÃ¤hrtheit*)

When deciding what to do, people look to what comparable others are doing and treat it as evidence of the right action. It's an efficient shortcut â€” usually we err less by following the crowd â€” which is exactly why it can be steered.

### Two amplifiers

1. **Uncertainty.** The more ambiguous the situation, the harder we lean on others' behavior. This produces **pluralistic ignorance**: in an emergency, each bystander reads the others' inaction as "no emergency," so no one acts (the diffusion-of-responsibility dynamic behind the Genovese case). A single clear cue ("you, in the blue jacket â€” call 911") collapses it.
2. **Similarity.** We copy people **like us** most. The Werther effect (spikes in imitative behavior after publicized cases) shows social proof flowing along lines of similarity. "People just like you chose X" beats generic popularity claims.

Used deliberately: canned laughter, "salted" tip jars and collection plates, "best-selling / fastest-growing / most popular," visible queues, and testimonials from similar customers.

## How to apply

To invoke it ethically:

1. **Show similar others already doing the desired thing** â€” peers, same role, same situation. Specific and comparable beats large and generic.
2. **Reduce uncertainty with a clear model.** When you want action, show one concrete instance of the right behavior rather than a vague norm.
3. **State the positive norm, not the violation.** "Most guests reuse their towels" works; "lots of people steal from this park" *teaches* the bad behavior by making it look common.

To detect it on yourself:

1. **Check whether the crowd actually has information you lack** â€” or whether everyone is just watching everyone else (pluralistic ignorance), or the proof was manufactured (paid reviews, fake scarcity of seats).
2. **Decouple "popular" from "correct."** Ask what the choice's merits are independent of how many others made it.

## Common failure modes

- **Negative social proof.** Broadcasting how many people do the wrong thing normalizes it and backfires.
- **Manufactured proof** (fake reviews, bot follower counts) â€” high blow-up risk and corrosive to trust once seen.
- **Mismatched referents.** Proof from dissimilar others barely moves people; the similarity channel is the point.
- **Relying on social proof in a genuine emergency** instead of assigning responsibility to a specific person.

## Ethical guardrail

Legitimate when the proof is true and the referents are genuinely comparable (real customers, real adoption). Manipulative when the evidence is fabricated or the crowd is engineered to imply a safety/correctness that doesn't exist.
