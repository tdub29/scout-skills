# Ogilvy: Advertising Homework

## When to Use

Use this when an advertising or campaign brief needs factual grounding before anyone writes copy, chooses a headline, or proposes a creative idea.

Trigger when the user asks to prepare an ad brief, research a product before writing ads, find the selling promise, avoid generic creative, interview customers for ad language, or diagnose why a campaign feels clever but unmoored.

Do not trigger for execution of ads or variants; route finished-writing work to `copywriting` or `ad-creative`. Do not trigger for a full market/ICP platform; route that to `brand-positioning`. Do not trigger for selective headline audits; route to `hopkins-headline-audit`. Do not trigger for proofing specific claims; route to `hopkins-scientific-claim-check`. Do not trigger for structured test-market pilots; route to `hopkins-test-campaign`.

## Framework

Ogilvy's premise is that successful advertising starts with homework. Without it, the writer skids on the "slippery surface of irrelevant brilliance."

1. Product study: learn the product until a surprising, concrete selling fact emerges. Ogilvy found the Rolls-Royce clock headline by reading technical material and built Mercedes, margarine, and Shell campaigns from product facts.
2. Competitive bearings: study what competitors have been saying, where they are strong or stale, and what claims have already been occupied.
3. Consumer research: learn how buyers think about the category, what language they use, what attributes matter, and what promise would most likely make them buy.
4. Direct contact if necessary: when formal research is unavailable, talk to a handful of real buyers yourself. The point is usable language and motives, not statistical grandeur.
5. Promise extraction: turn the strongest product fact plus buyer desire into a selling promise before moving to creative expression.

## How to Apply

1. Name the product, category, buyer, purchase situation, and business objective.
2. List product facts from the source material: mechanism, proof, ingredients, performance, price, origin, guarantees, limitations, and usage context.
3. Identify the fact that is surprising, useful, or uniquely credible.
4. Scan competitor claims and mark which promises are occupied, overused, or unsupported.
5. Extract consumer language: worries, category words, desired outcomes, objections, and buying triggers.
6. Draft the candidate promise in plain buyer language.
7. Mark missing evidence and research questions before allowing creative work to proceed.

## Output Format

Return a concise homework brief:

- Product facts that matter: concrete facts, ranked by selling potential.
- Competitive bearings: what competitors already claim and where openings remain.
- Consumer language: phrases, objections, desired outcomes, and decision criteria.
- Candidate promise: one main promise with supporting facts.
- Evidence gaps: what must be verified before execution.
- Next route: `copywriting` or `ad-creative` for execution, or a named specialist skill if the blocker is positioning, headline selection, claim proof, or testing.

## Common Failure Modes

- Starting with wordplay, visuals, or formats before the product fact is known.
- Treating desk research as enough when buyer language is missing.
- Asking consumers what ad they like instead of what they care about, fear, notice, and believe.
- Writing a broad promise that competitors can also claim.
- Preserving every fact instead of finding the one that can sell.
- Using this skill to write the campaign; it prepares the inputs and hands execution to `copywriting` or `ad-creative`.
