# Heath: Unexpected

## When to use

Trigger when the question is "how do I get and keep attention" â€” specifically the cognitive-mechanism layer, not just surface punch. Concrete cues:

- The user has a message that's important but boring (safety briefing, compliance update, financial disclosure)
- A presentation / talk / video / article that audiences abandon within 30 seconds or 200 words
- A complex topic that needs sustained attention across a long format
- The audience already has assumptions about the topic that are getting in the way
- The user is teaching something the audience thinks they already understand

Don't trigger for: sentence-level punch (use `prose-style` or `aristotle-style`), pure headline work (use `schwartz-headline-strengtheners`), or commercial conversion optimization (use `cro`). This skill is the cognitive mechanism â€” schema-violation + curiosity-gap â€” that operates *before* the audience even decides to engage with the content.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Heath's claim: getting attention and keeping attention are two distinct problems, solved by two distinct mechanisms.

- **Getting attention** is a one-shot move. The audience is in a steady state (scrolling, half-listening, expecting the usual), and you need to *interrupt* that state. The mechanism is *schema violation* â€” you break a mental pattern the audience was running on, and their brain pauses to figure out what just happened. The interrupt window is brief; you have it for seconds.

- **Keeping attention** is a sustained challenge. Once you have the audience's attention, you have to give them a reason to stay. The mechanism is the *curiosity gap* â€” you make them feel they're missing information they care about closing, and the felt-gap pulls them through the content. The Loewenstein insight: curiosity is *pain*, and closing the gap is *relief*.

The two work together. Schema violation gets the door open; the curiosity gap walks the audience through the room.

### Schema violation â€” the getting-attention mechanism

Humans run on schemas â€” pre-built mental patterns that let us skip over the predictable. When a pattern fits, attention doesn't need to engage. When a pattern breaks, attention is forced to engage because something must be re-evaluated.

Heath's canonical example: Karen Wood, a Southwest Airlines flight attendant, started a safety announcement with "If you wish to smoke, please use the wing â€” they have a nice movie out there." The schema "flight-safety briefings are boring and ignorable" broke. Passengers laughed and listened. The follow-up actual safety content landed.

**The relevance test.** Schema violation alone is not enough. The break must reinforce the core message, not distract from it. The "wolves ad" Heath cites â€” a striking ad with wolves running through a building â€” got attention but failed because nobody remembered what the ad was *for*. Gimmickry breaks patterns; rhetoric breaks patterns *in service of* the core.

**Three forms of schema violation:**

1. **Direct contradiction.** Audience expects X; you deliver not-X. "Most workplace injuries happen during the calm periods, not the busy ones."
2. **Tonal break.** Audience expects formal; you deliver humor (or vice versa). Karen Wood's joke in a safety briefing.
3. **Scale break.** Audience expects abstract; you deliver intimate (or vice versa). A national statistic delivered as one person's name and face.

**Decay risk.** Schema violations decay. Once an audience has seen the move twice, the pattern *includes* the move, and the violation no longer violates. This means schema breaks have to evolve â€” what surprised the audience last year is the schema this year.

### Curiosity gap â€” the keeping-attention mechanism

George Loewenstein's information-gap theory: curiosity arises when there's a gap between what we know and what we want to know, and the gap *feels* like an itch. Heath operationalizes this for communication.

**The gap requires prior knowledge.** You can't open a gap in a vacuum. The audience has to know enough about the topic to feel that something is missing. Roone Arledge's innovation in NCAA football broadcasts: before the game, give the audience the players' backstories and the rivalry context. Now the audience knows enough to *want to know* what happens next. Without the backstory, the game is just figures running on a field.

**Sequence over dump.** Once the gap is open, give the audience the answer in *pieces*, not all at once. A mystery novel doesn't dump the solution on page 5. A good speech plants a question early and resolves it late. The structural principle: tease the gap, parcel out clues, sustain the felt-need-to-close until you actually close it.

**Open before you close.** Heath's structural rule: state what the audience *doesn't yet know* before you give them what they should know. "There are five reasons most startups fail. The fourth one shocked even the founders we talked to." Now the audience can't disengage; they need to know reason four.

**Confront overconfidence.** Audiences who think they already know the answer feel no gap. The move: make them commit to a prediction or expose disagreement, then surprise them. "I asked twelve experts to predict X. They split 50/50. Here's what actually happened." Now the gap is alive because the audience has acknowledged uncertainty.

### Schema violation vs. curiosity gap â€” when each one

- **Schema violation** is for the *opening* â€” the first 5-30 seconds. One-shot, high-intensity, must resolve quickly into the content.
- **Curiosity gap** is for the *body* â€” sustained engagement across minutes or pages. Multi-shot, lower-intensity, structurally embedded.

A long-form piece needs both: schema violation in the hook, curiosity gap as the spine.

### Manipulation risk

Both mechanisms can be weaponized:

- **Schema violation as clickbait.** "You won't believe what happened next." The pattern-break is real, but the content doesn't deserve the attention. Standard clickbait pattern; audiences eventually learn to discount the violation.
- **Curiosity gap as bait-and-switch.** Open a felt-gap, then resolve it with content that doesn't actually answer the question. The audience invested attention; the resolution was a sales pitch, not the answer.
- **Manufactured urgency.** Schema-violating with "BREAKING" or "URGENT" framings on content that's neither. Trains the audience to ignore future urgency signals.
- **Surprise as cruelty.** Some schema-breaks are violent or shocking in ways that traumatize rather than engage. The "wolves ad" did this â€” surprising, but in a way that triggered defensive reactions rather than openness.

The honest version: surprise serves the *truth* of the content, not the *spread* of the content. Open gaps you can actually close.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

### For getting attention (schema violation)

1. **Identify the audience's current schema.** What does the audience *expect* this kind of communication to be? Boring? Routine? Predictable in a specific way? Name the schema in plain language: "They expect this safety briefing to be a recitation they can tune out."

2. **Generate schema-violation candidates.** Brainstorm 5+ ways to break that specific schema:
   - Direct contradiction of expected content
   - Tonal break (formal â†’ playful, or vice versa)
   - Scale break (abstract â†’ intimate)
   - Format break (paragraph â†’ image / silence / question)
   - Voice break (institutional â†’ personal)

3. **Apply the relevance test.** For each candidate, ask: does the break reinforce the core message, or just attract attention? If it doesn't reinforce, it's gimmickry â€” discard.

4. **Pick the candidate with highest tension between surprise and relevance.** Strong surprise + strong relevance = strong opening. Strong surprise + weak relevance = clickbait. Weak surprise + strong relevance = forgettable.

### For keeping attention (curiosity gap)

5. **Identify the audience's prior knowledge.** What do they already know about this topic? You can only open gaps within domains they understand at all. If the audience has zero context, build minimal context first.

6. **Find the genuine question.** What is the audience's natural unanswered question if they understood the topic better? Phrase it as a *felt gap*: "Why do most failures cluster in the second year, not the first?"

7. **Design the open.** State the gap explicitly near the start. "Most people think X. The data shows something different." Now the audience has a question they want answered.

8. **Sequence the clues.** Decide the path from open-gap to resolution. What's the first piece of evidence? The second? Where's the "huh, that's not what I expected" moment that re-energizes mid-piece curiosity? Where's the resolution?

9. **Confront overconfidence (optional but powerful).** Where the audience thinks they already know the answer, force them to acknowledge they might not â€” predictions, surveys, disagreement among experts. The gap only works if the audience feels it.

10. **Resolve genuinely.** The close has to deliver the answer the open promised. Bait-and-switch breaks audience trust permanently.

### Manipulation-risk check (run for both)

- Is the schema violation in service of the content's actual truth?
- Is the curiosity gap one you can honestly close, or are you leading the audience somewhere they didn't sign up for?
- Are you surprising the audience to help them, or to extract their attention?

### Produce the two-stage plan

- **Hook (schema violation):** the opening line / image / scene / move + the relevance link to the core
- **Spine (curiosity gap):** the felt-gap statement + the clue sequence + the resolution
- **Risk flags:** any traps the user should consciously avoid

## Common failure modes

- **Gimmickry.** Schema violation that doesn't reinforce the core. The audience remembers the surprise and forgets the content. The fix: pass the relevance test before committing to the move.

- **Misreading the schema.** Building a violation against a schema the audience doesn't actually hold. The break lands flat because the pattern wasn't there in the first place. Test by asking real audience members: "What do you expect when you hear about X?"

- **Surprise decay.** A violation that worked last year is the new normal this year. If audiences have seen "shocking opener" 20 times, it doesn't shock anymore. Iterate the move.

- **Opening a gap and closing it instantly.** "Why do startups fail? Capital reasons. Now let me tell you about our product." The gap was opened and closed in 8 seconds â€” no sustained tension. Stretch the resolution; let the gap pull through more content.

- **Opening a gap the audience doesn't care about.** "Did you know X about Y?" Audience: "I never thought about Y; I don't feel a gap." Build context first if the audience doesn't have the prior knowledge to feel the missing piece.

- **Sustaining a gap too long.** Cliffhanger fiction can do this; non-fiction can't. After 20 minutes of "the answer is coming," the audience disengages. Resolve in proportion to the attention demanded.

- **False resolution.** The close doesn't actually answer the open. Audience trust drops; next time the same audience encounters your communication, they discount the opening promise. One bait-and-switch can poison subsequent attention.

- **Confusing surprise with shock.** Shock breaks the schema but in a way that triggers defenses rather than openness. The audience's brain doesn't go "huh, interesting" â€” it goes "ugh, I'm out." The wolves ad is the canonical example.

- **Schema violation in a context that has no schema.** A first-time conversation has no established pattern to break. Trying to schema-violate someone you've never communicated with before is just unpredictability without contrast.

- **Skipping the relevance test in the name of memorability.** "Even if they don't remember the content, they'll remember the brand." Sometimes true for advertising; usually false for content that needs to *do* something. If the surprise doesn't deliver the message, the message didn't land.
