# Heinrichs: Decorum Fit

## The Framework

> Decorum means fit: an agreeable ethos matches the audience's expectations for tone, appearance, manners, and values. (Chapter 5)

Heinrichs's decorum is not politeness. It is rhetorical fit. You persuade people insofar as you can talk their language: speech, gesture, tonality, order, image, attitude, and idea.

### Three Sub-Rules

**1. Fit the audience's expectations without caricaturing them.**

Do not baby-talk to children or cosplay a group you do not belong to. Fit means making the audience comfortable enough to hear you.

**2. Build rhetorical virtue from their values.**

You look "good" in the audience's eyes when you embody what they value: independence, scholarship, profit, fairness, courage, service, precision, loyalty.

**3. Avoid indecorum that distracts from the choice.**

An outfit, joke, tone, slide style, or phrase can become the argument if it violates the room's norms.

**4. Adjust by audience and occasion.**

The same deck may need different decorum for executives, peers, frontline staff, donors, regulators, or teenagers.

## How To Apply

Run a decorum-fit audit:

1. **Name the audience and occasion.**
2. **List the audience's visible norms and values.**
3. **Define the speaker role they will trust.**
4. **Identify fit choices:** language, tone, dress/visual style, evidence, pacing.
5. **Identify misfit risks.**
6. **Choose one credibility move:** character reference, tactical flaw, practical wisdom, or value alignment.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing decorum with manners.** Politeness can still be indecorous if it does not fit the room.
- **Pandering.** Overfitting becomes obvious and destroys ethos.
- **Personal authenticity absolutism.** "I'm just telling it like it is" can be persuasive self-sabotage.
- **One-audience blindness.** A message that fits leadership may alienate implementers.
- **Virtue mismatch.** Leading with profit in a scholarship-valuing culture, or leading with ideals in a hard-nosed cost meeting.
