## The framework

> "We should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us." (Influence, Ch. 2 â€” German ed.: *Gegenseitigkeit*)

The reciprocation rule is one of the most overpowering norms in human culture: a favor, gift, concession, or piece of information creates a felt obligation to return one. The obligation is triggered even when the initial favor was **uninvited**, and even when what's returned is far larger than what was given.

### Three levers that make it work

1. **Uninvited debt.** You don't have to want the favor to feel the debt. The Hare Krishna flower (pressed into a hand, then a donation requested) and the free sample both work by installing an obligation the target never asked for.
2. **Asymmetric repayment.** Because the discomfort of feeling indebted is unpleasant, people often repay *more* than they received to discharge it. In Regan's classic study, a stranger who bought someone an unrequested Coke later sold them far more raffle tickets than the favor was worth.
3. **Reciprocal concessions (rejection-then-retreat / door-in-the-face).** A concession obligates a counter-concession. Open with a larger request that's refused, then "retreat" to the smaller one you wanted; the retreat reads as a concession the target feels pressure to match â€” and it raises compliance *and* the target's satisfaction and sense of responsibility for the outcome.

## How to apply

When you want to invoke reciprocity ethically:

1. **Give first, and give real.** A genuine, useful, unexpected gift (information, help, a connection) before any ask. Token or transparently strategic gifts read as bribes.
2. **Make the gift meaningful, customized, and unexpected** â€” Cialdini's three amplifiers. A personalized favor outperforms a generic one many times over.
3. **Use door-in-the-face honestly:** the larger first request must be plausible, not absurd. Then retreat to the genuine ask. The concession does the work.
4. **Don't erase the gift.** When thanked, "I know you'd do the same for me" preserves the norm better than "it was nothing," which discharges the obligation you may later hope is reciprocated.

When detecting reciprocity used on you:

1. **Separate the gift from the request.** A free sample is a sales tactic, not a gift; you may enjoy it and still owe nothing. Redefining a "favor" as the sales device it is releases the obligation.
2. **Watch for the manufactured concession.** If a first offer was never serious, the "concession" to the second is theater, not generosity.

## Common failure modes

- **Transparent quid pro quo.** A gift visibly given to obligate triggers resistance, not debt.
- **Over-giving then over-asking.** A small favor used to extract a wildly disproportionate return crosses into manipulation and is felt as such.
- **Discharging your own leverage** by waving off thanks.
- **Door-in-the-face with an absurd anchor** â€” the target rejects the whole exchange as a stunt.

## Ethical guardrail

Reciprocity is legitimate when the initial favor has real value and the return is proportionate. It becomes exploitation when (a) the "gift" is engineered solely to obligate, (b) the requested return is disproportionate to what was given, or (c) the target would feel used if they saw the sequence laid out.
