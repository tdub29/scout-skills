## The framework

> "Whatever captures a person's attention is likely to loom large in that person's assessment of causality." (Ch 3â€“4)

Two propositions, both demonstrated:
- **Channeled attention = perceived importance.** People assess importance from where attention is directed.
- **Channeled attention = perceived causality.** Whatever's focal feels causal. The thing in the spotlight is given credit (or blame) for the outcome.

### Demonstrations

- **The wallpaper studies:** Subjects shown furniture against a clouds-pattern wallpaper rated comfort as more important when buying a sofa. Against a pennies-pattern, rated price more important. The background shaped the foreground judgment.
- **The line-cutting interrogation studies:** Viewers shown a video of a confession from a single camera angle (focused on the suspect) judged the confession voluntary. Viewers shown the same from a different angle (focused on the detective) judged it coerced. Same content; different focal element.
- **The "Romance of Leadership" effect:** Stock returns are largely determined by market conditions. CEOs are focal in the narrative; investors assign them outsized credit for performance they didn't drive.

### Three named sub-mechanisms

**1. Agenda-Setting**
> "The media tells us not what to think, but what to think about." (Ch 3)

Whoever controls what's on the news's agenda controls what feels important. Same with PowerPoint slides, dashboards, meeting agendas.

**2. Background â†’ Foreground Substrate**
The background of attention is doing work even when it isn't the focus. Wallpaper, ambient music, environmental cues all become substrate for foreground judgments.

**3. Single-Chute Causality**
When attention is delivered via a single channel (one camera angle, one expert opinion, one chart), that channel determines the causal story. Multiple channels reduce the bias.

## How to apply

When designing communications:

1. **Identify what you want to feel central.** What's the focal point you want the audience to assign importance to?
2. **Channel attention there.** Visually (size, color, position), structurally (first, last, repeated), narratively (the protagonist of the story).
3. **Manage what's NOT focal carefully.** Backgrounds, ambient frames, peripheral cues â€” these become substrate. Don't ignore them; design them.
4. **For honest persuasion: make focal what's actually most important.** For manipulation: make focal what helps your case while hiding what doesn't.

When evaluating communications:

1. **Notice what's focal.** What did the design choose to spotlight?
2. **Ask: what's NOT focal?** What's been pushed to background or omitted?
3. **Decouple importance from focality.** Just because it's spotlighted doesn't mean it's actually most important.
4. **Seek other angles deliberately.** Single-chute attention reliably distorts causality.

## Common failure modes

- **Channeling on the wrong element.** Spotlighting a feature your audience doesn't care about; the importance-elevation lands on an irrelevance.
- **Believing your audience sees the whole picture.** They see what you put in the foreground. Always.
- **Treating focal in the design as focal in their head.** What's focal to YOU as the designer (because you've stared at it) isn't necessarily what's focal to a fresh viewer's eyes.
- **Falling for it yourself.** When evaluating, asking only "is this true?" â€” instead of "what got channeled to make me believe this is true?"

## Ethical guardrail

Channeled attention is one of the higher-leverage manipulation surfaces. Three checks:

- **Are the focal elements actually material?** Spotlighting irrelevant flattering features while hiding material drawbacks crosses into deception.
- **Are alternative views accessible?** Honest channeling acknowledges that other framings exist; manipulative channeling pretends the focal frame is the only one.
- **Would the audience endorse the channeling if they saw the choices behind it?** The transparency test.
