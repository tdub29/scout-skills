# Aristotle: Forensic Analysis

## When to use

Trigger when the question is "what happened, who did it, why, and what should follow from it?" â€” past-oriented argument. Concrete cues:

- A legal case (defense or prosecution) requiring motive and state-of-mind analysis
- An incident post-mortem ("why did the outage happen / who's responsible")
- An accountability conversation with a team member or partner about past conduct
- An HR or compliance investigation into alleged wrongdoing
- A regulatory filing or response about past behavior
- A journalism piece making a case that someone did something they shouldn't have
- A board-level discussion about accountability for past outcomes
- A personal dispute where the question is "what actually happened and who's at fault"
- A fraud/embezzlement/security-incident investigation

Don't trigger for: future-oriented decisions (route to deliberative-genre skills), generic persuasion (use `aristotle-three-modes-of-persuasion`), ceremonial praise/blame (route to `aristotle-three-genres` epideictic mode), or live legal practice requiring jurisdiction-specific knowledge (this is the rhetorical framework, not legal advice).

The boundary with `aristotle-three-genres`: that skill classifies a piece as forensic. This skill operationalizes forensic-genre arguments once that classification is made.

## The framework

### Core thesis

Forensic rhetoric â€” arguments about past wrongdoing â€” turns on three analytical layers, each with its own evidentiary requirements and argumentative moves. Aristotle treats wrongdoing not as a black box ("did they do it?") but as a decomposable phenomenon: every wrongful act has a *motive*, requires certain *states of mind* in the actor, and typically follows a *victim-selection pattern*. By systematically analyzing all three, a forensic argument can be either constructed (to establish wrongdoing) or refuted (to undermine the case).

The skill's posture: it produces arguments, not verdicts. Whether the actor *actually* did the act is a question of evidence; this skill helps structure the argument about what the evidence shows.

### Layer 1: Wrongdoing motives â€” the seven causes

Aristotle catalogs all human action under seven causes. For forensic argument, three are *non-volitional* (and tend toward exculpation) and four are *volitional* (and tend toward culpability, in varying degrees).

**Non-volitional (mitigating):**

1. **Chance.** Accidental, no intent. The act happened but the actor neither chose nor expected it. Argues toward innocence or reduced culpability. (Defense move: "This was an accident; no reasonable person could have anticipated this outcome.")

2. **Nature.** The actor's inherent disposition. Innate temperament that produces the action without deliberate choice. Mitigates because it implies diminished agency â€” though modern frameworks often treat habitual disposition as still culpable (the actor could have sought help / changed). Aristotle leans more toward partial exculpation.

3. **Compulsion.** External force or coercion. The actor was forced by circumstances they couldn't control. Strong mitigation; classical "duress" defense.

**Volitional (aggravating, with degrees):**

4. **Habit.** Repeated behavior become automatic; the actor wasn't deliberately choosing in the moment but has chosen the pattern over time. Aggravates because the actor is responsible for the pattern, even if not for the specific instance.

5. **Reasoning.** Calculated, deliberate decision; cost-benefit analysis applied to wrongdoing. The most aggravating cause â€” premeditation. Prosecution's strongest motive claim.

6. **Anger.** Passionate response to a real or perceived offense. Partial mitigation in classical and modern frameworks because the actor's deliberative faculty was compromised. Crime of passion vs. premeditation.

7. **Appetite.** Desire for pleasure or aversion to pain. Self-interested motive. Aggravating, especially when the appetite served by the act is base (greed, lust) rather than essential (hunger).

The forensic move is to **classify the alleged act by motive** and use the classification to support the argument:
- Prosecution: argue reasoning or appetite (premeditation, self-interest)
- Defense: argue chance, compulsion, or anger (accident, force, understandable passion)

Most real acts have *multiple* motives. The argumentative question is which dominates.

### Layer 2: States of mind that enable wrongdoing

Aristotle identifies three psychological *preconditions* for wrongdoing. All three typically have to be present â€” remove any one and the wrongdoing becomes less likely or impossible.

1. **Perceived possibility of success.** The actor believes the act is *achievable* â€” they have the means, the target is vulnerable, the conditions are right. Without this belief, no rational actor attempts. (Defense move: "The accused could not have believed this was achievable.")

2. **Belief in escape from punishment.** The actor believes they can avoid detection or, if detected, avoid consequences. Either invisibility or impunity. Crimes committed in public against well-defended targets imply this belief was wrong â€” but it had to be present at the time. (Defense move: "The crime occurred under conditions where escape was visibly impossible; the accused would not have believed escape achievable.")

3. **Advantage exceeds penalty (cost-benefit).** The actor calculates that the gain from wrongdoing exceeds the expected cost (probability of penalty Ã— severity of penalty). Petty theft for small gain when facing minor penalty: plausible. Petty theft for small gain when facing life imprisonment: implausibly irrational. (Defense move: "The advantage gained could not rationally outweigh the penalty risked; the accused would not have made this calculation.")

These are *subjective* states â€” what the actor believed at the time, not what was true. The forensic move is to argue from evidence (admissions, prior conduct, situational facts) what the actor *plausibly* believed.

### Layer 3: Victim profiles â€” targeting patterns

Aristotle observes that criminals select victims strategically based on vulnerability. The common profiles:

- **The wealthy.** Motive: gain. The target has resources worth taking.
- **The high-reputation.** Motive: cover. A high-status victim is less likely to be believed if they complain about an unusual or socially awkward act.
- **The careless / inattentive.** Motive: ease. Low detection risk.
- **The lacking courage / agency.** Motive: low retaliation risk. Victim unlikely to fight back or pursue justice.
- **The friendless / isolated.** Motive: no advocates. No one will champion the victim.
- **The previously victimized.** Motive: known weakness. The actor knows from prior episodes that the victim responds passively.

The forensic move is to **check victim-fit**:
- Does the alleged victim match the profile a perpetrator of this kind would target? If yes, the targeting pattern *supports* the prosecution case. If no, it weakens it. ("Why would someone seeking easy gain target a publicly defended high-witness environment?")
- Conversely, for the defense: if the alleged victim *strongly* fits a targeting profile but the accused has no demonstrated pattern of targeting that profile, the accused-victim link is weak.

**Critical guardrail:** victim profiling is for understanding *targeting* (perpetrator's logic), NOT for victim-blaming. The fact that a victim was careless does not make them at fault for being victimized â€” it explains why the perpetrator selected them. Treat victim-profile analysis as evidence about the perpetrator, not as moral judgment about the victim.

### Manipulation risk

Forensic rhetoric is the genre most susceptible to manipulation because the audience is often emotionally engaged ("someone did wrong; someone must pay") and not always positioned to verify evidence. Specific risks:

1. **Motive speculation.** Asserting reasoning (premeditation) without evidence of planning. Asserting appetite without evidence of gain. The motive claim should be *evidence-grounded*; absent evidence, the argument is speculation dressed as analysis.

2. **State-of-mind projection.** Claiming the accused "must have believed" they could succeed / escape / profit without showing why we know that. Subjective states require evidence (admissions, prior behavior, situational facts).

3. **Victim-blaming via profile analysis.** Using the profile framework to argue the victim "asked for it." This is a misuse of the tool. Victim profile illuminates perpetrator logic, not victim culpability.

4. **Selective evidence.** Forensic argument is one-sided by genre (advocacy, not neutral inquiry). The ethical move is to acknowledge the strongest counter-argument before refuting it, not to pretend it doesn't exist.

The skill bakes these guardrails into every forensic-argument plan.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Confirm this is a forensic-genre question.** Is the audience being asked to judge a past action (not decide a future course, not honor a present virtue)? If yes, proceed. If no, route to the appropriate genre skill.

2. **Define the alleged act precisely.** Who is alleged to have done what to whom, when, and how. Vague allegations produce vague arguments. Pin down the specifics.

3. **Classify the dominant motive.** Walk the seven causes (chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reasoning, anger, appetite). Which best explains the act, given available evidence? Most acts have multiple causes; identify the dominant one and the supporting ones. For each candidate, note the evidence that supports or undermines that motive.

4. **Assess the three states of mind.**
   - *Possibility:* would the accused, given what they knew at the time, have plausibly believed this act was achievable? What evidence?
   - *Escape:* would they have plausibly believed they could avoid detection or consequence? What evidence?
   - *Advantage > penalty:* would the calculation have plausibly favored acting? What was the actual or perceived advantage; what was the actual or perceived penalty?
   
   Each precondition can be argued in the affirmative (supports the wrongdoing-occurred case) or negative (undermines it).

5. **Run the victim-fit check.** Does the alleged victim match a pattern this kind of perpetrator typically targets? Note: this is about understanding the perpetrator's logic, not blaming the victim. The check is evidentiary, not moral.

6. **Run the manipulation-risk gate.**
   - Are motive claims evidence-based or speculative?
   - Are state-of-mind claims evidence-based or projected?
   - Is the victim-profile analysis being used to understand targeting, or sliding into victim-blame?
   - Has the strongest counter-argument been acknowledged?
   
   If any check fails, redesign the argument honestly.

7. **Construct the forensic argument.** Produce a structured analysis:
   - **Motive layer:** dominant cause + supporting causes + evidence
   - **State-of-mind layer:** the three preconditions + evidence for each + the implications if any were absent
   - **Victim-fit layer:** profile match (or non-match) + what it implies about the perpetrator's logic
   - **Counter-argument acknowledgment:** the strongest argument against this analysis + the response

8. **Pressure-test.** Read the argument aloud as if you were the opposing rhetor. Where's it weakest? Where's the evidence thinnest? Where could the manipulation guardrails be tightened? Iterate.

## Common failure modes

- **Speculating motive without evidence.** "The accused was clearly motivated by greed" â€” without proof of expected gain. Motive claims are *evidentiary*, not free-form. If you can't point to evidence, you're projecting.

- **Treating one motive as exclusive.** Real human action usually has multiple causes â€” anger + reasoning + appetite all contributing. Naming a single motive cleanly is rhetorically efficient but may overstate certainty. Acknowledge complexity where it exists.

- **Confusing capacity with intent.** "They had the means" does not establish they used them. State-of-mind analysis bridges from capacity to belief-and-decision, but each step requires its own evidence.

- **Projecting modern rationality onto the actor.** "No rational person would have believed they could escape" â€” but the accused may have *miscalculated*. Most criminals overestimate escape probability. Aristotle's framework is about what the actor *plausibly believed* at the time, not what was objectively true.

- **Using victim profiles to blame the victim.** This is the most common ethical failure. Victim-fit analysis answers "why did this perpetrator pick this victim?" not "why was the victim at fault?" If your analysis is sliding toward the latter, stop.

- **Cherry-picking evidence for motive.** The forensic genre is adversarial â€” but the ethical version still acknowledges the strongest counter-evidence. Hiding it doesn't make it disappear; it just makes the argument fragile when the counter-evidence surfaces.

- **Confusing this skill with legal advice.** This is the rhetorical-argument framework, not jurisdiction-specific legal doctrine. Real legal practice requires knowing the actual statutes, precedents, and procedures in the relevant jurisdiction. Use this skill to *structure* forensic argument; consult an actual lawyer for *legal* questions.

- **Applying this skill to non-forensic contexts.** Trying to do "motive analysis" on a forward-looking decision is genre confusion. Route to `aristotle-three-genres` to confirm the piece is actually forensic before reaching for this skill.

- **Treating the motive list as exhaustive of modern psychology.** Aristotle's seven causes are a useful starting taxonomy but don't capture every modern motive category (e.g., addiction-driven action is not cleanly any of the seven). Use the framework as a structural prompt; don't pretend it's a complete psychological model.

- **Skipping the manipulation-risk gate.** "I'm advocating, so why should I steelman the opposition?" Because the audience will eventually encounter the opposition's argument, and an analysis that ignored it then is less credible than one that engaged it. Acknowledge â†’ refute is stronger than ignore.
