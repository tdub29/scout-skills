## The framework

Twelve laws clustered around: **how to execute once a strategic intent is set.** Not strategy itself; tactics within strategy.

### The twelve laws

**Law 7 â€” Get others to do the work; take the credit.** Use their wisdom, knowledge, legwork. They get forgotten; you're remembered.

**Law 15 â€” Crush your enemy totally.** Half-measures invite revenge. Total annihilation closes the loop.

**Law 22 â€” Use surrender as a tactic.** When weaker, never fight for honor's sake. Surrender first; recover; outlast.

**Law 24 â€” Play the perfect courtier.** Master indirection, flattery, oblique assertion. Decorum is the medium.

**Law 28 â€” Enter action with boldness.** Hesitation infects execution. Mistakes via audacity are correctable; via timidity, not.

**Law 29 â€” Plan all the way to the end.** Consider all consequences, obstacles, possible reversals. Stop only after the ending.

**Law 35 â€” Master the art of timing.** Never seem in a hurry. Detect the right moment; strike fiercely when it arrives.

**Law 36 â€” Disdain things you cannot have.** Pursuing the unattainable elevates it. Show contempt; appear superior.

**Law 40 â€” Despise the free lunch.** What's free involves trick or obligation. Pay your way; remain free.

**Law 44 â€” Mirror effect.** Mirror opponents' tactics back; mock their behavior; teach a lesson; share their values illusively.

**Law 45 â€” Preach the need for change, but reform slowly.** Too much innovation triggers revolt. Frame as gentle improvement.

**Law 47 â€” Don't go past the mark; know when to stop.** Victory's heat tempts overreach. Set a goal; reach it; stop.

### The unifying logic

Strategic intent without tactical discipline fails. The twelve tactical laws answer execution questions:

- **Speed:** Bold (Law 28) but with timing (Law 35).
- **Force:** Totally (Law 15), or surrender (Law 22). Never half.
- **Visibility:** Take credit (Law 7); hide effort.
- **Manner:** Courtier (Law 24); indirect.
- **Limits:** Stop at the mark (Law 47); don't overreach.

### Three tactical patterns

**1. Total commitment vs. tactical retreat.**
> Law 15 + Law 22 + Law 28. Once you've committed, commit fully. If you're outmatched, retreat fully (don't half-fight).

The error: half-commitment. Engaging an enemy without crushing them, surrendering without genuine retreat. Both produce worse outcomes than the extremes.

**2. Plan-to-the-end then stop.**
> Law 29 + Law 47. Plan past the immediate goal to ENDING considerations. But when you reach the planned end, STOP. Victory's heat is the most dangerous moment.

**3. Indirect motion.**
> Law 9 (from earlier cluster) + Law 24 + Law 44. Power moves rarely through direct argument. Demonstrate; play the courtier; mirror back. Indirection is the medium.

### The mirror tactic (Law 44)

Greene's most-cited tactical law. Three versions:

- **Mirror their behavior:** mimic to disorient.
- **Mirror their values:** appear to share what they care about (illusive seduction).
- **Mirror their attacks:** return their tactics; they can't object without indicting themselves.

The defense against mirroring: notice the mimicry, name it privately, refuse to be drawn into the reflected reality.

## How to apply

For any tactical execution:

1. **Boldness check (Law 28).** Are you committed? Half-commitment is the failure mode.
2. **End-planning (Law 29).** Where does this stop? What's the moment to put down the tool?
3. **Timing (Law 35).** Is this the moment? Or are you striking too early?
4. **Indirection (Laws 9, 24).** Are you arguing where you could be demonstrating?
5. **Stop rule (Law 47).** What's the trigger for stopping? Set it BEFORE engagement; over-extension is the modal failure mode.

After execution:
- **Did you crush totally (Law 15) where total was needed?** Half-measures persist as future problems.
- **Did you take credit (Law 7) without obviously claiming?** Indirect credit-taking outlasts direct credit-taking.
- **Did you stop at the mark (Law 47)?** Overreach is the most common post-victory error.

For recognizing tactics on you:

- Someone mirroring your behavior to disarming effect â†’ Law 44.
- A reform proposed as "just a small improvement" â†’ Law 45 may be in play.
- A bold action that seems excessively confident â†’ Law 28; bold execution is itself a tactic.

## Common failure modes

- **Half-measures (violation of Law 15).** Pursuing an enemy enough to provoke, not enough to neutralize. Worst of both worlds.
- **Honor-driven combat (violation of Law 22).** Fighting at structural disadvantage because surrender feels weak. Often the disciplined surrender preserves the position.
- **Hesitation as caution (violation of Law 28).** What feels like care often shows as weakness. Boldness errors are recoverable; hesitation errors compound.
- **Reform too much, too fast (violation of Law 45).** New leaders changing too many things trigger immediate revolt. Greene's pace: gentle, framed as continuity.
- **Victory overreach (violation of Law 47).** The strongest moment IS the most dangerous. Set the stop-rule in advance.

## Ethical guardrail

This cluster contains some explicitly cynical operations:

- **Law 7** (take credit for others' work) is straightforwardly extractive. Doing it openly destroys teams; doing it covertly is theft.
- **Law 15** (crush enemy totally) is brutal counsel for adversarial contexts. Misapplied to ordinary disagreements, it's pathological.
- **Law 44** (mirror to deceive) is manipulation. Detection ends relationships.

The legitimate uses:
- Law 28 (boldness), Law 29 (plan to end), Law 35 (timing), Law 47 (stop at mark) are unambiguously useful.
- Law 22 (tactical surrender), Law 36 (disdain unattainable), Law 40 (pay your way) are wisdom in most contexts.

The questionable ones (7, 15, 44) require adversarial context to be defensible. Misapplied in cooperative settings, they destroy.
