## The framework

> Most situations â€¦ provide some clue for coordinating behavior, some focal point for each person's expectation of what the other expects him to expect to be expected to do. Finding the key â€” or rather finding a key â€” any key that is mutually recognized as the key becomes the key. (Chapter 3)

When two parties want the **same** outcome but **cannot communicate**, the problem is not conflicting interests; it is **coordinating expectations**. A man who loses his wife in a department store with no prior plan will often still find her, because each thinks of "some obvious place to meet, so obvious that each will be sure that the other is sure that it is 'obvious' to both of them." The reasoning is not "what would I do if I were she?" but the infinite regress "what would I do if I were she wondering what she would do if she were Iâ€¦" The way out is not deeper logic but a **mutually recognized signal** â€” a *focal point* (a "Schelling point") on which expectations converge.

### Tacit coordination experiments

Schelling posed problems to subjects who had to match a partner with no communication and a prize only for matching:
- "Heads or tails?" â€” most say **heads**.
- "Name a meeting place and time in New York City, no instructions" â€” large agreement on **Grand Central Station at noon**.
- "Name a positive number" â€” a plurality choose **1**.
- "Divide \$100 into two piles A and B; you both win only if your splits match" â€” overwhelmingly **\$50/\$50**.

People coordinate far more often than blind chance would predict. The "solution" is arbitrary in one sense â€” *any* answer is correct if enough people pick it â€” yet it is reliably findable.

### What makes a point focal

- **Prominence / conspicuousness** â€” it stands out (the lone landmark on the map, noon as the obvious hour, the round number). But prominence is *relative to time, place, and who the parties are* â€” only a mathematician "naturally" meets at the centroid of an irregular area.
- **Uniqueness** â€” there must be exactly one. A store with several "lost and found" desks loses the focal point. Schelling's map experiments: many houses + one crossroads sends people to the crossroads; many crossroads + one house sends them to the house. Uniqueness "avoids ambiguousness."
- **Salience drawn from imagination as much as logic** â€” analogy, precedent, symmetry, aesthetic or geometric configuration, casuistry, shared culture. "Poets may do better than logicians at this game."

### From coordination to tacit bargaining

The same mechanism solves problems where interests **partly conflict** (the parachutists who must meet but dislike walking). With no communication, the focal point becomes the only standing "offer" â€” there is no counter-proposal to make â€” so the conflict is reconciled, or simply ignored, "as a by-product of the dominant need for coordination." The party who happens to be closer to the focal point "wins" the bargain. This is why focal points govern not just lost-couple problems but tacit limits in war, unspoken price norms, and division rules ("split the difference," round numbers, the status quo line) in explicit bargaining too.

## How to apply

1. **Confirm it's a coordination problem.** Do the parties want to *match* (or land on a shared outcome), with communication absent, costly, or untrusted? If interests are purely opposed, this is the wrong lens.
2. **Look for the existing focal point.** What answer is *obvious* â€” prominent, unique, sanctioned by precedent, culture, symmetry, or a round number â€” such that each party expects the other to expect it? Reason about the regress only far enough to find a key, not to "solve" it.
3. **Engineer one if it's missing.** Make a default conspicuous and unique: a Schelling point ("we always meet at the main entrance," "round to the nearest \$5," "the existing boundary"). Ambiguity or ties destroy focality â€” collapse to *one* salient option.
4. **In tacit bargaining, recognize the focal point as the de facto offer.** Without communication, the salient division is the one that will hold; whoever is favored by it gets the better deal. To change the split, you must either communicate or shift the salience.
5. **Account for who the parties are.** Focality depends on shared frame of reference. A point obvious to one culture, profession, or era may not be focal to another; coordinate on a key they will recognize.

## Common failure modes

- **Solving for logic instead of salience.** Grinding the "I think that you thinkâ€¦" regress produces no answer; a *recognizable* key does. Don't out-think the focal point.
- **Destroying uniqueness.** Offering several equally-good options removes the focal point (three identical houses on the map; multiple lost-and-found desks). One salient default beats many fair ones.
- **Assuming a universal focal point.** Salience is local to time, place, and culture; "obvious to me" is not "obvious to them."
- **Ignoring the bargaining by-product.** In partly-conflicting coordination, the focal point silently *distributes* â€” celebrating that "we coordinated" can mask that the salient point handed one side the advantage.
- **Over-communicating it away.** Sometimes proposing alternatives breaks a perfectly good tacit convention; if a focal point already holds, naming competitors can muddy it.
