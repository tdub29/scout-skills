## The framework

> Commitments, threats, and promises will not improve your outcome in a game if they are not credible. (Chapter 7)

A **strategic move** is an action that *changes the game* â€” it alters the players, their available moves, or their payoffs â€” so that the new game yields a better outcome for the one who moves. New Year's resolutions are commitments to the future self; a dieter who hands embarrassing photos to a TV producer changes the future self's incentives. The three instruments are:

- **Commitment** â€” an unconditional move that binds you to a course of action regardless of what the other does.
- **Threat** â€” "if you do X, I will respond with something painful to you" (and usually costly to me).
- **Promise** â€” "if you do X, I will respond with something good for you."

Each only works on the *other* player's reasoning if it is **credible** â€” if the other believes you will actually carry it out. The hard part is that threats and promises are often *not in your interest to execute when the time comes*. God threatens death for eating the apple, then â€” facing the cost of destroying his own creation â€” settles for banishment; "the snake was right after all." Harry Potter promises Griphook the sword he intends to keep. Mere words are cheap: "A verbal contract isn't worth the paper it's written on."

### Why credibility is the whole problem

Run the threat through backward reasoning (see `references/dixit-backward-reasoning-game-trees.md`). The other player asks: *when my action has already happened, will it actually be in your interest to carry out the threat?* If executing the threat hurts you too, the answer is often no â€” so the threat is empty and the other player ignores it. Credibility means arranging the game so that following through is genuinely your best response at that future node â€” or removing your ability to *not* follow through.

### The Eightfold Path to credibility (three principles)

**A. Change your own payoffs** so following through becomes your interest (turn a threat into a warning, a promise into an assurance):
1. **Write contracts** that impose a penalty for not following through.
2. **Establish and use a reputation** â€” make the cost of being seen to bluff exceed the cost of executing.

**B. Limit your ability to back out** â€” restrict your own future freedom:
3. **Cut off communication** (you can't be talked out of it / can't receive a counter-offer).
4. **Burn bridges behind you** â€” eliminate your own retreat (the chicken player who unscrews the steering wheel and throws it out the window has *no* move but to go straight, so the rival must swerve).
5. **Leave the outcome beyond your control, or to chance** (the bridge to brinkmanship â€” see `references/schelling-commitment-brinkmanship.md`).

**C. Use others / structure:**
6. **Move in small steps** â€” break one large commitment into many small ones so the gain from breaking a little one is swamped by losing the rest.
7. **Develop credibility through teamwork** (a team enforces what an individual can't).
8. **Employ mandated agents** â€” hire someone whose pay or reputation depends on carrying out the move, who is relieved of the after-the-fact cost.

### The renegotiation flaw

A commitment device fails if it can be undone. Mr. Russo's \$25,000 "catch me eating" bounty is worthless because, facing dessert, he can simply renegotiate his way out (buy the enforcers a round of drinks) â€” the enforcers gain nothing by holding him to it. A commitment only bites when **the enforcing party has an independent incentive to enforce** (a supplier's penalty clause works because the buyer actually wants the goods, not the penalty) and when renegotiation is blocked.

## How to apply

1. **Identify which instrument you need** â€” commitment (bind yourself unconditionally), threat (deter the other's action), or promise (induce the other's action).
2. **Stress-test credibility by backward reasoning.** At the moment you'd have to execute, is doing so actually your best response? If not, the move is empty as stated.
3. **Pick a path to credibility** from the eight â€” change your payoffs (contract, reputation), remove your escape (cut communication, burn bridges, small steps), or delegate (teamwork, mandated agent).
4. **Make it visible and irreversible.** A commitment is "a needless surrender of flexibility unless it can be made fully evident and understandable to the other side." If they can't see it, it doesn't change their reasoning.
5. **Block renegotiation.** Confirm the enforcer has independent incentive and that you cannot quietly buy your way out.

## Common failure modes

- **Empty threats.** Threatening a response that would hurt you more than the offense; the other player back-inducts that you'll fold.
- **Invisible commitment.** Binding yourself in a way the other side never learns about â€” it changes nothing.
- **The renegotiation loophole.** A penalty no one has an incentive to collect (Mr. Russo) can be undone by mutual agreement.
- **Symmetric burning of bridges.** Both drivers throw out their steering wheels at once â€” now neither can swerve and they crash. Strategic moves "are not guaranteed, and sometimes they can be outright dangerous."
- **Over-large single commitment.** One big promise is easy to break at the right moment; many small ones are harder to abandon.

## Manipulation-literacy / ethical guardrail

Commitment and threats are *offensive* tactics â€” apply the discrimination note. **Name the move** (is this a commitment, a threat, or a promise?). **Model the payoffs** so the coercion is legible (who bears the cost if it executes?). **Flag coercion vs. legitimate commitment:** a legitimate commitment removes *your own* future temptation or credibly assures a partner and would be endorsed by both sides (a contractor's penalty-for-delay clause, a dieter's photo bond); a coercive move manufactures a threat whose only function is to extract value from someone who would refuse if free â€” and the more it relies on the other party being unable to talk, retreat, or verify, the more it shades into duress. **Offer the cooperative alternative:** can a *mutual* commitment (an enforceable contract both want, a reputation both rely on, a third-party escrow) achieve the same stability while creating value rather than only transferring it? Prefer commitments that are visible, reciprocal, and reversible-by-mutual-consent over unilateral threats that depend on the other side's helplessness.
